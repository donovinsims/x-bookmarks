# how to build a business with dots (FULL GUIDE)

what dots are
dots are openai's new always-on agents inside chatgpt. an agent is basically an ai that can go and do things for you, like reading your email, updating a sheet or replying to a customer, instead of just answering questions in a chat.
each dot is powered by gpt-6 astra, which is openai's newest model, and it has its own cloud computer. a cloud computer just means a computer running on openai's side, so the dot can keep working when yours is basically closed.
it learns from your feedback over time, and it can work towards your goals. it connects to over 4,000 apps through plugins, which are small add-ons that let the dot use a specific app, like gmail, google calendar, quickbooks or slack.
you can message your dot inside chatgpt, in slack or in teams, with texting coming soon.
a dot remembers how you like things done, and can act inside the apps you've connected, like drafting a reply in your inbox or updating a row in a sheet etc.,
two features matter the most for this guide.
the first is that when you're not working with it, the dot looks for ways to help in the background using the apps you've connected. while it's doing that on its own, it only has read-only access, which means it can look at things but not change or send anything.
for a business, that means the dot can notice things like an invoice that's gone overdue, an enquiry that's been sitting in the inbox, or like a quote that went out a while ago with no reply, and then bring them to the owner with a draft ready to go.
the second is custom rules. custom rules let you decide what the dot can do on its own, what it needs your approval for, and what it's never allowed to do. so you could let it draft every reply but require approval before anything gets sent.
those two features are what make this a business. a dot out of the box knows nothing about a business. it doesn't know the prices, the policies, what the business does and doesn't do, or what it's allowed to send without asking. someone has to connect the apps, load the prices and rules, and tell it what it can do alone, and most business owners will never do that themselves.
how to get access to dots
https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/

the cheapest plan that includes dots is pro, at $100 a month.
for the first month, using your dot doesn't count toward your plan's allowances, so it's a good time to test it heavily.
setting one up is quick. open the chatgpt desktop app, create your dot, give it a name, connect the apps you want it to use, and then set your custom rules.
for the rules, require approval for anything that sends a message, moves money or changes a record, and loosen it later once you trust what it's doing.
for a business, a sensible starting point is letting it read everything and draft anything, requiring approval for every email, text or invoice that goes out, and blocking anything to do with refunds, payments or deleting records. that way the owner sees every single thing before a customer does, and nothing can go badly wrong while they get used to it.
 
method one, setting up dots for businesses
this is the most direct way to get paid, and it's a service you sell to business owners.
the buyers are owner-led businesses that already use chatgpt and lose money in the same few places. invoices that never get chased, new leads that wait for a reply, and quotes that nobody follows up on. they've probably heard about dots, and they definitely don't have the time to set one up properly. and probably don't have the technical knowledge too.
the easiest ones to start with are trades that deal with a lot of small jobs, like plumbers, electricians, hvac companies, cleaners and landscapers. they send a lot of invoices, get a lot of enquiries, and the owner is usually out on a job when both need dealing with.
you can often spot a good fit before you talk to them. reviews that mention never hearing back, a website with a contact form and nothing else, stuff like this.
what you sell them is the setup. you connect their email, their calendar and their invoicing, load their prices and policies, write the approval rules, and give the dot standing jobs to do every day, like chasing invoices or answering new leads.
a standing job is just a task the dot does on a schedule without being asked, like checking for overdue invoices every morning or drafting a reply to every new enquiry as it comes in.
you charge a setup fee to do all of that, plus a monthly fee to keep it tuned as their prices, services and rules change.
the jobs worth setting up first are the ones that lose them money. like chasing overdue invoices, replying to new leads quickly, and following up on quotes etc.,
quote follow-ups work the same way as invoices. the dot watches for quotes that haven't had a reply, drafts a short follow-up after a set number of days, and stops the moment the customer answers, and you set it up with the same kind of rules as the invoices.
the monthly part is real work, too. prices change, the business starts or stops offering a service, a season changes what customers ask for, and the owner will want the tone adjusted once they've seen a few weeks of drafts. you go through what the dot drafted that month, fix anything that wasn't right, and update its rules and price sheet so it doesn't happen again.
starting with a mock business
before you touch a real client's accounts, set your dot up with a made-up business in the trade you want to sell to. you get to see exactly how it handles prices, invoices and customers, and you end up with something to show owners.
a dot works best when it splits a job like this across helper agents, one researching prices, one on invoices, one on replies, and has a separate reviewer check their work before anything comes back to you. ask for that directly.
 
 
the price sheet
the price sheet is the base for everything else, because every price the dot ever quotes to a customer comes from it. it should list the most common jobs in that trade, each with a real local price range and the source it came from.
the sources need checking. some sites that show up in price searches are referral sites, which collect leads and pass them on to businesses, rather than actual businesses in that trade, so they should be labelled as such. and when two sources give different ranges for the same job, the dot should flag it so you can decide, instead of it just picking one.
the invoices
chasing invoices is where a dot pays for itself fastest, as long as the rules are clear.
the rules work in stages. a friendly reminder first, then a firmer one if that doesn't work, then a flag for the owner to call them. and some invoices shouldn't be chased at all, like one the customer is disputing or one where they've already promised a date for example.
the tricky cases are the ones to get right. an invoice that isn't overdue yet gets left alone. one in dispute gets held instead of chased. and a customer who promised to pay by a date that's already passed goes back to being chased, with the reminder mentioning the missed promise.
every reminder should also include a line asking for the payment date if they've already paid, so a customer who's sent the money doesn't feel accused.
 
 
the lead replies
every enquiry falls into a handful of types, and the dot needs to know what to do with each one. a job that's on the price sheet gets a price range and a couple of times it could come out. 

a vague message like "need a plumber" gets one question back to find out what the problem is. something urgent, like a leak, gets safe first steps before anything else, so the customer can stop the damage while they wait. a job that isn't on the price sheet gets offered a visit instead of a guessed price.
why the setup is what they pay for
left to its defaults, a dot writes replies that are too long and full of disclaimers, the way ai replies often are. one tighter instruction on length and tone fixes that.
the bigger problem is what it doesn't know. if the dot hasn't been told whether the business handles emergencies, it might tell a customer with a burst pipe to call someone else. it isn't wrong, it just doesn't have that information, and an owner would never want that reply going out.
tuning the tone and telling the dot what the business actually does is the work the owner pays for.
 
 
selling it
you sell it by showing it working.
set up your own dot with a mock business in the trade you're pitching, run it through the invoices, the price sheet and the lead replies, and record the screen. the invoice decisions and the replies are the parts owners care about, so make sure those are clearly in the recording.
keep the recording short and in the order the owner would care about it. the invoices first, since that's money they're already owed, then the lead replies, then the price sheet. talk over it if you want, but the dot's decisions should be readable on their own.
send the recording by dm, or on a postcard with a qr code that goes to the video. a plumber watching a dot chase their invoices and answer their leads in their own trade will understand it right away.
when a business says yes, you connect their real accounts during the setup. that usually means getting on a call with the owner and having them log into each app from the dot's settings, so the access is theirs and they can remove it any time. 

then you load their real prices and policies in place of the mock ones, and run the same invoice and lead tests on their real data before turning anything on.

 custom rules keep every outgoing message behind their approval until they trust it, so nothing goes to a customer without the owner seeing it first. once they're comfortable, you loosen the rules for the simple stuff, like reminders at the first stage, and keep approval on the rest.
in the first few weeks, the owner approves every draft, and you watch what they change. if they keep shortening replies, you tighten the length. if they keep adding a line about warranties for ex, you add it to the instructions. every edit they make is telling you how the business actually talks, and the dot gets closer to sounding like them each time you update it.
automating the whole thing
you can automate almost all of method one. the only step you can't is the owner creating their dot and connecting their own accounts, because that happens inside their own chatgpt login. so the idea is to automate everything around that step and make it take them a few minutes.
your own dot can run the whole outreach itself. it has its own computer, so it can search google maps, open every website, build the pages and send the postcards through a connected print api.
first it finds the businesses, one trade in one city, pulled from google maps.
then it audits each one, the same test as method three. it tries to find a price and an open time on their website without filling in any forms, and logs where it got stuck. that becomes the hook, because most of them get stuck somewhere.
then it builds a demo landing page for each business. their name and logo at the top, then what an agent saw on their site, like "no price found, no open time found, stuck at the zip code form" something like that. 

under that is a prerecorded video of a dot running a business in their trade, chasing invoices and answering customers, and a few examples using their real services, like replies to the messages their customers typically send.
and last it mails a postcard. one side shows their site with the audit result, and the other side has a qr code that goes to their page. you can send it through scribeless or any print api.
 
 
the owner scans the card, watches the video, and pays straight on the page through whop, with no call needed.
once they've paid, they fill in one short form. their services, their price ranges, their hours, whether they take emergencies, their rules for chasing invoices, and what the dot should never do without asking.
from that form, your dot builds a setup pack in a google drive folder shared with them. a business profile doc with their services, prices, hours and area. the rulebooks for chasing invoices and replying to leads, written from their answers.
whop then sends them the guide, a short screen recording showing the only three things they do themselves. create their dot, connect their email, calendar and drive, and paste that first message.
 
if you'd rather keep it personal, you can do the same thing by hand. send the page yourself, do the setup on a short call with them, and use the same setup pack. either way, every month you update the folder when their prices or rules change, and their dot picks it up.
method two, building a plugin dots can call
this one is a product instead of a service.
a plugin is a small app that a dot can use. when someone asks their dot for something your plugin does, the dot calls your plugin, gets the answer back, and passes it on.
let's say you build a quote plugin for ac repair. someone tells their dot "get me a price for ac repair in phoenix". the dot calls your plugin, the plugin looks up a price range in its price table and finds an open booking slot, and sends both back. the dot shows the person the price and the time, and they book.
you get paid per lead or per booked job, either by the person using it or by the business on the other end that gets the job.
the dot decides whether to use your plugin by reading the plugin's description of what it does, so the description matters as much as the code. it needs to say clearly what the plugin is for, which trade and area it covers, and what it sends back, so a dot knows to call it when someone asks for exactly that.
the businesses on the other end are the easy part. the same price research that builds your table shows you which businesses do that job in that area, and those are the ones you'd send the booked jobs to.
here's how to build one.
first, pick one job that repeats in one trade, like ac repair, drain cleaning or garage door repair. something people ask for all the time and where the price falls in a clear range.
second, build the price table from public prices, using the same method as the price sheet in method one. real ranges, a source for every row, and anything that isn't a real business in that trade labelled as such.
the table should have the job, the size or type where it matters, a low and high price, and where each price came from. keep it to one city at first, because prices change a lot from one area to another.
third, have dots/codex build the app. it only needs two actions, one that returns a quote from the price table and one that books a slot.
for the booking side, the slots have to come from somewhere real. the simplest way is to connect it to the calendar or booking tool of a business that's agreed to take the jobs, so any time the plugin offers is a time that business can actually do.
fourth, connect it to your own chatgpt in developer mode. developer mode is a setting that lets you add your own plugin to your account before anyone else can use it.
and last, test it from your dot. ask for a price the way a normal person would, try a job that isn't in the table, and try booking a slot, and check the dot handles each one properly.
try it the messy way too, because that's how people will actually ask. "my ac is blowing warm air", "how much to fix an ac in phoenix" and "can someone come out tomorrow" should all end up at the same quote and the same booking. if any of them don't, the description of the plugin needs to be clearer about what it's for.
once it works for one job in one city, you add the next job to the table, or the same job in the next city. each one is just a new set of rows.
 
method three, making businesses findable by dots
this one is a service again, but for a different reason.
as more people get a dot, some of them will stop searching for a plumber or an ac company themselves and just ask their dot to find one, get a price and book it. the businesses the dot can actually read and book will get those jobs, and the ones it can't will lose them without ever knowing.
and right now, most businesses are very hard for a dot to use, even the big ones with thousands of reviews. send a dot to a typical service business's website to find a repair price and the next open appointment, the way a customer would, and it usually comes back with neither.
the reasons are almost always the same. prices aren't on the page at all. the booking tool asks for a zip code and the service before it shows any times, so the dot can't see a single one without filling in a form. an advertised page goes to a 404, which is the error you get when a page doesn't exist. or the site blocks the dot completely with a bot check, the kind of test that asks you to prove you're a person.
 
what you sell is the fix for exactly what the dot got stuck on. plain prices or ranges on the page, so a dot can quote them. the next open times visible without a form. a booking process that works in a few simple steps. and a google listing an agent can actually read, with the hours, services and prices filled in.
none of this is complicated work. it's mostly putting information that's already in the owner's head, or already inside their booking tool, somewhere a dot can see it without having to fill in a form. a lot of it overlaps with what makes a site easier for a person to use too, which makes it an easy yes.
agent-readable just means the information is written on the page as normal text, so the dot can read it without clicking through anything.
you sell it by running the same audit on the business's own site first. send them the dot's log of where it got stuck, and show them a fixed version of the page next to theirs.
the fixed version doesn't need to be a whole new website. it can be one page with their real prices or ranges written out, their next open times pulled from their booking tool, and a booking button that goes straight to a time. run the audit again on that page and show them the dot finding both.
