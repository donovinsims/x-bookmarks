# The 5 Best Cold Emails We've Ever Sent

## Post

Our team has sent 17M+ cold emails.

Out of everything we've written, these are 5 of the best performing emails we've ever sent for clients.

1 of them got 1,100 positive replies in 21 days.

1 of them came out of a pivot, after emailing the buyers directly barely worked, and now books 10 to 20 meetings a week.

1 of them is a single sentence.

A good cold email does 1 job: it gets the right person to say "yes, send it over" in under 5 seconds.

That's about how long you get.

Ecom brands get somewhere between 20 and 100 cold emails a day.

Your email has to be understood at a glance, or it's gone.

By the end of this article, you'll have:

The 5 best cold emails we've ever sent, as templates you can copy

The real campaign and the real numbers behind each one

The rule that makes each one work

A weak version and a strong version of each, so you can see the difference

What all 5 have in common, from our analysis of 4,055 scripts

The testing habit we use to find emails like these

Email 1: Show them the finished result before they buy:

 

(We write and run campaigns like these for B2B companies. DM me "SCRIPTS" if you want us to run yours.)

The campaign:

Our client Eden Studio makes landscape design software.

It turns a landscaping job into a full design the client can see before any work starts.

We went after landscaping companies with 11 to 50 employees.

Big enough to have budget, small enough to have no designer in house.

The list behind it:

We scraped 69,204 landscaping and hardscaping businesses off Google Maps and enriched every account across 19 data points.

Then we scored each one for intent:

12 points: they outsource design (designed projects shown, no designer on the team)

10 points: reviews complaining about quote speed, or clients who "couldn't visualize" the job

10 points: hiring a designer, estimator or project manager

10 points: a high ticket portfolio with a small team

10 points: a spike in new reviews in the last 90 days

6 points: running paid ads

5 points: a manufacturer certified hardscape installer

5 points: entry level or no design software

The email works because the list is full of accounts that already have the problem it describes.

The email:

"Hey {FIRST_NAME}, most {PAIN} start the same way - the client pictured {X} thing, then {X}.

We use {SYSTEM} to turn a job into a full design with {MECHANISM}, clients sign off on before you break ground.

Happy to build one from a recent {COMPANY} project.

Worth a look?"

How it's built:

Open on how the pain starts, in their words

Say what the system does and what it produces

Offer to build 1 from a recent project of theirs

End on "worth a look?"

There's no personalization anywhere in it.

The specificity comes from the list and the offer.

The results:

41,150 emails sent

47 interested leads

20 meetings from email alone

In week 1, emails pulled from their websites ran a 5.6% reply rate and a 6.12% interested rate

9 calls booked in the first 6 days, 2 of them on day 1

Add the cold calling we ran on the same market, and the campaign booked 53 meetings in 50 days.

The rule:

Offer to build them a piece of the result using their own work.

A landscaper can picture "a full design of a project you already did" instantly.

Weak vs strong:

Weak: "We help landscaping companies win more jobs with better design tools. Open to a quick call?"

Strong: "Happy to build one from a recent {COMPANY} project. Worth a look?"

The strong version hands them something with their own company's name on it.

How to use it for your offer:

Write down the moment your buyer's problem starts, in the words they'd use

Describe what your product produces, as a finished thing they can look at

Offer to make 1 for them from their own work (a past project, their website, their data)

End on "worth a look?"

If you can't make a free piece of the result, offer a short demo built on their company instead.

Email 2: Sell to the person the buyer already listens to:

The campaign:

Our client Glidepath runs an exchange fund.

It helps people holding a lot of 1 company's stock diversify, with no management fee.

Their buyers usually hold $2M to $10M in a single stock (think Nvidia, SpaceX, Google).

The version that didn't work:

The first campaign emailed those stockholders directly: senior engineers and directors at Google and Nvidia.

Cold calls kind of worked.

The email barely worked at all.

Senior people at big companies get buried in email, and enterprise inboxes are hard to land in.

The pivot:

We stopped emailing the stockholders and started emailing their financial advisors.

An advisor's job already includes bringing clients solutions like this.

And 1 advisor can manage $10M to $20M in client assets that fit, sometimes a few hundred million.

So 1 yes from an advisor can be worth several stockholders.

The email:

"Do you have a client on a concentrated stock?

We help them diversify with no extra fees and no big tax bill.

Will this be worth the conversation?"

An alternative ask: "What's a ticker your client might be overleveraged on?"

How it's built:

Line 1 is a question every advisor can answer in their head

Line 2 is the outcome for their client, in plain words

Line 3 is a soft ask

No personalization.

3 lines.

The results (when I filmed the breakdown):

466,000 emails sent

About 251,000 people contacted

Around 5,000 replies

364 interested leads

About 690 contacts per interested lead

10 to 20 meetings a week at 200,000 emails a month

Around 40% of interested replies asked for the meeting themselves

Interested to booked ran at roughly 30 to 40%, the highest of all our clients

The client just closed its first deal from it: a $2M investment

The rule:

Find the champion, the person who brings solutions to your buyer, and sell to them.

Weak vs strong:

Weak: "Hi {first name}, we've built an innovative exchange fund for high net worth individuals. Would you like to learn more?"

Strong: "Do you have a client on a concentrated stock?"

The strong version asks about the advisor's clients, which is what they think about all day.

How to use it for your offer:

List everyone your buyer trusts for advice on this problem (advisors, consultants, accountants, agencies)

Pick the one whose job already includes finding solutions like yours

Open with a question about their clients, never about them

Keep it to 3 lines

This works best when the deal size is big and the end buyer is hard to reach.

1 champion can open the door to dozens of buyers.

What ran alongside the email:

Across our Glidepath work, the phone ran as a primary channel from day 1.

We called 3 to 5 contacts per account, never just 1.

And we checked every contact's email provider before sending, so accounts behind security gateways like Barracuda and Proofpoint went to the phone instead.

If you sell into companies with more than 1,000 employees, cold email on its own will never reach the person who signs.

Email 3: The free mockup with no first name:

 

The campaign:

A custom foodservice disposables manufacturer.

They make branded cups, bowls, plates and other disposables for restaurants and local shops.

Their minimum order is $5K.

The normal minimum in their industry is 20,000 units, or $10K to $20K.

The data behind it:

We pulled restaurants and local businesses straight from Google Maps.

Local owners are on Google Maps, and the usual databases pull from LinkedIn.

We scraped their websites for info@ and personal emails, because at a local business that inbox is the owner's lifeline.

The email, line by line:

Subject: "branded cups"

No {FIRST_NAME} at all, because we can't reliably know who reads an info@ inbox

What they make, and that it's for businesses like theirs

The 2 reasons to switch: smaller minimum orders and faster delivery

The free offer: an AI generated mockup of a cup with their logo on it

Here's the structure as a template:

"{What you make} for {type of business}.

Smaller minimums than most manufacturers, and faster delivery.

Want me to send over a free mockup with your logo on it?"

As I put it in the breakdown: "The email stays short, the mockup does the selling."

On the missing first names: "We just get straight into the email and no one cares."

The results:

Phase 1:

1,061 interested leads in 70 days, at 100K+ emails a month

86% of leads qualified in HubSpot

8 deals closed inside the window, with more in pipeline

Phase 2:

1,100 positive replies in 21 days

84,000 emails to 66,000 people

1 positive reply for every 60 people contacted

4,000 emails a day

137 positive replies on the best day

The industry average sits around 500 to 1,000 contacts per lead.

This ran close to 10X better than that.

It's probably the best campaign I've ever seen in terms of efficiency at scale.

The rule:

Give them a free sample of the finished product, made for them, before they've spent a dollar.

Weak vs strong:

Weak: "Hi {first name}, we're a leading manufacturer of custom disposables. Can we set up a call to discuss your packaging needs?"

Strong: "Want me to send over a free mockup with your logo on it?"

Saying yes to the strong version costs them nothing and takes 2 seconds.

Why the market made it work:

Restaurants and local businesses get far fewer cold emails than SaaS and ecom companies do.

The info@ inboxes we reached are ones other senders don't know how to scrape at scale.

And the mapping turned up segments nobody had on their list, like megachurches ordering 5,000 branded cups a month.

How the replies were handled:

Positive replies were scored automatically, pushed to Slack, synced into HubSpot, and routed to a 5 person sales team the minute they landed.

Later, an AI reply agent took over, answering inside 30 seconds at around 95% accuracy.

The whole sales process runs over email, because local owners would rather not do Zoom calls.

How to use it for your offer:

Find a market that's easy to reach and rarely cold emailed (local businesses, restaurants, trades)

Pull the data from Google Maps and their websites, info@ included

Pick the 2 reasons someone would switch to you, and say them in 1 line

Use AI to make a free visual sample with their logo or their details on it

Drop the first name when you don't have one

Send those emails from a separate EmailBison account to protect your main sending reputation.

Email 4: The 1 sentence email:

The campaign:

An ecom agency selling to brands doing $100K+ a month.

Ecom decision makers see hundreds of emails a day, and they've heard every generic ROAS promise.

The email:

"{first name}, interested in {XYZ thing} I created for your brand?"

That's the full email.

Under 20 words.

The longer version of the same idea adds 1 line of proof underneath:

"{first name} - interested in {free work} for {company_name}?

P.S. {social proof}"

What "XYZ thing" means:

A free welcome flow build

A free ad creative

A free checkout mockup

A free case study, written as a playbook for their brand

From our course: for ecom agencies, a 1 sentence free offer email was "the only thing that was really getting consistent meetings on the calendar."

The results:

45 leads in 20 days for an ecom agency selling to $100K+ a month brands

On a separate, longer engagement for an agency targeting 7 figure ecom brands: 176 qualified leads in 90 days from 46,790 contacts, about 266 contacts per lead, using 15 to 30 word emails that led with the offer

It works outside ecom too:

For a health tech company selling body composition scales, we went after golf clubs, country clubs and wellness resorts.

The follow up that offered to ship them a scale generated the most positive responses of the whole campaign.

That campaign got 35 interested leads in 30 days, and a meeting with Hyatt about adding the scale to its wellness package.

Private golf and country clubs alone produced 28 of the 35, at about 217 contacts per lead.

The rule:

The faster a prospect can read and answer your email, the more of them do.

Make the offer free, specific and made for them.

The list behind it:

For the 45 leads in 20 days campaign, the list was hyper qualified:

Claygent confirmed every company was an ecom brand

We scored their online presence as a budget signal

We filtered for Shopify and WooCommerce stores

We used Apify to verify they were actively running ads

For the 176 leads campaign, we built the list with Apollo keyword and tech filters.

We qualified every brand in Clay as ecom and running Meta ads, then ran a waterfall to find emails.

Filtering for brands running ads matters.

Even at 11+ employees, you'll find brands spending under $1K a month on ads, and this filter keeps them out.

Weak vs strong:

Weak: "Hi {first name}, I hope this finds you well. I run an agency that helps 7 figure brands scale their revenue through email marketing, paid ads and creative strategy..."

Strong: "{first name}, interested in a free welcome flow I built for {brand}?"

By the time the weak version gets to the point, they've already deleted it.

How to use it for your offer:

Pick 1 small piece of your service you can do for free

Make it specific to their brand (their welcome flow, their ad, their checkout)

Write 1 sentence that offers it

Put your proof in a P.S. if you have strong proof from their industry

If you're worried about free work bringing in bad leads, decide who gets the free work after they reply.

Email 5: Point at a gap they can check themselves:

 

The campaign:

This one ran on a live Pipeline.tech account, so I'm keeping the vertical private.

Same account, same 3 weeks, same inboxes.

The email:

"You don't have a {role}, so who is handling {that role's work}?"

The results:

The missing hire angle: 464 prospects per positive reply

Everything else on that account: 2,587 prospects per positive reply

Same sender, same timing, over 5X better.

Why it works:

The signal is something they can verify from the outside.

A missing role on an org chart is a fact.

When your email names a gap they know is real, it reads like you did your homework.

Same idea, different gap:

For a web and app agency that builds for churches, we used a Clay workflow to audit each church's website.

It checked for 4 features: an event calendar, a mobile app, live streaming, and digital donations or recurring tithing.

Each site got a score from 0 to 100.

The offer was a custom website app built around the exact feature that church was missing, paired with social proof about what that feature does.

Result: 72 interested leads from 14,110 people contacted, about 196 contacts per lead.

Another version I've shared before:

"{first name}, signed up to your email list last week and noticed you don't have a welcome flow set up.

I never got a single email after subscribing.

Interested in a free welcome flow build out?"

The rule:

Scrape a gap you can prove, name it in 1 line, and offer the fix.

Weak vs strong:

Weak: "Hi {first name}, we help churches improve their online presence and engagement."

Strong: "Noticed {church} doesn't have online giving set up yet. Want to see what it would look like on your site?"

The strong version names something specific to that church's own website.

How to use it for your offer:

List the signals that mean someone needs you (a missing hire, a missing feature, a slow site, no welcome flow)

Keep only the ones you can scrape and prove from the outside

Build a Clay workflow or an agent that checks every account for that gap

Write 1 line naming the gap, 1 line on the fix, and a soft CTA

The more checkable the gap, the better the email reads.

What all 5 have in common:

We once fed 4,055 scripts from 1,160 campaigns into Claude Code to see what the top performers had in common.

The top performers:

Opened with a question about the prospect's world

Put a tangible asset at the center (a design, a mockup, a playbook, an audit)

Used soft, permission based CTAs like "Mind if I share it?" or "Want me to send it over?"

Used specific numbers ("$4/sq ft savings") instead of vague claims

Used variables beyond first name, like {PROJECT} and {LOCATION}

Name dropped recognizable brands in the proof

Sounded casual, like a message to a coworker

Length made no difference:

Top performers averaged about 50 words.

Bottom performers also averaged about 50 words.

What separated them was the CTA type, whether they led with value or a pitch, how specific the numbers were, and how relevant the email was to the person's actual situation.

The rules we run every campaign on:

Under 65 words

A 1 to 4 word lowercase subject line

Plain text, no images, no links in the first email

A soft CTA like "worth a look?"

1 follow up, then new contacts

2 more we'd add to the list:

A public event creates a deadline the prospect already feels.

For a New York financial services firm, an acquisition left a group of companies needing a new provider.

The email referenced a recent article about the acquisition, explained why they needed to act, and offered 2 years of free service.

Result: 106 interested leads from 23,418 people contacted, about 221 per lead.

And for a construction event client, leading with the conference itself beat leading with the continuing education credits, on buyer quality.

That campaign got 197 interested leads from 19,501 people contacted.

The habit that found all 5:

 

Every one of these emails came out of testing.

How we test:

Test the offer first

Keep the copy identical and change only the offer line

Test 3 to 5 core offers at once

Give every variant 1,000+ sends before deciding anything

Kill the bottom 60 to 70% after 2 weeks

Take the winner and test the wording, the CTA and the P.S.

What to watch:

Contacts per lead.

Under 500 is a good range.

Around 1,000 is where demand capture offers tend to sit.

At 2,000 to 3,000, keep tweaking the offer or the segment.

Reply rate can go up while contacts per lead stays flat, so contacts per lead is the number we judge every email on.
