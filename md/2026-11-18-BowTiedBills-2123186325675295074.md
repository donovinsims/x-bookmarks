Part 1: The pages that catch the search:

These 3 formats are the foundation.

Format 1: The service page:

1 page for every service you want clients for.

A family lawyer gets a divorce page, a child custody page, a child support page and an alimony page.

A dentist gets an implants page, an Invisalign page, an emergency dentist page and so on.

Why it prints:

Google can only rank a page for a search if the page is clearly about that search.

When 6 services share 1 page, none of them is the main topic.

When each one has its own page, each one gets its own shot at page 1.

Whitespark's experts ranked "dedicated page for each service" as the #2 factor for showing up in AI search, out of 187.

What goes on it:

The service and your town in the meta title and the H1

Who it's for, in the words your clients use

What happens step by step when they hire you

What it costs, or what affects the cost

Your reviews that mention that service

The questions people ask about it, answered plainly

A call button and a booking option near the top

The order I lay it out in:

The H1, with the service and the town

2 or 3 lines on who it's for and what you do about it

A call button and a booking option

Your rating and 2 or 3 reviews about this exact service

How the process works, step by step

Cost, or what affects the cost

The questions people ask, each with a short answer

Links to the related service pages and town pages

The call button again at the bottom

Someone who lands on the page should know within 5 seconds that they're in the right place and how to call you.

Weak vs strong:

Weak H1: "Our Services"

Strong H1: "Child Custody Lawyer In [Town]"

The weak one could be on any website.

The strong one tells Google and the visitor exactly what the page is about.

Add schema:

Schema is code that tells Google what the page is about in its own format.

For a local business, that's LocalBusiness schema, or the most specific type that fits (like LegalService or Dentist).

The details in it (name, address, phone, hours) should match your Google Business Profile word for word.

You can test it with Google's Rich Results Test.

What it looks like with a result:

A divorce and family lawyer in a mid size Midwest city came to me sitting on page 3, with ~4 calls a month from Google.

We worked on his website and his Google profile together.

In 90 days, he was #1, website clicks went from ~40 to 156 a month, and calls went to 50+ a month.

His words: "I have so many leads I can barely service the business."

How to use this:

List every service you'd be happy to get 10 more clients for

Check which ones have their own page today

Build the missing ones, starting with the most profitable

Format 2: The service + town page:

Same service, but built for 1 town at a time.

"Car accident lawyer Marietta."

"Emergency dentist Roswell."

Why it prints:

Rankings drop the further a searcher is from your office.

A service + town page gives Google a reason to show you to people searching from the next town over.

Whitespark ranked city and neighborhood keyword relevance of your content in the top 10 factors for AI visibility too.

A real example to study:

Gary Martin Hays, a personal injury firm in Georgia, has a page built exactly for "car accident attorney Marietta."

The H1 reads: "A Marietta, GA Car Accident Attorney Focused On Results."

The page mentions local roads like I-75 and Roswell Street.

The phone number stays on screen as you scroll.

And it links to the firm's other Marietta pages (truck, motorcycle and pedestrian accidents) and to its pages for other towns like Duluth, Atlanta and Conyers.

That's a service + town page done properly.

(They're not a client of mine, so go look at it.)

What makes it local:

The town in the title, the H1 and the first paragraph

Roads, landmarks and courthouses or hospitals people in that town know

Directions from that town to your office

Reviews from clients in or near that town, if you have them

An embedded map

The mistake:

Copying 1 page 20 times and swapping the town name.

Google sees through that, and it reads badly to a real person too.

Each page needs details only someone who works in that town would know.

How far to go:

Start with the towns closest to your office, then work outward.

The further a town is from your office, the harder it is to rank there on the map, so the website page carries more of the weight.

That's exactly where these pages earn their keep.

A simple template:

H1: "[Service] Lawyer In [Town]"

2 lines on the service, for people in that town

How you help people in that town specifically (the local court, the local hospital, the roads where accidents happen)

Reviews from clients nearby

Directions from the town to your office, with a map

Links to the same service in nearby towns, and to your other services in this town

How to use this:

List the 5 to 10 towns you want clients from

Start with your best service, and build a page for it in each town

Write each one fresh, with real local details

Format 3: The office page (for more than 1 location):

If you have more than 1 office, every office gets its own page.

A real example to study:

Aspen Dental has nearly 1,100 offices, and Semrush estimates its site pulls around 1.57M organic visits a month in the US.

Every office has its own page, sitting inside a clean tree: state, then city, then office.

Each office page has the town in the title and the H1, the address and hours matching its Google profile, reviews, a booking path, the dentists who work there, local FAQs and directions.

Each office also links to around 15 service pages built for that office.

Why it prints:

Every office has its own Google Business Profile.

The website link on that profile should point to that office's page, never the homepage.

When the page and the profile say the same thing, Google trusts both more.

What it needs:

The office address, phone and hours, matching the Google profile exactly

The people who work there, with photos

The services offered at that office

Reviews for that office

Parking, directions and what's nearby

How a multi office client did it:

A traffic ticket firm with offices in 3 California markets had every market built out with its own pages and profile.

The firm went #1 in 3 of 3 markets.

Organic clicks went from 1,200 to 4,800 a month (4x), and ad spend dropped from $50K to $5K a month.

How to use this:

Give every office its own page, and link each Google profile to it

Check the address, phone and hours match on both, character for character

Add the services offered at each office as links from that page

Format 4: The cost page:

A page that answers "how much does [service] cost?" honestly.

A real example to study:

River Pools, a pool installer in Virginia, is the most famous case of this.

Back in 2009, during the crash, Marcus Sheridan (then a co owner) started answering every question buyers asked on the company blog.

The first big one was the cost of a fiberglass pool.

Sheridan has said that 1 article has been tied to more than $2M in sales.

River Pools still updates its cost content for the current year.

Why it prints:

Price is one of the first things people search, and most businesses refuse to talk about it.

So whoever answers it gets the traffic, and earns the trust.

A cost page doesn't need an exact quote.

It needs ranges, what pushes the price up or down, and an example or 2.

What goes on it:

A price range, or how you charge (flat fee, hourly, contingency, per tooth, per square foot)

The 3 to 5 things that change the price

A typical example, like "most uncontested divorces we handle land between X and Y"

What's included, and what costs extra

Payment plans or financing, if you offer them

A call button for a real quote

For lawyers:

Contingency firms have it easy: explain the percentage, when it's paid, and what happens if you lose.

Hourly firms can explain retainers and the factors that move the total.

What a cost page outline looks like (for an uncontested divorce):

H1: "How Much Does An Uncontested Divorce Cost In [Town]?"

The short answer, as a range

How you charge (flat fee or hourly)

What's included: filing, drafting, court appearances

What pushes the cost up: kids, property, a spouse who stops cooperating

Court filing fees, which are separate from your fee

2 or 3 example situations and what they usually cost

How to get an exact quote

Every one of those headings is a question someone types into Google.

How to use this:

Write down the price question you get on every single call

Answer it on a page, with ranges and examples

Link to it from every related service page

Format 5: The "best of" page:

A page that answers "who's the best [service] in [town]?"

Yes, including your competitors.

A real example to study:

River Pools wrote posts like "Who Are The Best Pool Builders in Richmond, Virginia?"

That page lists 4 local competitors by name, and River Pools doesn't put itself on the list.

According to PRNEWS, a similar post on the best pool builders in Virginia was tied to about $150K in business.

Why it prints:

People search "best [service] near me" and "who's the best [service] in [town]" constantly.

And this is the format AI tools love most.

Whitespark's 2026 report put "presence on expert curated best of and similar lists" at #1 out of 187 factors for AI search visibility.

So there are 2 ways to win this:

Write your own honest "best of" page for your area and your industry

Get onto other people's "best of" lists (local news sites, industry associations, local magazines and directories)

How to write it without looking shady:

Explain how you judged them (years in business, reviews, specialties)

Be fair to the competitors you list

Explain who each one is the right fit for

Explain who you're the right fit for, on your own pages

How to use this:

Search "best [your service] in [your town]" and see who's ranking

Make a list of local "best of" articles and directories, and get onto them

Write 1 honest guide for your area

Where your business can get listed:

Whitespark's 2026 report also ranked these in the top 10 for AI visibility:

#3: being prominent on key industry sites

#4: quality mentions on sites like newspapers, blogs, government sites and industry associations

#7: the number of those mentions

So the "best of" format works both ways.

Your own page catches the search, and mentions on other people's lists are what AI tools trust when they decide who to recommend.

Places to start:

Your local newspaper's "best of" or readers' choice awards

Your city magazine's top lists

Your state or local bar association, dental association or trade association

Your chamber of commerce

Local sponsorships and events that list their sponsors

Format 6: The comparison page:

A page that compares 2 or 3 options the buyer is choosing between.

"Implants vs dentures."

"Mediation vs going to court."

"Fiberglass vs vinyl liner pools."

A real example to study:

River Pools has "Vinyl Liner Pools vs. Fiberglass Pools: An Honest Comparison," last updated in August 2025.

River Pools makes fiberglass pools, and the page still admits fiberglass costs more upfront and offers fewer shape options.

It goes through installation time, cost, maintenance and durability, declares a winner on each point, gives price ranges, and ends with a comparison table and who each type of pool suits best.

Why it prints:

Someone searching "X vs Y" is close to buying.

They've decided they need the thing, and now they're choosing which version.

If your page helps them decide, you're the business they call.

What goes on it:

The options, side by side, in plain words

Cost for each

Time for each

The downsides of each, including the one you sell

Who each option is right for

A simple table at the end

How to use this:

Write down the "should I do X or Y?" questions clients ask you

Build 1 page per question

Link each one to the service pages it's about

Traffic only matters if it turns into calls, bookings and cases.

Format 7: The answers page:

A page (or a set of pages) that answers the questions clients ask you every week, 1 short answer at a time.

Why it prints right now:

Google retired the Q&A section on business profiles.

The Q&A API shut down on November 3 2025, and the public section was phased out after that.

Now, Ask Maps (Gemini inside Google Maps, launched March 2026) and Google's other AI answers pull from your profile, your website and other sources around the web.

So the answers people used to find on your profile need to live on your website, where AI can read them.

What River Pools did:

Sheridan's rule was to answer every question a customer had ever asked.

According to PRNEWS, buyers who read around 30 pages of the site bought about 80% of the time.

People who read your answers come in already sold.

How to write it:

1 question per heading

A direct answer in the first 1 or 2 sentences

Then the detail

Written at a level a 12 year old could follow

Linked to the service it's about

About FAQ schema:

In 2023, Google limited FAQ rich results to well known government and health sites.

So for most local businesses, FAQ schema won't get you extra space in Google anymore.

Write the answers for the person reading, and for the AI tools that pull from your site.

Questions worth answering (for a law firm):

How long does this usually take?

Do I need a lawyer for this?

What should I bring to the first meeting?

Will I have to go to court?

How do you get paid?

Swap them for the questions your own clients ask.

How to use this:

Ask your front desk for the 10 questions they hear most

Answer each one in 100 to 300 words

Put them on the related service pages, or on their own pages for the big ones

Format 8: The calculator page:

A page with a tool that gives the visitor a number.

Why it prints:

People love a number for their own situation.

A calculator can rank for its own searches, and it keeps the visitor on your site long enough to call you.

After a car accident, people call several firms.

A calculator that gives them a rough case value makes you the firm they remember.

Which ones work:

Personal injury: a case value calculator

Family law: alimony, child support and custody calculators

Dentists: a cost or financing calculator

Home services: a project cost estimator

Where it doesn't work:

Immigration and criminal defense, where every case depends on facts a calculator can't weigh.

How to build it:

Ask 3 to 6 simple questions

Give a range, never a promise

Show the range on screen, then offer a free consultation for a real number

Ask for contact details after showing the result

Why the result comes first:

If you ask for an email before showing the number, a lot of people leave.

If you show the number first, then offer a free consultation to get the exact figure, the people who stay are the ones ready to talk.

Where to put it:

On its own page, so it can rank for "[calculator] [state]"

Linked from the matching service page

Mentioned in your Google Business Profile posts now and then

How to use this:

Pick the calculator that matches the first number your clients ask about

Put it on its own page, and link to it from the matching service page

Track how many calls come from it

Format 9: The second language page:

Your service pages, written properly in the language your clients speak at home.

Why it prints:

Spanish speaking clients are underserved in a lot of markets.

The competition usually stops at English.

So second language pages go after the same clients with far less competition.

What it did for a client:

An immigration firm on the East Coast, 30+ years in business, came to me with ~32 calls a month.

We built every service page in English and Spanish, and set up the profile in both.

In the first 4 months, calls tripled to 104 a month, while the firm cut its ad spend.

On the map, the firm went from outside the top 3 at all 225 points we track across the state to #1 at all 225.

His words: "He feels like an in-house teammate."

How to do it right:

Translate with a native speaker, not just a tool

Give each language its own pages and URLs

Make sure someone who speaks that language answers the phone

Mention the language in your Google profile and attributes

How to use this:

Check your call logs for how many callers prefer another language

Start with your top 3 service pages

Add the language to your hours and contact details, so people know they'll be understood

The mistakes that kill these pages:

 

I see the same mistakes on almost every site that comes to me.

These are the ones worth checking first.

Copy and paste town pages:

20 town pages with the same text and a different town name.

Google treats them as near duplicates, and they rarely rank.

Write each one fresh.

Pages nobody can reach:

A page that isn't linked from anywhere on your site is hard for Google to find, and nobody visits it.

Every page should be linked from at least 1 other page: services from the homepage and menu, towns from the service pages, answers from the services they're about.

Pages that don't match the Google profile:

If your page says you close at 5 and your profile says 6, Google trusts both a little less.

Name, address, phone and hours should match everywhere, character for character.

Slow pages on a phone:

A lot of local searches happen on a phone, and Google indexes the mobile version of your site.

Google's Core Web Vitals thresholds for a "good" page:

Largest Contentful Paint (how fast the main content shows): 2.5 seconds or less

Interaction to Next Paint (how fast it reacts when someone taps): 200 milliseconds or less

Cumulative Layout Shift (how much things jump around while loading): 0.1 or less

You can check yours for free with PageSpeed Insights.

No way to call:

A visitor who's ready to call shouldn't have to hunt for the number.

Put a call button at the top of every page, keep it visible on mobile as they scroll, and add a booking option.

Writing for people who'll never pay you:

"How to file your own divorce" brings traffic from people trying to avoid a lawyer.

"What a divorce lawyer does in your first 30 days" brings traffic from people about to hire one.

Write for the second group.

How to use this:

Open your site on your phone and try to call from 3 different pages

Run your top 3 pages through PageSpeed Insights

Check your hours and phone number match your Google profile

How to put this into practice:

You don't need all 9 at once.

Here's the order I'd build them in.

This week:

List your services, your towns and your offices

Check which have their own pages

Fix the meta title and H1 on the pages you already have, so each one names the service and the town

This month:

Build the missing service pages (Format 1)

Build service + town pages for your top service in your top 5 towns (Format 2)

Give every office its own page, linked from its Google profile (Format 3)

Next month:

Write your cost page (Format 4)

Write 1 comparison page for the most common "X or Y" question (Format 6)

Write answers to your 10 most common questions (Format 7)

After that:

Build a calculator if your industry has a number people want (Format 8)

Add second language pages if your market needs them (Format 9)

Write your honest "best of" guide and get onto other people's lists (Format 5)

How the 9 link together:

The homepage links to every service and every office

Every service page links to its town pages, its cost page, its comparison pages and its answers

Every town page links back to the service, and to the same service in nearby towns

Every office page links to the services offered there

The calculator links to the service it's about, and the service page links back

When the pages link to each other like this, Google can understand how your site fits together, and visitors can move from "what does this cost?" to "book a call" in 1 or 2 clicks.

What to expect:

In my experience, the first 30 days bring an uptick in clicks, calls and visits.

Around 3 months in, my clients are usually #1 on the map in a 20 to 25 square mile radius, with keywords moving onto pages 1 and 2.

In very competitive cities, give it closer to 6 months.

Website pages move slower than your Google profile (often 2 to 4 weeks for organic changes to show), so start them early.
