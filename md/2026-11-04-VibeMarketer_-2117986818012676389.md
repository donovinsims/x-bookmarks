# The Complete Guide to Growing Your Brand With Positioning, Narrative & Content

## Post

Build your narrative, shape what people associate with you, and turn content into customers.

This guide will help you understand how people form an opinion of your brand, then deliberately build the positioning and narrative that make them want to follow you or buy from you.

Because you can spend months posting useful content and still leave people confused about what you do.

And when the moment comes where they should hire you, recommend you, or think of your product, the connection just isn’t there.

But there are deliberate strategies behind rapid audience growth you can tap into.

Position yourself around a problem people care about, give them a compelling explanation of what's happening, and give others reasons to spread that explanation. When that happens, you can grow very quickly.

So I'm going to break down the thinking behind those strategies, so you can apply it to your own brand or business.

We’re going to cover positioning, how to build your narrative, the beliefs your content needs to address, how to create proof for the ideas you want to own, and how to get that idea in front of people repeatedly.

I’ll also show you how I’d turn all of that into a month of content.

1. Start with the conclusion you want people to reach

There’s a story from Edward Bernays that explains this better than most modern marketing frameworks.

In Propaganda, he uses the example of selling pianos. Instead of starting with “buy a piano,” the idea is to make people want the room the piano belongs in. Get architects designing music rooms. Put them in exhibitions. Get people with taste talking about them.

Once somebody wants that kind of room in their house, the piano starts to feel like the obvious thing missing from it.

Key point IMO.

A lot of the highest-leverage work happens before somebody starts comparing products. If you can shape how they understand the problem, opportunity, or future, you also shape what they eventually look for in a solution.

So I’d start with one question:

What do I want people to believe before they ever get to the offer?

Then work backwards from there.

What do they believe today? What would have to change in their head for my product, service, or point of view to suddenly make more sense? And what could I actually show them that would help move them there?

That's where content starts to get very interesting.

Maybe you publish a post that makes them notice the problem. Then a teardown shows why the current approach breaks. A piece of research proves it is happening more broadly. A build or demo shows what the better version looks like. A customer story gives them proof that it works outside your own bubble.

Each piece is doing a different job, but they are all moving the audience in the same direction.

And if you do that well enough for long enough, someone can spend weeks getting closer to your worldview before they ever need what you sell.

So when the moment finally comes, you are not introducing the whole idea from scratch. They already understand the problem.. they already see the shift, and your offer fits into a story they have been following for a while.

BTW if you want a masterclass in this exact idea - how copy shifts the beliefs someone holds before they ever get to the offer read Alen Sultanic’s “Copywriter’s Guide to Shifting Beliefs.” It's one of the best breakdowns I’ve seen on narrative engineering from a direct-response angle.

2. Find the gap you have a right to own

This is basically how VibeMarketer started for me.

A couple of years ago, AI "marketing" content was exploding. n8n workflows everywhere. Agents, automations, increasingly elaborate demos getting huge attention.

A lot of it was cool, but I kept having the same reaction:

What would I actually use this for?

There was plenty of content showing what the tools could do and much less showing how an actual marketer would use them to make money, improve performance, research customers, create better ads, or operate differently.

And that gap made sense for me because I already came from marketing and growth.

I did not need to become the world’s best automation expert. I could answer a different question:

What happens when somebody who understands marketing gets access to all of this?

That became my wedge.

Look at your market the same way. Find the conversation already getting attention, then look one layer underneath it. What keeps getting asked? Where does the content stop right before it becomes genuinely useful?

Then ask whether you actually have something unusual to contribute there: experience, access, data, taste, technical skill, or a useful combination of backgrounds.

For me it was marketing + AI.

Today that has expanded further into things like how AI changes marketing, distribution, GTM, and what happens to the marketer as execution gets cheaper.

Those subjects all reinforce the same association.

And that also means leaving some good ideas alone.

A good content strategy is partly deciding what you refuse to become known for.

One simple test before committing to a position: can you already think of ten pieces of original work you could create around it?

Things you can build, test, explain, measure or argue from experience.

If all you have is a nice sentence for the bio, you probably have branding, not a position.

3. Give the position a point of view

Once you know the territory, decide what you actually believe about it.

A topic gives you things to post about. A point of view gives those posts somewhere to go.

I’d rather build around a belief about what is changing in the market and keep finding new ways to prove, challenge and explain it.

That is what turns positioning into narrative.

When Salesforce launched, it could have positioned itself as another CRM with better features.

Instead, it attacked the delivery model itself: “The End of Software.”

That changes the buyer’s question from “Is Salesforce a better CRM?" ... to “do I still want to buy and maintain software this way at all?”

Which was obviously a much better frame for Salesforce.

And they reinforced it everywhere: the messaging, the launch, the mock protests outside a Siebel conference, the events. The narrative and the go-to-market were saying the same thing.

Another example was Gong’s “Goodbye opinions. Hello, reality.”

The enemy was managing sales from gut feel.

Then Gong Labs kept publishing research based on actual sales conversations like talk ratios, pricing, competitor mentions, and patterns in successful calls.

A salesperson could care about that research without caring about Gong.

But every study also made the same underlying argument more believable:

sales conversations contain valuable data, and you should probably be analyzing them.

That is what I want content to do.

Help the audience with something real while quietly strengthening the worldview behind the brand.

For VibeMarketer, one of my core beliefs is that AI is making execution dramatically cheaper, which pushes the valuable part of marketing toward judgment, systems and distribution.

A Figma Marketing Engineer role becomes evidence. An agent workflow becomes a practical example. A “super IC” piece becomes the career implication. A company building a creator machine becomes another proof point.

Formats might change but the conclusion keeps getting stronger.

So when everyone is talking about some new launch, I would not ask:

“How do I post about this too?”

I’d ask:

What does this give me a chance to prove, question, or explain about the world I already talk about?

That's the difference between covering news and building a narrative.

4. Map your content to where the audience is

Once you have the position and the narrative, the next question is simple:

What does the audience need to hear next?

Not everyone seeing your content is at the same stage. Someone might not even recognize the problem yet. Someone else already understands it and just wants proof that your approach works.

Take an AI marketing workflow as an example.

If someone still thinks their current process is fine, a 20-minute tutorial  will probably not move them. You have to first show them why the workflow is worth changing.

And then once they accept the problem, you show what a better version looks like. Then teach them how to do it. Then finally, show why your product, service, or approach is worth trusting.

That gives you a much more practical content funnel:

Top of funnel → create awareness. Reach new people and introduce the problem, shift, or opportunity.

Middle of funnel → build belief and trust. Show your thinking, proof, processes, results, and practical depth.

Bottom of funnel → make the commercial connection. Show the offer, who it is for, results, testimonials, and what to do next.

I would not copy some fixed 60/30/10 content ratio from another account. The mix depends on what you sell.

If you are trying to grow a broad personal brand, you will probably need more top-of-funnel content. If you sell a high-ticket offer to a very specific ICP, you can afford to spend much more time in the middle and bottom of the funnel.

The useful question is: where is my audience getting stuck?

If they read but do not follow, the positioning might be weak. If they follow but never use anything, the content may not be practical enough. If they use your ideas but still do not understand what you sell, the bottom of the funnel is missing.

Figure out the gap first.. then make the next piece.

5. Use language people can pass on

Once the positioning is clear, you need language people can actually remember.

A useful test is to ask: what would someone say if they recommended your account or company to a friend?

“JB posts about AI and marketing” is cool but does'nt carry much of the positioning with it. “He shows marketers how to actually use AI and talks about what the role is turning into” is much stronger.

Most of your positioning happens when you are not in the room. Someone forwards an article, mentions you in a meeting, or explains your product to a colleague. If the idea needs a five-minute explanation, a lot of it gets lost.

I like creating a small vocabulary around the brand:

You do not have to invent every term. I certainly did not invent “marketing engineer” or “super IC.” The opportunity is to keep explaining those ideas through your particular worldview until people start connecting them with you.

And then use the same language everywhere: content, homepage, sales deck, founder posts, podcast appearances, product launches.

You will get bored of your own phrases much faster than the market will.

Keep using them...

A simple check: show somebody five pieces of your content and ask what they think you talk about. Their answer is a much better measure of your positioning than whatever you wrote in your bio.

6. Show the work

If you want to be known for something, produce work people can judge.

Saying “I understand AI marketing” is weak. Building a workflow that solves an actual marketing problem is much stronger.

This is why I like builds, experiments and teardowns so much. They let you show the outcome, but also the judgment behind it.

And don't make the mistake of only showing the polished demo. Show what went wrong, or what you rejected. Show the part the AI could not handle. Explain why you chose one approach over another.

That is where people start learning how you think.

7. Give people something worth spreading

Your narrative gets much stronger once other people start carrying it for you.

The mistake is thinking, “Who can promote this?”

A better question is what can I create that people in the market genuinely want to react to, use, test, or share?

That could be a benchmark, a dataset, a teardown, a strong take, a public experiment, or a product people can actually try.

A good example is Hyperorganic’s creator wrap-up. It turns someone’s own content data into a visual summary of what they are known for, which posts drove the most attention, and which topics dominate their content.

The key here is that the output is about the creator, so they actually want to share it.

The best share loops make the person sharing look smart, interesting, successful, or more self-aware.

The same idea applies elsewhere. Let people test the workflow, compare themselves against the benchmark, join the experiment, or add their own result.

The more useful or personally relevant the output is, the less you have to force the distribution yourself.

For a startup, that is especially valuable because people can start engaging with the idea before they are ready to buy.

8. Turn the narrative into a content campaign

Once the pieces are there, I would stop thinking about content one post at a time.

Pick one important narrative and spend a month developing it properly.

For VibeMarketer, the core idea is along the lines of:

AI is moving marketing away from manual execution and toward systems, judgment and distribution.

Then structure the month around four jobs:

The topics can look completely different while still pointing in the same direction.

A Figma hiring breakdown. A tutorial on building an agent. A “super IC” article. A creator-distribution case study. A meme about giant GTM teams.

That is the part people sometimes misunderstand about consistency.

You do not need to repeat the same sentence 100 times.

You want 100 different pieces of evidence pointing toward the same conclusion.

Then repeat the cycle with new evidence. Most of your audience did not see everything the first time anyway.

9. Concentrate the signal when you have something worth launching

Once you know the narrative works, you can go much harder.

I like the “lightning strike” idea from Play Bigger: instead of spreading every asset across months, concentrate the activity into a short window.

The point is not to fake momentum.

The point is to make sure the real things you are doing land together instead of disappearing one at a time.

If someone encounters you four times that week, from four different sources, they should hear roughly the same story.

Then keep talking about it.

Most brands stop repeating a message right around the point the market is finally starting to notice it.

10. Measure what people are starting to know you for

Views matter, but they're only one signal.

A piece can do huge numbers and build completely the wrong association.

So I would track performance at a few different levels:

The association layer is the one most people ignore.

Pay attention to what new contacts say when they reach out. Ask customers how they found you. Look at the words people use to describe the problem.

If somebody starts using your language back to you, that is a very strong signal.

For VibeMarketer, a workflow demo going viral because people think it is a cool AI trick tells me something very different from marketers sending it around because they think it changes how their team should work.

Both generate views, but only one is really strengthening the position I care about.

Then use the metrics diagnostically.

If people view but do not follow, the promise might be weak. If they follow but never use anything, you might need more practical depth. If they love the work but do not know what you sell, the commercial bridge is missing.

Build your own version

Now if I were doing this from scratch, I'd fill out one page with eight questions:

What does my audience already care about?

What are they still missing from the content they see?

What do I have (experience, access, data, skills or taste) that lets me contribute something different?

What do I believe is changing?

What does that change mean for the audience?

Which beliefs are stopping them from seeing it the same way, and what evidence could change those beliefs?

What language do I want them repeating?

Once they agree with the narrative, what do I want them to do next?

Then use those answers as a filter for what you publish.

You could even hand this to your AI agent and say:

“Based on these answers, help me turn this into a clear positioning statement, a 3–4 pillar content strategy, and 20 content ideas that all reinforce the same narrative. Flag anything that feels too broad, generic, or disconnected from the position I’m trying to own.”

That should be plenty to get you from a blank page to something you can actually start using.

And obviously this doesn't mean every post you put out has to perfectly reinforce your positioning. That would get boring very fast.

But zoom out over three months and the body of your work should make the association stronger.

People should know why they follow you. They should know roughly what you believe. They should understand the problems you are unusually good at helping with.

And when one of those problems finally becomes important enough to act on, ideally your name is already sitting there 🫡
