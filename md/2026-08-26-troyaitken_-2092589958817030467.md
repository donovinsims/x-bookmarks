# How to Send 100,000 Cold Emails a Day and Get Smarter Every Week

## Post

Most outbound teams treat their campaigns like school projects.

You build the list. You write the copy. You submit to your teacher. You wait for the grade. Then you do it again, slightly differently, hoping the next version works better than the last.
 
That is not a system. That is just resubmitting projects with revisions hoping for a better grade. 

The difference between a campaign and a compounding engine is what happens between sends. 
Does the pipeline learn? 
Does it kill what is not working before it wastes another thousand sends? 
Does it carry the winning patterns forward into the next campaign automatically, or does the next copywriter start from scratch?

We built a client an engine that runs at 30,000 emails a day, heading toward 100,000, with a weekly scoring loop that kills losing variants, promotes winning ones, and builds a library of proven framework and angle combinations that every future campaign inherits.

The product is a payroll tax benefit. The market is every US business with 50 or more full-time W-2 employees. The TAM is 537,868 qualifying companies before contacts. Roughly triple that when enriched. The system has to work at that scale without a human touching every campaign.

Here is how it works.
The architecture
 
One dispatcher. One environment variable per Railway service. Every job runs from the same entry point.

The scale problem

At 30,000 emails a day, you cannot write campaigns one at a time.

You also cannot run one variant per campaign and call it a test. One variant tells you whether that specific combination of framework, angle, and persona worked. It tells you nothing about which element was responsible or which combination to try next.

The engine generates 20 variants per campaign. Not 20 versions of the same email. 20 distinct combinations across a controlled matrix of dimensions.
 
Each Step-1 slot fixes the framework and persona tier. The model chooses the angle, offer type, proof element, CTA type, subject style, and length tier within those constraints, validated against the schema's enums.

One call per slot, not one call for the whole matrix. That isolation matters. A rejected slot retries alone. It does not contaminate the other 19 variants that already passed.

The angle taxonomy

This is the piece most outbound systems skip and then wonder why they cannot learn anything from their data.

If every variant's angle is described as a unique prose sentence, the scoring system can never identify a winner. It can tell you that variant 7 worked. It cannot tell you that the "skeptic disarm" angle works across campaigns, because variant 7 called it something slightly different from variant 12 and the system has no way to connect them.

Every angle is a named entry in a controlled vocabulary. 21 angles. Each with a stable key, a description of what it argues, and guidance on when to use it.
 
Some angles were in the brief from day one. Some emerged from early generation runs and got named and added because they kept showing up on their own. The emergent ones are the interesting ones. That is discovery, not planning.

A key stays stable once it is assigned. The description can change. The key cannot. Scoring is built on the key. If you rename it, you sever the history.

The segment system

At this scale, copy that sounds generic is worse than no copy at all.

A construction company with 250 employees does not sound like a SaaS CFO at a 100-person firm. They use different words for their people, their work, their problems. An email that cites a gym as a case study when landing in a manufacturing owner's inbox signals immediately that you are talking to someone else.

Every industry in the pipeline has a segment profile: what the workforce looks like, what the organization calls its own people and work, and which approved case study is the closest real match by workforce shape and headcount.
 
Matching is by workforce shape first, then headcount ratio. A segment with no shape-compatible reference gets case_study = None and leans on the compliance opinion and per-participant figure instead. Citing a business the reader recognizes as unlike their own is worse than citing none. Inventing a reference company is not an option.

That rule exists because someone tried to relax it once. The comment in the code still explains why it went back.
The spam guard

Before any variant is considered generated, it runs through a deterministic guard that encodes the full banned vocabulary programmatically.

This is not a vibe check... it is a compiled set of rules that catches what the model will reliably try to sneak past.
 
Beyond vocabulary, the guard enforces structural rules. The Proximity rule bans hypothetical framing: "can help," "could help," "might be able to," "we will build." The email must position the outcome as already in motion, not as something that might happen if the prospect agrees.

It also enforces the scheme-priming ban. One of the angles is called "skeptic disarm", it is designed to preempt the "this sounds like a tax scheme" objection. An earlier version of the angle named the objection explicitly, which planted the doubt rather than answering it. The guard now catches any variant that voices the suspicion before defusing it. The angle was rewritten. The key stayed the same so the historical scoring data did not get severed.

Six retries per slot, not three. The constraint stack roughly doubled in one session: mechanism clause, readability ceiling, abstraction ban, segment register, on top of the existing length and spintax checks. At three retries, slots were exhausting attempts on three different legitimate problems rather than one stubborn one. A dropped slot costs an A/B cell for the whole campaign. Six retries is cheaper than a missing column.
The scoring loop

Nightly. Two grains. The distinction took three days and two pushbacks to get right.

Per-variant scoring uses the EmailBison step-level stats endpoint.
 
Returns sequence_step_stats broken out per sequence step — sent, replies, interested. A sequence step is a variant. Aggregate per-variant performance is exactly what A/B testing needs.

The mistake was generalising "we cannot know which variant any one lead received" into "per-variant performance is unmeasurable." Those are different claims. One is about individual lead attribution. The other is about aggregate performance. The endpoint was there the whole time.

Promote threshold: 500 delivered, 1 positive reply.
Retire threshold: 1,000 delivered, 0 positive replies.
 
One guard on the retire path: if a campaign has accumulated 25 or more total replies but zero interested tags, that is probably a tagging failure, not a copy failure. Someone is on holiday. The reply sync broke. A workspace permission changed. Auto-retiring campaigns because the inbox manager stopped tagging would poison the copy library with false negatives on frameworks that actually work. The _tagging_looks_broken check catches that before it happens.

The copy library

This is what makes the engine compound rather than just run.

Every framework and angle combination that earns a validated verdict across two or more campaigns gets promoted to copy_library. Future generation calls read the library before writing. They know which pairs have produced positive replies, at what volume, in which segments. That context shapes the generation without hardcoding it.

A pair earns a place in the library on its own measured evidence at the variant grain. Stricter than the per-campaign promote rule, deliberately. The library is write-once and compounds into every future campaign. A wrong entry is far more expensive than a slow one.
 
After enough cycles, the library contains what actually works in this market, for this product, for this audience. Not what a copywriter thought would work. What buyers responded to.

The list engine

1,046,012 companies ingested. 537,868 qualifying after targeting filters. Contacts building from a multi-stage waterfall: corpus sweep, contact vacuum, gap fill.

The list builder applies four rules on every contact selection: DNC suppression at the email and domain level. Format validation on first name and email. Global deduplication against status. And a 90-day recycle rule: a contact with status=sent and fewer than three prior sends goes back into the queue after 90 days. Not forever. Three times. Then they are done.
 
At 30,000 emails a day against a 537,000-company universe, the math works. The list does not fatigue because the recycle window is long enough to let the market breathe and short enough to stay relevant.

The deploy

One Railway project. One dispatcher. Each service sets its own job via environment variable.
 
Adding a new job requires no code change. Add a line to the JOBS dict in dispatch.py, set the environment variable on a new Railway service, and it runs.
 
Where this goes

30,000 a day is Phase 1. The infrastructure runs the same way at 100,000.

The copy library keeps building. Every week more winning pairs get promoted. Every week the generation model has more evidence to work with. Every week the campaigns the engine writes are informed by more real signal from real buyers.

That is the compounding part. Not just that the system scales. That it gets better as it runs.

Most outbound engines stop learning the moment the campaign launches. This one starts learning then.
