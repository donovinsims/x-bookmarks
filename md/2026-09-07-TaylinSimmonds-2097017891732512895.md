# I’m not disciplined. But here’s how I made a lazy $18K this month

## Post

I’ve never been a “disciplined” person.

No morning routine. No 5am work block. None of that “wake up before the enemy” stuff.

But so far this month I’ve made $18,000. Most of it while I was, by conventional standards, slacking off. And I’ve been earning like this for about 4 years now.

How?

By finally accepting that my creative, multi-interested brain is wired different to those who worship at the altar of The 5am Club.

And if you’re a deeply creative person, whether that be a writer or an entrepreneur, who’s ever felt like a failure for not being able to stick to a rigid work schedule, this article might finally explain you to yourself.

Because I’m betting “discipline” was never your problem. You’re just disciplined in a completely different way.

You’re a hunter. But you’ve been living as a farmer. And by leaning into your nature, over a long enough time horizon, you’ll have a much better chance at earning stable income your way, on your terms.

The guilt creative people never chose

Like many 20 something year olds, Gary V inspired me to become an entrepreneur. I hate him for it. Because back then I had no idea that I wasn’t the same type of person as him. I just assumed I was defective.

The world runs on type-A personalities that assume there’s only one way to be a functional, high-earning human. Work for 8 straight hours (or more). 300+ days a year. Fuelled by caffeine, nicotine and the drive to get so rich your ex regrets dumping you for the guy with the jet-black Lamborghini.

But when I tested this lifestyle for myself it felt off.

Forced. Against my nature.

Each day, I’d wake up after the enemy and the voice in my head would belittle me: Why aren’t you working already? Everyone else is working harder than you. You should be more like Hemingway & Hormozi.

Creative work is hard enough as it is. My inner critic steps in to judge every article, software and poem I create. Now imagine that critic working a double shift, telling me I’m shit before I’ve even had the chance to take a shower. It made entering flow states more difficult. Let alone actually publishing work consistently. That is, until an ADHD course from the Harvard-trained psychiatrist Dr. K pointed me to the hypothesis behind the hunter and farmer idea I opened with. It’s what finally shut my red-faced inner critic down. And if you carry that same guilt, shame, or pressure to conform to society’s robotic work standards, it can shut yours down too.

Here’s where it comes from.

ADHD as an evolutionary theory

The internet loves to claim that ADHD is a superpower. As someone who was diagnosed, I think that’s a little silly... but also not completely off-base. The same traits that got me labeled disordered helped me build a 6-figure writing biz on my own creative terms. Before I explain how, a Polymathic Psychotherapist named Thom Hartmann (who happens to be a massive Substacker) might be able to explain why.

After Hartmann’s son was diagnosed with ADHD, he started wondering why traits like impulsivity, novelty-seeking, and hyper-focus exist at all. If they were purely a defect, evolution should have filed them down long ago. So why did they survive?

Hartmann believed these traits survived because they were useful for hunters. But less suited to the rigid work schedules and routines of farmers.

The problem is, farmers built the modern world. Any advantage hunters had years ago seems to have faded away... or has it?

I’ve since tested Hartmann’s hypothesis as a way of life. And trust me, you’ll want to know which one you are.

Because the frantic, creative mind that helped my ancestors dine on juicy meat made me so broke I was dining on $1 ramen noodles. Until I learned how to harness my hunter nature in the modern world...

The Farmer lives by the clock

“Sleep until I wake up. Earplugs. Noise-cancelling headphones. Nicotine. Coffee. A room with no windows and no distractions. Do Not Disturb on the phone.”

This is Alex Hormozi’s morning routine. It’s a monument to protecting his consistency. And to a farmer, that monument is sacred. Showing up every day is what progress feels like.

Farmers love predictability. Same time, same work, same desk, every day. They run on repetition, and it doesn’t cost them their soul. They love slow burn living.

Think engineers, accountants, lawyers, executives. Entrepreneurs. Even certain artist archetypes like Hirohiko Araki (the creator of JoJo’s Bizare Adventure). Anyone whose work rewards showing up and doing the same thing exceptionally well, over and over, without wearing them down.

If that’s you, keep farming. It works. And we need you to keep society functioning.

But to me, the farmer’s routine reads like a description of my own personal hell. And if that’s true for you too, you already know you’re wired against the modern system. The system that will never tell you there’s another way, because another way doesn’t serve it. It needs farmers. So anyone else is ostracized and made to believe they’re messed in the head.

That lie cost me years of meaningful, creative work. Years of calling myself lazy. A decade of my own approval. A significant chunk of my life that could’ve been lived my way, on my terms.

But I, like you, was never broken. We were built to hunt.

The Hunter lives by their obsession

This idea from Naval reframed how I see my own laziness:

Lions don’t chase prey all day. They wait. They observe. Then, when the perfect moment comes, they attack with everything they’ve got.

―Naval Ravikant

Hunters are like lions.

Conserve → Hunt → eat → conserve.

Urgency, novelty, and high stimulation sharpen their mind, while routine drains them dry. They don’t see boredom as a “sacrifice to be made at the altar of success.” They see it as a sign they’re working on the wrong things.

But when something matters... they become obsessive. They work in short, intense bursts until they’re empty (which is often how their best work is created). And they repeat when the tank is full again and inspiration strikes, not when the clock says so.

As a result, I have a strong hunch that they’re wired for creative, insight-driven work:

Writers

Designers

Musicians

Filmmakers

Innovation-focused founders

Think Leonardo DaVinci, who spent his days wandering the city, sketching what caught his eye, and wondering what the tongue of a woodpecker looked like. A quick glance at one of his notebooks shows you just how creative Leonardo was:

If you judged him day-by-day, he might look lazy or inconsistent. But zoom out—centuries out—and what you see is masterpieces.

I believe it’s because Leonardo didn’t track hours worked.

He did whatever led to him producing the best work.

And that’s how hunters are meant to live. It’s also why I don’t force myself to work like a farmer.

But let me make this concrete. Because “embrace your hunter instincts” can be easily misinterpreted into permission to fuck around, avoid the pain of discipline, and never commit yourself to anything meaningful. So to avoid confusion, let’s paint a picture of what this actually looks like on a random Tuesday. That way you can align with your nature to build a creative life on your own terms without starving like an incompetent hunter.

What modern hunting actually looks like (and how it pays)

“The secret to doing good research is always to be a little underemployed. You waste years by not being able to waste hours.” ―Amos Tversky

I spend large chunks of my day walking, meditating, reading, staring at the roof, and looking for big ideas that bring childlike wonder to my eyes.

On the surface, I look lazy.

My workdays are rarely structured. And I don’t have a preset schedule. But when something grabs me, I work in short, intense bursts and get a massive amount done fast.

I don’t measure my productivity by hours or days. I measure it by weekly output.

For writing, I collect ideas as voice notes while I walk. No desk, no pressure. And then once a week, in one long charged sitting, I edit and schedule the entire week’s posts at once.

For products, I let an idea marinate in the background of my mind for weeks until enough of it clicks into place. Then I build the whole thing in a week-long sprint. When I first discovered vibe-coding, I spent an entire week building a software to synthesize multiple interests into a cohesive brand philosophy. Barely slept. Didn’t require any discipline. I just couldn’t stop creating.

This isn’t a traditional routine.

Corporate would never accept it.

Many successful people would happily spit on it.

But it’s the routine of a creative. And it’s worked for me, to the tune of a lazy $18,000 this month. The once per week burst of inspiration gets out all my writing and marketing materials. The week-long sprints build the software, consulting packages, and systems my customers buy. The meditating and ‘wasted hours’ are where all the ideas come from.

The bursts just turn them into something I can tangibly earn with.

With that said, I’m not a delusional idealist. There is a reality that every hunter has to face sooner or later if they want to be successful in a world built by farmers. And if you don’t align with it, you really will struggle to build a stable career and earn with your creative interests.

How to hunt in the modern world

When I first realized I was a hunter, the realization alone didn’t improve my life. It actually made it worse because I’d use it as an excuse to dodge responsibility. I’d rely too heavily on inspiration. So when I was uninspired, I’d rest for too long. Sprint too rarely. Then wonder why I couldn’t finish any writing or land good paying work.

I don’t know about you, but I can’t just sit around waiting for inspiration to strike. I have a business to run. Clients to serve. A mortgage to pay.

That’s why being a hunter isn’t about avoiding discipline. It’s about understanding how you produce your best work, and optimizing your life around your creative bursts.

Which is why I recommend that all modern hunters adopt the following three principles:

1) Guard your peak window like it’s territory.

As a hunter, the point isn’t when you work. It’s whether you work during your peak cognitive window.

Every hunter has a time of day, a sacred zone, when their mind feels sharp, alive, and strange in the best way. For me, it feels like my brain gets a jolt of energy around 8pm and fades around 2am. For you, it might be early morning or mid day after light exercise.

Protect that time.

If you have a day job, carve out your protected pocket around it. For a serious hunter, keeping this window clear of meetings, chores, and other people’s priorities is non-negotiable.

2) Your conditions are more important than your time.

Guarding sacred time is step one. What happens inside it is step two, and that’s not always the focused-work hours you might expect.

Hunters don’t roam on a clock. They roam when conditions are right. For example, if I wake up foggy, dysregulated, or uninspired, I don’t force work. Even if it’s during my peak cognitive window. I regulate my nervous system first with meditation, breathwork, or a walk.

Because a calm nervous system expands my mind, making creative bursts more likely. I call it productive delay. I’m not procrastinating by doing something easier. I’m respecting my nature and taking 20 minutes to work with it, rather than against it.

3) Stalk inspiration. Don’t wait for it.

I don’t wait for inspiration like lightning. I track it like an animal, by learning which paths it most frequently visits.

Inspiration usually comes to me while meditating, doing holotropic breathwork, or when I’m bored staring at the roof. I also know I can find it when studying philosophy, consciousness, or other people’s creative process.

Make daily contact with your fields of fascination.

Give yourself permission to hunt

Most of the world is farmers, and most farmers will tell you their way is the only way. Some of them will look at how you work and feel personally offended by it. Disgusted even.

That’s fine. They’re not wrong about themselves. They’re just wrong about you. And dead wrong about the type of discipline you need.

And if you keep taking their word over your own nature, you get another year of dragging yourself through a schedule that erodes you. Another year of producing your worst work during your best hours. Another year of second-guessing if the problem is you.

Because forcing yourself to farm is a practice at being someone who believes they’re broken.

So don’t. Develop a smarter rhythm instead of a strict routine. Track down inspiration instead of waiting for it. Create peak work hours instead of forcing 8 uninspired ones.

Stop trying to farm with a mind that was built to hunt.
