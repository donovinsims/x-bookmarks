# Our Full Cold Calling System

## Post

We're writing this because cold calling is the channel B2B teams give up on fastest, and on one of our biggest builds it booked most of the meetings.

There's no single resource that lays out how a calling system works from end to end, so THIS is that resource.

It comes out of 700K cold calls and 17M+ cold emails run for 100+ B2B companies, behind $100M+ in pipeline.

By the end, the whole system is on the page:

The half a prospect hears, the half they never see, and one account followed through both.

Why the phone is open again

In 2017, a sequencer and 1,000 emails booked around 10 meetings at an 8.5% reply rate

That window closed between 2022 and 2024.

Every sales team bought a sequencer, Google and Yahoo tightened their sender rules in February 2024, and average reply rates fell to around 3.4%.

The whole market moved from the phone to the inbox in the same few years.

So the average B2B decision maker now gets 15 cold emails a week and almost no real calls.

Cold calling in 2026 sits about where cold email sat in 2017.

Here's what that looked like for our client Eden Studio, a company selling landscape design software to contractors.

Email and phone ran into the same market for 50 days.

41,150 emails booked 20 meetings

32,577 dials booked 33 meetings

The phone booked 62% of the meetings while making up 44% of the touchpoints.

Per booked meeting, the phone needed about 987 dials and email needed about 2,058 sends.

Calling has 2 halves

When people picture cold calling, they picture the call itself.

A rep, a headset, a script, and someone picking up on the other end.

That's half of it.

The other half sits underneath, and the prospect never sees any of it:

The list, and which contacts on it get called at all

The phone data behind every number

The dialer, and the hours it runs in

The routing that decides who calls whom, and how fast

A great caller on a bad list spends the day talking to receptionists.

Put an untrained caller on a great list, and the best accounts in the market get burned through in a week.

Both halves have to be built, and they get built in order:

The hidden half first, then the half the prospect hears.

The half the prospect never sees

It starts with the whole market.

On Eden, the list was every landscaping and hardscaping business in the market, 69,204 of them, scraped off Google Maps city by city.

Each account got enriched across 19 data points, from the owner's name and direct phone number to the Google review count and whether the website was live.

The ICP came out of closed won data:

Landscaping companies with 11 to 50 employees, big enough to have budget and small enough to have no in-house designer.

Every account then got scored on fit and intent, using signals like hiring activity, outsourced design work, paid ads and portfolio quality.

That scored map is what both the callers and the emails work from.

The calling list and the email list are one list.

Every account gets 3 to 5 contacts, because a single contact on an account is one job change away from a dead account.

Before a single email goes out, we pull the email provider behind every contact.

Contacts behind a security gateway like Mimecast or Barracuda get their own email campaign, and the phone leads on them.

Everyone else gets email and phone together.

Then the phone data.

Direct dials get enriched, and switchboard numbers get skipped.

A switchboard number puts a receptionist between the caller and the buyer on every dial.

The provider behind those numbers decides how many dials it takes to reach anyone useful.

Same list, dialer and reps once took 50 dials to reach one person in the right profile.

After moving the phone data to Prospeo, that dropped to around 15.

TitanX and LeadMagic sit next to it in the stack.

Then the dialer.

A rep dialing by hand gets through around 40 dials an hour.

A parallel dialer runs 150 to 300, and the caller only picks up when a real person answers.

We run Salesfinity, at 500 dials a day per caller.

Every connect gets logged with the time it happened.

After a couple of weeks, the log shows when each market picks up, and the dialing schedule moves to those hours.

Then the benchmark that checks all of it.

Eden's calling ran at a 9.2% connect rate, 2,991 connects off 32,577 dials.

That's the number every list on every build gets measured against.

Under 9%, the list is wrong, and the fix starts in the data before anyone touches the script.

The half the prospect hears

The callers.

This is the HARDEST piece of the system, and it's the main reason teams stop calling.

Callers are hard to hire, hard to train and hard to manage.

Every caller gets hired and trained on the client's product for 2 weeks before they make a single dial.

A caller guessing at what the product does loses the conversation inside the first 20 seconds.

The recordings.

Every connect lands in Slack with the recording attached.

The client hears exactly what got said on every conversation, the good ones and the bad ones.

The script.

Calling has 1 big advantage over every other channel:

The feedback comes back on every single conversation, in real time.

When calls keep dying at the same line, that line gets rewritten the same day.

The next 1,000 dials run on the new version.

Once a script works, it keeps working for a long time, with no inbox placement to protect and no domains burning out underneath it.

The loop back into email.

Every sales call gets recorded and transcribed into the client's GTM knowledge base.

New objections turn into new lines in the calling script.

The pains that keep coming up turn into angles for the next email campaign.

So each call improves the next 10,000 emails, and each email reply improves the next 1,000 dials.

From kickoff to the first dial

A calling build takes 3 weeks to go live, and the first 2 run on 2 tracks at once.

Track one is the setup.

The sequencer and inboxes go in on day 1, because new inboxes need 14 days of warming before they send a single cold email.

The callers get sourced in the same week and start their 2 weeks of training on the client's offer.

By the time the inboxes are ready, so are the callers.

Track 2 is the strategy, and it's where the value sits.

It starts with an executive brief on the ICP: the full TAM map, every segment inside it, every persona reachable in each segment, and the intent signals that flag who's ready now.

Our GTM skill in Claude Code produces the first pass from the client's domain in 5 to 10 minutes, and the team checks every line of it.

Then an ICP workshop with the client, where the tier one segments get agreed.

Then the scripts:

Several variants for every persona, drafted with AI and checked line by line by a person before anything goes out.

Clients rarely know what their own market looks like until they see it mapped.

Week 3 is launch.

Campaigns go live, straight into the client's CRM, and the calling starts once the callers are trained.

Eden's first week looked like this:

9 calls booked in the first 6 days, 2 of them on day one

1 interested email lead per 576 emails, at 1,000 emails a day

1 booked meeting per 800 dials on the phone

1 account, followed through the system

Here's how a single account moves through it, from the day it lands on the list to the day the meeting happens

Day 1: the account gets scored.

It gets scored against the client's own rubric before anyone touches it.

For Eden, the scores split into 3 tiers.

Tier 1 gets a special offer or a personal gift

Tier 2 gets called straight away

Tier 3 gets a template

The strongest callers spend 80% of their time on the top 10% of accounts.

Day 1: the contacts get checked.

3 to 5 people at the account get pulled, with direct dials and a check on which email provider each one sits behind.

If the company runs a security gateway, the phone leads.

Day 1 onwards: email and phone run together.

The email primes the call, so pickup rates climb when the prospect has already seen the name.

Reply rates climb after a voicemail gets left.

A blocklist keeps anyone in an active email conversation off the dialer.

The reply.

Say one of the contacts replies "interested" to an email.

The reply lands in Slack with the prospect's number and a named owner.

The owner calls inside a minute, instead of sending a calendar link.

When a positive reply picks up the phone, 80 to 90% of them book the meeting.

Inside 5 minutes, 20 to 30% of positive replies book.

After an hour, that drops to about 5%.

The booking.

The meeting gets booked inside 2 days.

A meeting 2 weeks out is a meeting the prospect forgets.

The days before the meeting.

With no follow-through, cold outbound meetings show 20 to 30% of the time.

This is what takes show rates to 60 to 80%:

A confirmation email with a 60-second video of who they'll meet, with the case study linked on the same page

A LinkedIn connection request the day they book Reminders at 24 hours, 4 hours and 1 hour, by email and text

A call from the rep before the meeting, whenever there's a number

If they still no-show, the follow-up restarts automatically.

That's 1 account.

The system runs that same path on every account in the market, every day.

"Email is cheaper"

Per send, it is.

Cold email has cheap data, volume with no ceiling, the fastest feedback across many angles at once, and about 90% of it runs automated.

It also carries deliverability risk, a crowded inbox and filters that change under it.

Cold calling has a high barrier, and the callers are hard to train and manage.

It also has no deliverability to worry about, works in almost every industry, and gives feedback on every single conversation.

Per booked meeting, the gap closed for Eden: about 987 dials against about 2,058 emails.

So email covers the full market every 30 to 60 days, and the phone turns the right part of it into conversations.

Both stay in the system.
