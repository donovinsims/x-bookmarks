# How To FORCE Meta to Bring You Higher Quality Leads (Step-By-Step)

## Post

I've spent $1,000,000s on paid ads across my own agency and dozens of other agencies and coaches.
Through Systemised, we also see inside the ad accounts of over 100 active agency owners and coaches.
The thing holding most of them back is the same.
Lead quality.
They're booking calls, but the wrong people keep showing up.
This is exactly how to fix it, step by step, so the people on your calendar can actually afford what you sell.
 
What Lead Quality Actually Means
Lead quality is hard to measure, but it comes down to how qualified someone is for your offer.
A high quality lead has 3 things:
The money. They can afford your offer, whether that's a high ticket coaching package or a recurring monthly service.
The attitude. They show up educated and ready to buy, ask a couple of questions, and give you their card.
The fit. They're the exact type of business you get great results for, with the right revenue, team size and offer.
If a lead has all 3, the sales call is easy.
If they're missing one, you get long calls, endless follow ups, or a client you can't actually help.
 
How Meta Sees Your Funnel
To control lead quality, you first need to understand how Meta reads what happens after someone clicks your ad.
It all comes back to the pixel and conversion events.
Take a standard book a call funnel:
The ad sends people to a landing page with the offer and an application form
After the form, they land on a call page with a calendar
After booking, they're redirected to a thank you page with testimonials
The pixel should be installed on every one of those pages.
Then you tell Meta what each page means.
Landing on the first page is just a page view.
Submitting the form and reaching the call page makes them a lead, because they've given you contact details.
Booking the call makes them a schedule.
These are called standard events, and they're how Meta learns what a result looks like for you.
 
Optimise for the Action You Actually Want
When you set up your campaign, you choose which event Meta should chase.
Optimising for page views is a mistake, because Meta will find you the cheapest possible people who view pages, which is junk traffic.
I optimise for schedules.
You'll hear people say you should optimise for whichever event gets you 50 conversions a week, so the campaign exits the learning phase.
In my experience, that only moves performance by a couple of percentage points.
Tell Meta the action you really want, which is a booked call.
 
The Lead Quality Problem
Once you optimise for schedules, every booked call gets sent back to Meta as a success.
Meta only sees the schedule.
It has no idea whether that person could afford you, showed up, or closed.
All it knows is that it got a result, and its goal is to get you that result at the cheapest possible cost.
That works perfectly for e-com, where it doesn't matter who buys a product as long as they buy it.
In lead generation, a cheaper cost per result can easily mean less money.
An example:
Say you spend $1,000 on 2 campaigns.
Campaign 1 gets a $100 cost per call, so you book 10 calls.
Campaign 2 gets a $300 cost per call, so you book around 3.
Campaign 1 looks better on paper.
But if those 10 calls are people with no money, a bad attitude and the wrong type of business, you could close none of them and make $0.
If campaign 2's 3 calls are qualified, warm and in your niche, they could all close on a $20,000 offer.
That's a theoretical $60,000 from the campaign with the "worse" cost per call.
That's the entire lead quality problem.
Meta wasn't built to factor in quality, so you have to stop low quality people from ever triggering the schedule event.
There are 6 levers that do it.

Step 1: Design an Offer the Top of the Market Wants
The first filter is the offer itself.
You want an offer that your best prospects are drawn to and your worst prospects instinctively avoid.
My own example:
On cold traffic, I offer to help agency owners and coaches build out their paid advertising system.
Baked into that is the assumption that only more established businesses are ready to spend money on ads.
People who aren't financially qualified hear "paid ads" and leave.
If I pushed a cold email offer instead, I'd attract far more people who can't afford me, because cold email is close to free to run.
The offer quietly decides who raises their hand.
 
Step 2: Write the Ad for Your Best Prospects
The ad is the second filter.
Use the language of the top of your market.
The more technical and specific your wording, the more you attract the businesses who actually speak that way.
For agency owners, beginners obsess over close rate.
More advanced people care about cash collected per call and revenue per call across a sales team.
Talking about one versus the other attracts completely different levels of business.
Choose the avatar carefully.
A sophisticated business owner who looks the part attracts a different audience to someone who looks like they're just starting out.
Call out a revenue range in the hook.
Naming the level of business you work with is one of the fastest ways to filter who stops scrolling.
 
Step 3: Make the Landing Page Clear and Professional
The landing page has a smaller effect, but it still matters.
The offer needs to be presented clearly and compellingly, and the page should look professional.
A cheap looking page tends to attract people who aren't serious buyers.
Step 4: Engineer the Application Form
This is the most important lever of all.
The form sits before any lead or schedule event fires.
That means it physically blocks the wrong people from ever being counted as a result.
The questions I like to include:
A niche question. A simple yes or no on whether they're in the market you serve.
A revenue question. Their average monthly revenue over the last 3 months, with a short reason for asking so people don't just lie.
An offer question. A text field asking them to describe their offer and pricing.
A context question. Something like average ad spend, employee count or monthly landing page traffic.
Mix the field types between yes or no, multiple choice and text, because it keeps people engaged instead of clicking through on autopilot.
Then ask for contact details last.
Name, email and phone number go at the end, with a partial submit set up so you can still contact anyone who fills in their details but doesn't book.
Then route people based on their answers.
If someone isn't in your niche, has very low revenue, or has never spent money on ads, the form automatically sends them to a disqualification page.
No lead event fires and no schedule event fires.
We use Typeform for this, so it happens without anyone on the team lifting a finger.
Keep tuning it
The form is never finished.
Your sales team, or you if you're taking the calls, will keep spotting patterns in who closes and who doesn't.
Use that feedback to tighten the criteria.
If you were disqualifying people under $5k a month, test disqualifying people under $10k a month and watch the average revenue of the people on your calls rise.
Use friction deliberately
More friction means fewer leads, and better ones.
Every extra question adds friction, and only genuinely interested people push through a demanding form.
The most extreme version is stating your price inside the form.
Something like "just so you know, our price starts at $10,000" is a huge point of friction.
The people who still say yes are almost always serious, as long as they aren't lying.
 
Step 5: Route Borderline Leads Without Training Meta on Them
The setup above is all you need to get started.
Once you're spending somewhere around $500 to $1,000 a day, there are 2 more advanced moves.
The first deals with leads you'd still like to talk to, but don't want Meta chasing.
Business revenue is one of the strongest predictors of whether someone closes and how much they pay.
Think of the market as a pyramid.
There are far more businesses at $5k to $10k a month than at $100k a month, so they're cheaper for Meta to find.
As you move up the pyramid, CPMs and cost per call rise, but so does the close rate.
You might close 30% of prospects at $25k to $50k a month, and only 10% at $5k to $10k.
If Meta optimises toward the lower band, you get cheap calls that rarely close, clog your calendar, and take slots from better prospects.
The fix is conditional logic in the form.
Under $5k a month gets disqualified completely
$5k to $10k a month gets sent to a separate call page with its own calendar, usually linked to a setter or a ramping closer
That booking lands on a thank you page that looks identical but doesn't fire the schedule event
Everyone above your threshold goes through the main flow and fires the pixel as normal
You keep the extra call volume without teaching Meta to find more of the wrong people.
 
Step 6: Fire the Conversion Manually With the Conversions API
The second advanced move gives you the most control.
So far, the pixel has been installed in the browser, meaning code on the page fires the event automatically.
The alternative is the Conversions API, also called server side tracking.
This is how it works:
Remove the schedule event from the page completely, so self booked calls don't fire anything
Send the conversion back to Meta manually from your CRM instead
Only fire it once your sales team confirms the lead is genuinely qualified
You can trigger it from tools like GHL or Close, or through Zapier.
I'm not a developer, which is why team members handle the technical setup for me.
The result is that Meta only ever learns from people your team has personally qualified.
Every step here moves you further along the same spectrum.
More friction and more control usually means a higher cost per call.
It also usually means a lower cost to acquire a client, because your sales team spends their day talking to people who can actually buy.
 
Why This Compounds
Improving lead quality on organic content doesn't change how a platform distributes your posts, whereas on Meta it feeds straight back into targeting.
Every qualified lead that fires the pixel tells Meta to go and find more people like them.
Those people fire the pixel too, which sharpens the targeting again.
Your performance improves on a loop.
In my own business and in clients' businesses, fixing lead quality has taken close rates from around 15% to 30%, and show rates from around 40% to 80%.
When your sales team is speaking to 8 qualified prospects a day, closing most of them stops being unusual.
Start with the form, because it's the biggest lever and the fastest to change.
Then work up to the advanced routing once your spend justifies it.
