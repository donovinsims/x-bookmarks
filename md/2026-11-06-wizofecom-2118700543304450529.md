Every content format needs a specific purpose

Before I get into the formats, I want to explain something about how we build our content strategies.

When somebody comes into Mogul Media, we spend a ridiculous amount of time on the foundations.

We look at their business.

→ Their ICP

→ Their revenue level

→ The problems their buyers are trying to solve

→ The keywords those buyers are interested in

→ And the micro-communities where they spend their time

We also figure out exactly how we want the founder to be perceived.

For example, an agency owner doing $20M a year probably has an enormous amount of experience building teams, acquiring clients, improving margins, and solving problems inside their industry.

But if all we do is publish generic marketing tips on their account, we're wasting a huge amount of that expertise.

Their content should make the right buyers associate their name with the problems they actually solve.

That's where the content formats come in.

We generally build around three layers:

TOP OF FUNNEL: Reach the right people.

Opinion posts, relevant industry conversations, snipes, and broader educational content help introduce the founder to people inside their target market.

MIDDLE OF FUNNEL: Establish authority.

Articles, technical breakdowns, infographics, Google Doc screenshots, and founder stories show people how the founder thinks and operates.

BOTTOM OF FUNNEL: Generate opportunities.

Case studies, client results, testimonials, and promotions give interested prospects a reason to take the next step.

One topic can travel through multiple format

Let's say you're a CRO agency owner.

You could publish an article explaining the conversion problems you keep finding inside eight-figure ecommerce brands.

Take the strongest framework and turn it into an infographic.

Quote a relevant conversation about conversion rates with your opinion.

Then show an actual client result demonstrating what happened when you fixed one of those problems.

Same expertise. Multiple ways for the right person to discover it.

Articles are one of our strongest authority-building formats

I've become a huge fan of X articles.

We run them on my account and across client accounts because they let us demonstrate a level of expertise that a normal post cannot fit.

Think about somebody who has spent the last decade operating a successful business.

They've developed internal systems.

Made expensive mistakes.

Solved problems their competitors haven't encountered yet.

And have an enormous amount of experience that could be valuable to their market.

Trying to communicate all of that through a 10-line tweet is pretty limiting.

An article gives us room to take one specific problem and explain exactly how the founder solves it.

We can include:

→ Their actual process

→ Screenshots from inside the business

→ Technical explanations

→ Case studies and results

→ Frameworks the reader can implement

→ Their opinion on why something works

The perfect prospect is usually an established founder.

They've already built something successful.

They're not looking for another motivational quote about consistency.

They want to understand whether you actually know what we're doing.

A great article lets somebody evaluate your expertise before ever speaking to you.

How we actually create them

We rarely need to come up with everything from scratch.

Our founders are already sitting on valuable information.

It usually exists inside:

→ internal SOPs

→ founder interviews

→ client call transcripts

→ winning X posts

→ processes their teams use

→ real business results

Our job is to identify the strongest idea, build the argument around it, and package it into something people want to read.

And the packaging matters enormously.

A strong title communicates the outcome.

The banner earns attention.

The opening establishes credibility and tells the reader exactly what they're going to learn.

The article itself delivers the process.

What to do:

Take one process inside your business that repeatedly produces a valuable outcome.

Write down the steps, decisions, and common mistakes involved.

Then turn it into an article explaining how the process works and why you approach it that way.

If you're an established operator, you probably have years of this material sitting inside your business already.

Google Doc screenshots make technical value easier to consume

Google Doc screenshots have become a staple across our content strategies.

I use them constantly because they make it much easier to package valuable information in a way people actually want to consume.

I've had individual content-system visuals on my account generate tens of thousands of views and hundreds of bookmarks.

And this is a format we've continued to develop across our founder accounts.

At the beginning, it was literally just plain text on a white Google Doc.

Then we started experimenting with better hierarchy.

Different heading styles.

Tables.

Color-coded sections.

Diagrams.

More intentional visual layouts.

The underlying concept stayed the same.

Take an idea that's valuable and make it ridiculously easy to understand.

This works particularly well for technical content.

Think about a founder explaining a complicated process involving attribution, customer acquisition, email marketing, or AI infrastructure.

Written out, that explanation might take 800 words.

A well-designed document screenshot can make the structure understandable within a few seconds.

And that increases the chances somebody saves it, shares it with their team, or comes back to it later.

KEEP IN MIND:

The same topic will not perform equally across formats.

You can take a great technical idea and write it as a plain-text tweet.

Then take that same idea and package it into a document screenshot.

The second version might make the information significantly easier to consume.

Which is why we test vehicles alongside topics.

What to do:

Find a technical explanation you've already written.

Extract the underlying framework.

Give it a strong headline, organize it into clear sections, and remove anything the reader doesn't need to understand the concept.

Then publish the visual and compare how it performs against your original written version.

Pay attention to bookmarks, profile visits, and the actual people engaging with the post.

Custom infographics help founders own complicated topics

There's another visual format we use that's worth separating from Google Doc screenshots.

Custom infographics.

These are especially useful for founders whose expertise involves complicated processes, systems, or technical information.

A SaaS founder might need to explain how different parts of their platform work together.

An agency owner might want to break down their customer acquisition process.

A technical operator might want to visualize an internal workflow.

Those are all subjects where a diagram can make the information easier to follow.

The strongest founder accounts in technical industries know how to clearly communicate complicated ideas.

They make their knowledge accessible to people who might not have the same depth of experience.

Your potential buyers aren't always experts in the service you provide.

A CEO hiring a retention agency probably doesn't want to read 20 paragraphs about the technical architecture of an email system.

They want to understand the problem, how it gets solved, and what changes for their business.

An infographic can communicate that quickly.

How I would structure one

Start with the outcome.

Then map out the components that produce it.

For example, if you're explaining how an X personal brand generates leads, the system might look like:

ICP + Positioning → Content Strategy → Distribution → Lead Capture → Qualified Calls

Each stage gets a short explanation showing what happens there.

You can expand on the process in the caption.

Now somebody scrolling through X can understand the basic mechanism before they even start reading the post.

And if they want more context, the caption is there.

The same framework can then be expanded into a longer article or video.

One strong idea becomes multiple useful assets.

Your content strategy needs to account for that.

This is one of the reasons we use opinion posts and snipes across our accounts.

A “snipe” is where we take an existing conversation and add a founder's perspective to it.

That could be:

→ A relevant industry announcement

→ A strong opinion from another founder

→ A new development affecting the market

→ A common misconception within the niche

→ A conversation the founder has actual experience contributing to

The advantage is that the conversation already exists.

You're joining it with something useful.

But I don't want our founders quote-posting random viral content just because it's getting attention.

We've tested what happens when accounts chase broad engagement.

I've done that on my own account.

You can end up with thousands of new followers who have absolutely no interest in what your business does.

The better approach is finding conversations that overlap with your expertise and your ICP.

What to do:

Identify five accounts your ideal buyers already pay attention to.

Study the topics they're consistently discussing.

Find conversations where you have a genuine opinion, relevant experience, or a result worth sharing.

Then join those conversations.

The goal is to become a recognizable name inside the communities you actually want to own.
