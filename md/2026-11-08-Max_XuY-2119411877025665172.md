# Your AI Copy Sounds Like Five Different Companies. Give It One Brand Voice.

## Post

100 Skills Worth Keeping · 001 · AIMax (@Max_XuY)

A homepage promises a revolution. The onboarding email sounds like a legal notice. A support reply reads like a motivational speech. Each piece might be grammatically fine, yet the business sounds different every time a customer meets it.

For an English-speaking SaaS founder, consultant, or small agency, this is a useful problem to address before producing more content. The skill I reviewed for this issue, brand-voice, turns a brand brief into a structured writing guide. Its useful output is a set of decisions a writer can apply across channels: vocabulary, tone boundaries, examples, and phrases to avoid.

What is inside the skill

The supplied skill has eight output sections: voice overview, tone dimensions, voice qualities, vocabulary, writing rules, channel adaptations, before-and-after examples, and things the brand never says. It first checks for an existing brand-context file, then asks for missing details such as audience, positioning, admired voices, and the tone the brand should avoid.

That sequence makes sense for a small business using AI across several customer touchpoints. A prompt like “make this more professional” leaves the assistant to interpret professionalism again on every request. A shared guide makes those decisions explicit. It can tell a writer to use plain verbs, explain the next step, avoid exaggerated promises, and keep humor out of a billing problem.

The skill is a Markdown instruction package. It does not connect to your CRM, inspect your customer database, or automatically enforce a voice across your team’s tools. You provide the context and use the resulting guide in subsequent writing tasks.

An English demonstration: one voice, three customer moments

I applied the instructions to a fictional product called Northline, a project-status tool for independent consultants. The demonstration brief specified US and UK English-speaking consultants, a calm and direct voice, no invented product capabilities, and three protected facts: a $19 monthly plan, a 14-day trial, and no credit card required for that trial. These are fictional inputs, not a real customer’s product or campaign results.

The voice decision was simple: Northline should sound like an organized colleague who explains what happens next. Use concrete actions such as “review,” “send,” and “track.” Avoid “revolutionary,” “effortless,” and claims that a tool will transform someone’s entire business. Keep the same character across channels while adapting the level of reassurance.

For a homepage, that produced: “Keep client updates in one place. Start a 14-day trial. No credit card required.” For an onboarding email: “Your trial is ready. Choose a project to review, then prepare your first client update. Your trial lasts 14 days.” For a pricing answer: “The plan costs $19 per month. The 14-day trial does not require a credit card.” The example onboarding action is proposed copy for the fictional brief, not verification of a live software interface.

A manual check found that all three protected facts appeared correctly in the combined copy. None of the three samples used the banned hype words. That is a small, inspectable result from one author-controlled demonstration. It supports the usefulness of a shared writing brief; it does not establish conversion lift, independent model reliability, or customer preference.

The most useful part is the boundary

“Friendly” means different things in a product announcement and a failed-payment email. The channel-adaptation section gives the guide somewhere to explain that difference. For Northline, a homepage can be concise and inviting, while a pricing answer should put the amount and terms first. A support response should acknowledge the issue without adding jokes or making an unsupported promise.

The before-and-after section makes those choices easier to apply. An off-brand line such as “Revolutionize your workflow with our game-changing platform” becomes “Keep client updates in one place.” The revision removes a broad promise and names the proposed job. It still needs a product fact check before use: a voice guide cannot establish that a capability exists.

For an agency, I would keep one guide per client and include a short approved-facts list beside it. For a founder, I would begin with the homepage, one onboarding message, and one support answer. If those sound consistent and preserve the facts, the guide has a concrete starting point for the next writing task.

TRACE review: 3.40 out of 5

I completed a 20-item AI rubric review using skill-trace-checker 2.0.0, from the pinned full-stack-skills/agent-skills source. The five dimension scores were Trust 3.25, Reliability 3.25, Adaptability 4.00, Convention 2.75, and Effectiveness 3.75. Their equally weighted average is 3.40/5. This is a content-quality assessment, not a runtime benchmark or official certification.

The strengths are concrete: the skill names its output sections, collects useful brand inputs, provides channel-specific adaptation, and asks for examples writers can compare. Its weaknesses are also visible: the package has no deep FAQ or reference layer, limited exception handling, and no explicit safeguard against inventing product claims. The AIMax companion notes add a fact-preservation checklist for practical use; those notes were kept outside the scored upstream entrypoint.

One scoring detail matters for interpreting the number. The original checker includes a China-localization criterion. An English-only skill loses points under that criterion, so this result should not be read as a neutral ranking of tools for US or European users. I retained the original scoring basis rather than inserting Chinese content to improve a number. The candidate does not pass the checker’s strict all-5.0 gate.

What I would use it for

I would use brand-voice to draft a working editorial guide for a founder, a consultant, or an agency client whose English copy lacks consistency. I would pair it with approved product facts and a human review of the examples. It gives a team a reusable starting point for writing decisions, while leaving positioning, factual approval, and final publication with the people responsible for them.
