# If I woke up bankrupt tomorrow, this is how I'd get to $10k/month building AI systems.

## Post

This is the playbook I'd follow if I had to rebuild my business from scratch.
I'd build it around the same service I sell today: implementing company brains.
The question is how I'd get that first client. What would I show them? What would they be paying for? And how would I deliver it?
I'd start by making one company's day-to-day work easier. Take a process they already run, build the company brain behind it, and teach the team how to use it.
I'll walk you through:
what to include in the service;
how to find businesses with a problem worth solving;
how to build a demo that makes the service easy to understand;
what to say in the first message and sales call; and
how to deliver the system and hand it over in five working days.
Throughout the article, I'll give you the pieces to put this into practice: a service you can adapt, messages you can send, questions to ask on the sales call, and a five-day plan for building and handing over the system.
Build your version as you read. Adapt the service, choose a prospect, and use the walkthrough to prepare something you can show them.
Let's start with what they're paying for.
1. Give someone a reason to pay you
Start with the last piece of work the owner had to fix.
Maybe a salesperson drafted a proposal with old pricing. The founder rewrote the scope, found a better case study, and explained which services they actually sell. Next week, someone needs the same help.
That gives you a specific job to improve.
Collect the current pricing, good proposals, service details, and the founder's corrections. Build a workflow that uses them when a new sales call comes in.
Now the salesperson has a starting point the company has already helped shape. The founder still reviews the proposal, but the system has instructions for the mistakes they kept correcting.
That's what I mean by a company brain. The knowledge and judgment stay with the business, ready for the next person and the next run.
 
My implementation work has been with agencies. You can apply the same approach to proposals, onboarding, campaign planning, or another recurring job you understand.
For the first client, I'd sell this:
I'll build a system that turns your sales-call notes into a proposal draft using your services, pricing, and best examples.

We'll work on one workflow over five working days. I'll build it around your company material, test it on three past proposals, and show your team how to run and update it.

It's $5,000. Half to book the project, and the rest once we've tested it together and your team can use it. I'll include a week of fixes for anything we missed in that workflow.
Two of those projects a month is $10k in revenue, before expenses.
Keep the first project small enough to finish. One workflow, one reviewer, and five to ten useful sources. Start the five days once you have the material and someone available to give feedback.
If they want CRM integrations, three departments, or custom software, price that separately. Make software and model costs clear before they book.
2. Find people who already pay for the problem
Pick a type of business whose work you can recognize. You'll have a much easier conversation if you understand what a good proposal, report, or campaign brief looks like.
Make a list of 20 businesses. For each one, write down:
the recurring job you could help with;
the person responsible for it;
something specific you noticed; and
how you could reach them.
Start with former clients, founders you know, and people who've already complained about their team's workload. Then look at company sites, case studies, and hiring posts.
A business hiring account managers might have an onboarding problem. A team publishing lots of case studies might write a lot of proposals. Those are reasons to ask, rather than proof they need your service.
Ask to see how the last one got made:
Can you walk me through your last proposal? Where did you get the information, and what did you have to change before sending it?
Follow the work. Ask which documents they opened, who they messaged, and where the draft got stuck.
Then ask how often they do it and roughly how long preparation and review take. Use their answers to decide whether a $5k project makes sense.
If they write one proposal every six months, I'd keep looking. If the founder reviews several every week and keeps making the same corrections, I'd explore that further.
You want a buyer who can show you the problem, judge the result, and make the decision.
3. Build something you can show
Your first client shouldn't be the first time you try to build this.
We built HQ to keep company knowledge, instructions, and reusable workflows together across sessions and teammates. You can use it with Claude Code, Codex, or Cursor, then hand over a workspace the client can keep improving.
 
Start with one of your own recurring jobs. Add the relevant information and a good example, run it, and save the useful corrections. Then try a fresh session. Can it do the job without you explaining everything again?
Once yours works, make a small demo for one prospect. For a proposal, use their public services page and a case study with a fictional customer request. Label assumptions and leave unpublished pricing blank.
Record two minutes showing the request, the company material it used, and the draft it produced. Explain what you'd need from the team to make it ready for real work.
Give the owner something they can react to. Their feedback will tell you what the paid version needs.
4. Show them the work, then ask about theirs
I'd send the first recording to someone I already know.
For example, if they've mentioned spending too much time on proposals:
Hey [name], you mentioned proposals were eating up your week. I put together a small example using your services page and a made-up customer brief. Want me to send the two-minute video?
For someone you don't know, use something you've actually seen:
Hey [name], I used your [specific case study] to put together a sample proposal workflow for [company]. It takes a customer brief and drafts the proposal using your company material. Want a quick look?
Only say you built it once you have. Keep the first message short enough that replying feels easy.
If they want to talk, open the draft and ask: “What would you change before you'd send this?”
Write down their corrections. Ask them to show you a good version and explain the difference. This is where you learn what a public-information demo couldn't tell you.
Then ask for three old calls or briefs and the finished proposals they became. You'll use those to check whether your build does the job.
Before quoting, settle four things:
What will the team give the system?
What should it produce?
Who will review it during the project?
What will you both check before calling it finished?
Keep the close just as concrete:
I can build this around your actual pricing, examples, and proposal process. We'll test it on those three past jobs, then have someone on your team run a new one with me watching.

The project is $5k. It's $2,500 to get started and $2,500 when we've checked it together. I can start [date], provided we have the documents and your reviewer ready.
Send a short scope that repeats what you agreed. Include what's outside the project, the review dates, and the payment schedule.
If they hesitate, find out what they're unsure about. The price, the result, and the team's ability to use it are different problems. Ask before changing the offer.
5. Give each day something concrete to finish
Build in the client's own HQ workspace and keep their private information separate from your demos and other clients.
This is the five-day plan I'd use for a proposal workflow. Each day ends with something you can show the client.
 
Day 1: Watch someone do the job.
Have the reviewer walk through a real proposal. Note what they open, what they look up, and what they know without checking anything.
Write a one-page description of the workflow: call notes go in; a proposal draft comes out; pricing comes from this document; this person reviews it. Get the three past examples you'll test with.
Finish with: a clear job description and the test material.
Day 2: Give the system the right information.
Add the services, current pricing, customer notes, and good proposals. Ask the client which document wins when two disagree. Label old examples so their useful structure doesn't override current facts.
Ask the agent a few questions you know the answer to. Which package fits this request? Where did that price come from? If it can't find the answer, fix the source or the instructions for finding it.
Finish with: a small set of sources the client has checked.
Day 3: Teach it what good looks like.
Generate a draft and review it with the person who normally checks proposals. Ask them to explain their edits as they make them.
Turn useful feedback into specific rules. For example: use the client's language for the problem; include one relevant case study; use approved pricing; ask about missing requirements before estimating the scope.
Save those rules in HQ and have the reviewer check them. Keep rules for this workflow close to the workflow itself.
Finish with: a reviewed example and the instructions that make it worth copying.
Day 4: Make it repeatable.
Save the process as an HQ skill or worker. Run the three past jobs through it and compare the drafts with the approved proposals.
Keep a simple list of misses: wrong fact, missing section, unsupported claim, bad wording. Fix what caused each one, then rerun that example.
Also try a brief with no budget or conflicting information. It should ask or flag the gap, rather than fill it with a confident guess.
Finish with: a workflow that passes the checks you agreed with the client.
Day 5: Let the team drive.
Ask someone on the team to open a fresh session and run a new example. Let them try before you explain. Wherever they get stuck, improve the instructions.
Show them how to change a price, add a better example, and save a correction. Keep a person reviewing drafts before anything goes to a customer.
Record that walkthrough and leave a short handoff: where the sources live, how to run the workflow, who updates it, and how to get help during the included week of fixes.
Finish with: a teammate who can run it, a named owner, and a checked handoff. Then collect the remaining $2,500.
 
6. Use the first project to earn the second
Check back after the team has used it on real work. Ask what they still fix, whether anyone stopped using it, and why.
Compare preparation and review time with what they told you before the build. Keep the actual numbers. If the result is useful, ask permission to share a before-and-after and request an introduction.
Save your discovery questions, build checklist, and handoff template. Keep the client's documents and private corrections in their workspace.
Now you have a better starting point for another $5k project. You can show the kind of work you deliver and explain what the client needs to contribute.
 
Keep reaching out while you deliver. Two projects a month requires a continuing pipeline; one good build won't create that on its own.
I'd keep a simple list of who I've contacted, who asked for the demo, who has a real project, and the next follow-up date. That's enough to see where the next client might come from.
What I'd do first
Tonight, I'd set up HQ and build one workflow for myself. I'd run it again in a fresh session before calling it a demo.
Then I'd list 20 businesses, pick the one whose work I understand best, and make a short example for them. I'd send it to the person responsible for that work and ask what they'd change.
The first goal is a useful conversation. From there: a clear $5k project, a working handoff, and a result you can show the next buyer with permission.
If you want to follow this blueprint, try HQ. Build your own company brain, get one workflow working, and use that experience to build the first version for a client.
