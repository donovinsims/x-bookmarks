# How to Run Multichannel Outreach Like the Top 1%

## Post

Most outreach runs on a single channel and hopes for the best. The account on the other end usually has more than one way in, an inbox and an active LinkedIn profile at minimum, sometimes a phone number you can dial to as well, and most teams only ever try the first.
This article walks through picking channels and building a multi-channel sequence, then wiring the whole thing into Claude Code so it keeps running without you.
 
1. Give each channel the job it's good at
Four channels, four different jobs, and mixing them up is the single most common way multichannel outreach turns into noise instead of signal.
Email is best for volume. It scales past every other channel here, but it is crowded and deliverability-sensitive, so it earns the widest reach and the lightest personalization budget per send.
LinkedIn is best for warm touches. Messages get delivered almost every time and get read on mobile, but the platform caps how many actions you can run per day, so it earns the accounts where building an actual relationship is the goal.
Phone is best for tier 1. It is hard to scale and most people will not pick up, but when you connect, the hit rate is higher than anything else on this list. It earns your highest-value accounts only.
In person and gifts are best for whales. Completely unscalable, and the highest signal of them all. It earns the handful of accounts where the deal size justifies the cost.
 
One rule falls out of this: never run all four channels against every account. Run the channel that matches the account's tier. A tier 3 account gets email. A tier 1 account gets all three, email, LinkedIn, then a phone call, in that order.
 
2. Wire the channels into one branching sequence
A real multichannel sequence runs as a single flow with one branch point, where what happens next depends on what the prospect did, rather than as four separate campaigns firing at the same time.
The sequence:
Email goes out first.
Visit their LinkedIn profile. This alone is a soft touch that shows up in their notifications.
Send a connection invitation.
Check after 1 day: did they accept? Yes, send a chat message on LinkedIn, they already said yes to hearing from you. No, send a follow-up email instead, LinkedIn didn't open the door this time, so go back to the channel that always delivers.
 
The branch point turns acceptance into a signal. 
Someone who accepted your invite within a day is telling you they're open to hearing more, so you use the warmer channel. Someone who didn't accept is telling you nothing yet, so you keep working the channel that doesn't depend on them saying yes to anything first.

Before any of this runs, you need the list itself: verified emails, phone numbers, and the firmographic and signal data that feeds the tiering step in section 9.

4. Layer signals on top of fit
A list built purely on firmographic fit tells you who to reach, and stops there. Signals answer a second question: when. That timing is usually what decides whether your message gets read at all.
Five signals worth building into your targeting:
New hire. The first 90 days is when new leaders rebuild the stack. They are actively looking for tools and vendors right now.
Job change. New company, new problems, fresh budget. Whatever they used at their last company, they have to decide again.
They're hiring. A job posting tells you the pain and the mission in the requirements section. Reach the hiring manager directly.
Churn risk. A new exec shows up at a customer account. Loop in customer success before that account re-evaluates the relationship.
Profile visit. They looked at you first. This is the warmest door in the building, because they already showed intent before you said a word.
 
The search_companies and find_people endpoints from section 3 both accept signal filters, so this step chains directly onto the list build rather than requiring a separate tool.
 
5. Relevancy beats personalization
People conflate the two. Most "personalized" outreach gets ignored because of it.
"I saw you have a dog" is personalization. It shows you looked at a profile. It says nothing about why you're reaching out now.
"I saw your post on X" is relevancy. It connects directly to something they did that relates to the reason you're messaging them.
There's a strength ladder here, strongest to weakest:
Self-authored (something they personally wrote or said) beats company (something their company did, like a funding round) beats role or industry (a generic fact true of anyone in that seat).
 
Four rules for the message itself:
Skip the intro. They will check your profile anyway, so a paragraph introducing yourself is wasted space.
Create context fast. Lead with the signal instead of a warm-up sentence.
One benefit, tied to their KPI. The single thing that matters to the number they're measured on.
One proof point, one simple CTA. Pick one of each, nothing more.

9. Match effort to tier, then check deliverability before you send
This is the effort tier from the intro, a separate axis from ICP tier. The question here is how much manual work an already-qualified account earns.
Tier 1, manual. Hand-written, multi-touch, phone included. Reserved for the accounts worth a human's full attention.
Tier 2, multichannel. Semi-automated. Email plus a LinkedIn sequence, running the branch from section 2.
Tier 3, automated. One-to-many, email-led, light touches. The volume tier.
 
Before any of it sends, two safety checks:
LinkedIn: keep activity around 20 per day, on a consistent IP, and treat the account as irreplaceable, because a ban here costs you your entire warm network.
Email: keep each mailbox under roughly 30 sends per day, rotate inboxes, set SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, and use verified addresses only.
These are operating rules of thumb. Check your own account's actual thresholds before you scale volume against them.
Before you hit send, six checks:
First line is about them
Opener references something real about them, specific enough that it couldn't apply to anyone else
One benefit tied to their KPI
One CTA, one question, no parentheses stuffed with extra asks
Sentence case, no em dashes, no banned words
Every stat in the message is real and verified
None of this needs new tools. It needs the channels you already run, email and LinkedIn and phone, routed by a decision instead of a coin flip, plus Claude Code holding onto what worked so the next list starts smarter than the last.
