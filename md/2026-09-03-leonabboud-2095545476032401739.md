# The 6 human biases you must understand to master marketing

## Post

Marketing is the study of human decisions.
If you sell to humans, you are selling to minds that use shortcuts.
People do not weigh every claim, price, and risk from zero. They judge through reference points, familiar patterns, other people's behavior, and the possibility of loss.
Learn those patterns and you’ll become a marketing machine.
Ignore them and you’ll lose to people who respect these laws.
The same offer can feel obvious to one buyer and confusing to another. Safe to one buyer, risky to the next. A bargain at noon, too expensive by dinner.
Change the first number a buyer sees and you can change what the price feels like.
Describe the same outcome as a gain or a loss and you can change which option feels safer.
This is the hidden psychology underneath marketing that the best marketers have learned to master.
#1 Anchoring: the first believable reference point owns the comparison
 
The Experiment
The famous magazine example came from a real Economist subscription page that Dan Ariely recreated with MIT students.
The students saw three choices:
Web only for $59.
Print only for $125.
Print plus web for $125.
The middle option looked useless. Nobody chose it.
But it made the bundle feel like an obvious bargain.
With all three options visible, 16% chose web only and 84% chose print plus web. When Ariely removed the print-only option, the preference flipped. Now 68% chose web only and 32% chose the bundle.
 
 
People rarely judge price, effort, speed, or value in a vacuum.
They compare.
The first believable number, standard, or alternative becomes a reference point. Later judgments stay pulled toward it.
Marketing creates anchors constantly.
The first plan on a pricing page anchors the rest.
Picture a piano showroom.
The salesperson walks you past a $300,000 flagship concert grand first. 
Only after that, they show you the $15,000 smaller piano that fits your home and budget.
The second piano has not changed. What feels expensive has.
 
How to action this: These principles become useful when they work together.
Start with desire.
What change does the customer want in health, wealth, relationships, status, security, freedom, or belonging?
Then run the message through the grunt test.
Can a stranger understand what they get, how life improves, what pain disappears, and why the claim is believable?
Choose the comparison.
What is the buyer using as an anchor right now? A freelancer? An internal hire? Doing nothing? Another category entirely?
Choose the frame.
Does the offer mean more deliverables, more leverage, less risk, more control, or a shorter path to the desired state?
Show the cost of standing still.
What does the customer keep losing in money, time, opportunity, status, or peace of mind if nothing changes?
Build recognition before asking for the sale.
Repeat one clear position through useful content until the market can explain what you believe.
Add proof from the closest credible peer.
Show the starting problem, the mechanism, and the verified result.
Give the decision an honest time boundary when a real one exists.
Only then choose the vehicle.
Meta ads, YouTube, email, LinkedIn, webinars, outbound, and landing pages carry the message.
They do not create the psychology underneath it.
Before you publish another campaign, put the offer on one page and answer:
What does the customer want?
What can they understand in three seconds?
What are they comparing us with?
Which truthful frame makes the value visible?
What are they afraid of losing?
Where will familiarity come from?
Whose behavior will make this choice feel safer?
What real constraint makes a decision necessary now?
Then write the copy.
Platforms will keep changing. The buyer is still human.
Once you understand how people decide, the next job is building marketing that gets stronger instead of resetting with every campaign.

1. Show the premium tier first
Imagine a security platform with two real plans.
The buyer sees Enterprise first at $2,400 a month. It includes custom policies, audit support, and a dedicated success manager.
Then they see Growth at $900 a month. It keeps automated monitoring and standard support but removes the high-touch services.
Because the buyer saw the full version first, the $900 plan is evaluated as a narrower version of a $2,400 capability. If Growth appeared first, $900 would arrive without that context.
2. Show monthly pricing before the annual equivalent
Imagine a workflow tool that costs $120 per month.
The pricing card shows that number first. Directly beneath it, the buyer sees $96 per month when billed annually.
The first comparison is $120 versus $96, so the annual rate reads as a $24 monthly saving. Showing the full annual charge keeps the cash commitment visible.
Do not hide "billed annually" in small type. An anchor should clarify the deal, not conceal when the customer pays.
Use allowances to define scale
Imagine an analytics product with a Business plan that supports 50 seats and 1 million tracked events each month.
The buyer sees that allowance before the Team plan with 15 seats and 250,000 events.
The larger allowance establishes what this product can handle. The smaller plan now reads as the right-sized version for a smaller team, rather than an arbitrary cap.
Only use limits tied to real product capacity or customer needs. If almost nobody needs 1 million events, presenting that volume as normal would distort the comparison.
4. Price the status quo before the software
Imagine a sales demo for an operations platform.
Before showing the subscription, the seller builds the current-cost worksheet with the buyer. The illustrative inputs are two full-time hires at $8,000 a month each, plus $2,000 in tools. That produces an $18,000 monthly reference point.
Then the buyer sees the hypothetical software price of $3,500 a month.
The buyer is no longer comparing $3,500 with zero. They are comparing it with the work and tools already required to run the process.
#2 Framing: how you position your point changes its meaning
 
The experiment
Researchers Tversky and Kahneman asked people to choose between 2 options for a disease expected to kill 600 people.
One group saw the options as lives saved. The Save option saved 200 people. The gamble had a one-third chance of saving all 600 and a two-thirds chance of saving nobody. In that frame, 72% chose the Save option.
The second group had the same statistics, but the framing was inverted.
The second group saw the same outcomes as lives lost. The Save option meant 400 people would die. The Gamble had a one-third chance that nobody would die and a two-thirds chance that all 600 would die. In that frame, 78% chose the Gamble.
Framing changes how a choice is understood by changing which part of the choice becomes most visible.
 
How Avis and Salesforce used framing
Avis was the second-largest rental-car company in America when it adopted "We Try Harder" in 1962.
Most companies would hide second place.
Avis made it the reason to choose them.
The campaign framed being number two as evidence that employees had to work harder for every customer. Their position as #2 on the market did not change. What the rank meant changed. The campaign was so powerful that Avis kept the slogan for 50 years.
Salesforce used the same move against installed enterprise software.
At its 2000 launch, Salesforce themed the event "The End of Software." Soon after, it hired actors to stage a mock protest outside a Siebel Systems conference carrying anti-software signs.
Salesforce was still selling software. But it framed the old category as something customers should escape and its cloud product as the end of that burden.
Avis turned a weakness into effort.
Salesforce turned a product category into the villain.
That is framing in practice. The company does not change the facts. It changes which fact organizes the buyer's interpretation.
The right frame depends on the customer's current reality.
If the product requires work, say so.
If the result takes time, say so.
A strong frame helps the customer see why the product matters.
It does not blind them to what the product costs.
#3 Loss aversion: buyers protect before they progress
 
The experiment
Researchers Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Richard Thaler randomly gave Cornell students ceramic mugs, then opened a market where owners could sell and non-owners could buy.
The object was identical for everyone.
The reference point was not.
In all four mug markets, the median seller wanted $5.25. In three of the four rounds, the median buyer offered $2.25. Far fewer mugs traded than standard theory predicted.
Giving up a mug you owned felt different from acquiring the same mug.
 
Ilan Ganzach and Nili Karsahi later tested loss-framed marketing in the field. They contacted 246 inactive credit-card customers with the same card benefits framed either as gains from using the card or losses from not using it.
The loss-framed message produced more card use over the following two months. Among check users in month two, 54.8% used the card after the loss frame versus 16.4% after the gain frame. The authors warned that context and other mechanisms could have contributed, so this is not a universal rule.
Loss aversion describes the tendency for losses to carry more psychological weight than comparable gains around a reference point.
The internet often turns this into a fake law that says a loss is always exactly twice as powerful as a gain.
Do not repeat that.
The size of the effect depends on the decision and context. The useful point is simpler.
People care deeply about protecting what they believe they already have.
How Duolingo uses loss aversion
Duolingo turns practice into something the user owns: a streak measured in consecutive days.
As the number grows, skipping one lesson no longer feels like missing five minutes of practice. It feels like losing a 30, 300, or 1,000 day streak.
Duolingo says this directly in its own product research. Early streak growth feels like achievement. Longer streaks begin to motivate through loss aversion because users want to protect what they built.
The company also learned that too much fear can break the habit.
Its Streak Freeze lets a learner miss a day without losing the streak. Duolingo later allowed users to equip two freezes. The company reported that this change increased its relative number of daily active learners by 0.38%.
That is the smart version of loss aversion.
Duolingo makes progress visible, gives it emotional value, then provides a way to protect it from an occasional missed day. But it also adds enough flexibility that one missed day does not make the user quit.
The principle appears in the product, not just the copy.
Snapchat uses the same mechanism socially. A Snapstreak begins when two friends exchange photo or video Snaps every day. The fire emoji and number turn the ongoing exchange into visible property. When the streak is close to expiring, Snapchat displays an hourglass and tells both people to send a Snap quickly.
Starbucks applies the mechanism to accumulated rewards. Under its US program, Green Level Stars expire six months after the month they were earned unless the member stays active. A qualifying purchase, Reward redemption, or eligible $30 digital reload extends the Stars for another month. Starbucks also shows members their balance and expiration dates.
Duolingo protects learning progress. Snapchat protects a shared ritual. Starbucks protects accumulated rewards. In each case, continued use is connected to preserving something the customer already earned.
Loss aversion can shape the product as well as the copy. Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow program asked employees to commit part of future raises to retirement savings.
The design avoided an immediate reduction in take-home pay. In its first implementation, 78% of those offered the plan joined, 80% of enrollees stayed through the fourth raise, and average saving rates rose from 3.5% to 13.6% over 40 months. The result also involved inertia and self-control, so it should not be credited to loss aversion alone.
For marketers, the application is concrete. Instead of leading with "Gain" lead with "Loss". 
Show the revenue already leaking, the hours already being spent, the customers churning.
Then show a credible route out. A loss without a mechanism creates fear. A loss paired with a believable plan creates a decision.
Use loss aversion ethically by exposing a real cost the buyer has underestimated.
Do not invent disaster.
Do not imply guaranteed failure.
Do not make a minor inconvenience sound existential.
The goal is not to make the customer afraid.
The goal is to help them price in the cost of doing nothing.
#4 Mere exposure: recognition gets you considered
 
The experiment
Researchers Fang, Singh, and Ahluwalia asked participants to read an online article while an unfamiliar camera advertisement appeared beside it zero, five, or 20 times.
The readers were focused on the article, not the banner.
Yet average liking for the advertised camera rose from 3.91 with no exposure to 4.73 after five exposures and 5.11 after 20 exposures.
 
People liked the ad more without being able to report seeing it.
The experiment measured evaluation and processing ease. It did not prove trust or sales.
How GEICO and Salesforce manufactured familiarity
GEICO did not keep reinventing its identity every quarter.
After increasing its advertising budget in the 1990s, the company expanded national advertising across television and direct mail. The GEICO Gecko first appeared during the 2000 television season and became the recurring character people could recognize before they processed the insurance offer. More than 25 years later, GEICO still releases new Gecko advertisements.
The message had multiple creative vehicles, but the brand cues stayed familiar: the gecko, the GEICO name, the direct-response insurance category, and the repeated promise of an easier quote.
Salesforce did something similar with an idea instead of a mascot.
"The End of Software" appeared in its launch theme, protest signs outside a Siebel conference, and its early market positioning.
A prospect could encounter the company through an event, press coverage, a protest image, or a sales conversation and still meet the same argument.
That is mere exposure used as a company system.
GEICO repeated a character.
Salesforce repeated an enemy.
Coca-Cola repeated a shape. In 1915, it asked glassmakers for a bottle distinctive enough to recognize by touch in the dark or even as broken glass.
Production began in 1916. Coca-Cola then repeated that contour in national advertising, and most bottlers had adopted it by 1920.
The bottle became both packaging and a recurring visual asset. People met the same shape in the product, the store, and the advertising around it.
These examples do not prove that repetition alone caused a sale. The companies made the same distinctive cues easier to recognize across many encounters.
Media platforms already treat frequency as a design variable. Google Ads offers target-frequency buying for some YouTube awareness campaigns and frequency caps for Display and Video campaigns.
The useful lesson is not "show the ad forever." It is to choose enough repetition for recognition, then cap exposure before familiarity turns into fatigue.
The same principle applies to a founder. One podcast appearance introduces the idea. A short clip makes it recognizable. A case study gives it proof. A sales email applies it to a buying problem. A landing page connects it to the offer. The market hears one thesis through different evidence, not the same sentence copied everywhere.
You are not repeating the same sentence until people surrender.
You are giving the market several chances to understand the same position.
Use mere exposure ethically by making each exposure useful.
Teach a new example.
Answer a new objection.
Show another part of the mechanism.
Repeat the worldview without duplicating the post.
And remember the limit.
Familiarity can open the door. Proof and delivery still have to walk through it.
#5 Social proof: the right crowd lends certainty
 
The experiment
Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini, and Vladas Griskevicius changed one small card in hotel bathrooms.
The standard card asked guests to help the environment. It produced a 35.1% towel-reuse rate.
A second card said that most guests reused their towels. Reuse rose to 44.1%.
A third card said that guests who had stayed in that exact room reused their towels. Reuse reached 49.3% in the original study.
 
Social proof is one of Robert Cialdini's principles of influence.
People use other people's behavior as information, especially when they are unsure what to do.
Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds, and Duncan Watts demonstrated a more volatile side of social proof with an artificial music market involving 14,341 participants. When listeners could see what other people had downloaded, success became more unequal and less predictable across parallel versions of the market. Visible popularity influenced later popularity, although quality still mattered at the extremes.
That makes social proof different from mere exposure.
Mere exposure says, "I recognize this."
Social proof says, "People like me chose this."
The second statement reduces uncertainty.
Most companies waste this principle with logo wallpaper.
"Trusted by leading brands."
"Thousands of happy customers."
"The number one solution for modern teams."
These claims are broad enough to ignore.
How Booking dot com and Airbnb engineer social proof
Booking dot com restricts who can review a property. Its policy says only customers who booked through the platform can submit a review. Someone who canceled before check-in does not receive a review questionnaire.
The proof is tied to a platform-recorded booking rather than an open testimonial form.
Airbnb designs the timing of the proof as carefully as the review itself.
Only people connected to an eligible reservation can review the stay. Airbnb keeps the reviews hidden until both sides submit or the review period closes.
That reduces the pressure to copy or retaliate against the other person's review.
Booking dot com verifies the booking.
Airbnb verifies the stay and separates the submissions.
Both companies make the origin of the proof inspectable instead of relying on a wall of anonymous praise.
Useful social proof has four parts:
A recognizable type of customer.
A problem the buyer also has.
A mechanism the buyer can understand.
A verified result or change.
Instead of a wall of unrelated logos, show a case study from a founder at the same stage, with the same constraint, using the same part of the offer the prospect is evaluating.
If your buyer runs a bootstrapped SaaS company, proof from a global consumer brand may be impressive and still feel irrelevant.
Similarity beats fame when the buyer is asking, "Will this work for someone like me?"
This is how social proof reduces perceived distance.
The customer sees the desired outcome.
They see a person like them reach it.
They see the path used to get there.
The change feels less theoretical.
Social proof can also backfire when you advertise the wrong norm.
"Most founders neglect content" may sound urgent, but it also tells the reader that neglect is normal.
A better norm points toward the behavior you want:
"The founders who arrive warm in sales calls teach the market before the call happens."
That is a strategic claim, so support it with real customer evidence before using it as proof.
Use social proof ethically by telling the truth with enough detail to be checked.
Use real customers.
Verify the result.
Ask permission for the logo and quote.
Keep the conditions visible.
Never manufacture reviews, inflate counts, or borrow status from customers who did not use the offer being sold.
Proof works because it reduces uncertainty.
Fake proof transfers that uncertainty into distrust.
6. Scarcity: a real closing door makes waiting expensive
 
The experiment
Researchers Stephen Worchel, Jerry Lee, and Akanbi Adewole asked people to rate identical chocolate-chip cookies.
Some people saw a jar with ten cookies.
Others saw the same cookie in a jar with only two.
The cookies from the scarce jar were rated as more desirable. The strongest response came when a jar that began with ten cookies was suddenly reduced to two. Scarcity caused by apparent demand also produced higher ratings than scarcity caused by an accident.
 
The cookie did not improve, its availability changed.
Scarcity is another Cialdini principle of influence.
When access becomes limited, the option can feel more desirable and delay becomes more costly.
Rajesh Aggarwal, Mahesh Jun, and Jong Ho Huh later compared limited-quantity and limited-time messages. Limited quantity created stronger purchase intentions in their studies, especially for symbolic brands. Perceived competition between buyers helped explain the effect.
Marketers understand the tactic and routinely destroy it.
"Only three spots left."
A countdown timer that resets when the page reloads.
An offer that "closes forever" every Friday.
This may steal a few decisions today. It trains the market to distrust every deadline tomorrow.
How Supreme, Nike, and Kickstarter use real scarcity
Supreme does not release an entire season at once.
It built its retail rhythm around Thursday drops and limited-edition releases. Each drop turns a bounded assortment into a weekly event.
Nike makes the constraint visible through SNKRS Draw. For selected limited-edition shoes, the product page displays a countdown, each member can submit one entry during the limited window, and only selected entrants can buy the shoe.
Kickstarter uses time scarcity instead of product scarcity. Creators choose the goal and deadline before launch. If the deadline passes without the goal being met, backers are not charged and the creator receives no funds.
These constraints are different, but each one can be inspected.
Supreme has a limited weekly release.
Nike has limited supply, a countdown, and one entry per member.
Kickstarter has a fixed campaign clock with an all-or-nothing consequence.
The scarcity exists in the operating model before it appears in the copy.
That is the standard marketers should use. If the limit disappears when the page refreshes, it is not scarcity. It is theater.
Scarcity connects desire to timing.
The buyer may want the outcome. They may understand the mechanism. They may believe the proof. Yet without a reason to decide, the purchase remains a problem for later.
A real deadline converts "someday" into a choice.
Use scarcity ethically by pointing to limits that would survive a skeptical customer's audit.
Do not create fake supply limits for digital products.
Do not hide recurring enrollment behind a permanent last-chance claim.
Do not pressure a buyer who lacks the information needed to decide.
Urgency should make the decision clear.
It should not make clear thinking impossible.
