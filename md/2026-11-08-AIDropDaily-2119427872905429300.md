# 23% of Companies Never Answered a Lead. Copy This Free After-Hours AI Reply Kit

## Post

A business that tried to reach a new web lead within an hour was nearly 7x as likely to get a real conversation with a decision maker as one that waited even an hour longer. Wait 24 hours or more and it was more than 60x worse.

That's from a Harvard Business Review study of 1.25 million sales leads across 42 US companies. A separate audit in the same paper sent test inquiries to 2,241 US companies. 23% never answered at all.

So here's the useful part, free and ungated, right below: a 5-part After-Hours Reply Kit you can paste into any AI chat tool tonight. It sorts an inquiry, drafts a reply with two real times, checks it before you hit send, logs it, and follows up once.

No plugin, no paid course, no "comment KIT". Copy it.

THE AFTER-HOURS REPLY KIT (copy all 5 parts)

PART 1: Sort it (10 seconds)

Label every new inquiry as one of four:

HOT: specific job, date or budget mentioned. Reply first.

- FIT: clearly your kind of work, details missing.

- UNCLEAR: you can't tell what they want.

- NOT FOR US: outside what you do. Reply kindly anyway and point them somewhere useful.

PART 2: The reply prompt (paste into any AI chat)

"You are drafting a reply for my business, [business name], which does [what you do] in [area]. Here is a new inquiry: [paste inquiry]. Label it HOT, FIT, UNCLEAR or NOT FOR US. Then draft a reply under 90 words in a warm, plain voice. Thank them by name. Restate what they asked for in one sentence. Offer exactly these two times: [time 1] and [time 2]. Ask at most one question. Do not invent prices, availability, policies or promises. If something is missing, put it in [brackets] for me to fill."

PART 3: Check before sending (all 6 must pass)

Their name and request are correct.

2. Both times are real openings on your calendar right now.

3. No price, discount or guarantee you didn't write yourself.

4. One question at most.

5. Reads like you, not like a brochure.

6. Nothing in [brackets] is left.

PART 4: Log it (one row per lead)

Date | Name | Contact | Label | What they want | Times offered | Sent at | Reply? | Next step

PART 5: One follow-up, then stop

If no answer in 24 hours: "Hi [name], just checking whether [time 1] or [time 2] works for [job]. If neither does, reply with a better time and I'll make it work." Then leave it. One nudge is service. Five is spam.

What it looks like on a real-shaped inquiry (illustrative, not a real customer):

Inquiry at 9:40 PM on a Monday: "Hi, do you cater office lunches? About 30 people, sometime next week, mostly vegetarian."

Label: HOT (headcount, timing, dietary need).

Draft reply: "Hi Priya, thanks for reaching out! A vegetarian-leaning lunch for about 30 next week is right in our wheelhouse. I can walk you through menus and pricing on a quick call Tuesday at 10:00 AM or Wednesday at 2:30 PM. Which works better? And is there a budget per person we should plan around?"

Check: name right, two real times, no invented price, one question, reads human. Send. Log it.

Does a faster reply actually matter? Here's what's verified and what's claimed:

VERIFIED (study): HBR, March 2011, Oldroyd, McElheran and Elkington: ~7x likelier to qualify a lead within an hour vs. an hour later; 60x+ vs. 24 hours or more. Qualify here means a meaningful conversation with a decision maker, not a sale.

- VERIFIED (audit, same paper): of 2,241 US companies, 37% answered a test lead within an hour, 23% never answered, and the average among those that did reply within 30 days was 42 hours.

- COMPANY-REPORTED: On its launch page, Anthropic says Bambi Baby, a family-owned stroller and car-seat retailer, tied $60,000 in sales to a Claude-built lead-capture tool and its follow-up in the tool's first four days. That's one company's result, reported by the vendor. Results vary.

- COMPANY-REPORTED: Google says Brian Newham of Atlas Automotive Repair in Tulsa cut each customer repair report from about an hour to 15 minutes using Gemini. Different task, same idea: AI took the slow writingstep off the owner's desk.

Founder Andrew Jennings lists twelve roles he says he has replaced with AI this year. His first is an executive assistant that "chases what's due and drafts the replies," and he adds: "I still make every call." His results are his own claim. The approval step is the part worth copying.

Not all good, though.

That HBR number is 15 years old, and it measured online sales leads in general, not your bakery or your plumbing business. One co-author, David Elkington, was CEO of InsideSales.com, a company that sold lead-response software. It ran in HBR with two academic co-authors, so it isn't junk, but it isn't neutral either. And "7x likelier to qualify" never meant "7x more sales."

AI drafts also get things wrong. A model will happily invent a price, a slot you don't have or a policy you never wrote. That's why Part 3 exists, and it's why Anthropic's own workflow starts in approval mode, where nothing sends until you say yes. A fast reply that's wrong costs more than a slow one that's right.

Don't get me wrong. Despite these limits, this is one of the most practical things AI can do for a small business today. It doesn't need a new client or a new product. It just stops the people who already asked from slipping away while you sleep.

The big platforms are betting on this too. Zapier's launch-day post said the plugin had already been installed over 775,000 times. Anthropic's own page later said more than 900,000. Installs are not daily users, but the direction is clear.

The owners who win won't be the ones with the fanciest bot. They'll be the ones whose replies are fast and correct.
