# Your Grok Bot Is Burning Your Plan: The One-Bot Setup That Does 10x More (Full Course)

## Post

0. Your Grok Bot Is Burning Your Plan
One engineer spent an ordinary workday running his bots from his phone and got more done than he usually manages at his desk: Colab notebooks, fine-tuning experiments, a new page for his site. He has 29 bots, and one of them, a “chief of staff,” hands work out to the rest.
Most people get a different picture. The bot rereads the same pages every morning. A routine wakes the agent every 15 minutes so it can report “no changes.” The report arrives in a state you have to rewrite by hand. And the plan’s limit runs out on Wednesday.
The same token budget buys either 7 usable results or 70, on the same model and the same tier. What moves that number is how often the bot runs and how much it reads, and a pricier plan changes neither.
The “my phone is my workstation” setup runs on files and routines kept on the bot’s cloud computer, which keeps working while your laptop is shut. Without them, Grok Bot on mobile is just one more chat.

1. One Bot First, Then a Chief
The first instinct is to build a team: researcher, coder, copywriter, analyst. But every new bot is its own context, its own thread and its own token bill. And there’s a ceiling per account: 50 bots and group chats combined.
Before creating a bot, ask one question: why can’t the one I already have do this? Often the answer is “it can, once it gets a skill.”
Group chats are a separate trap: in them, every bot replies to every message. Keep them for decisions where you want competing opinions, not for daily work.
Once you’re at three bots, you need a chief, the only one you write to. It knows the roster, decides who takes a task, passes it on with the inputs and brings you back one summary. That’s exactly how the 29-bot setup runs: the chief pulls findings from the researcher and passes them to the coder, while the human only reviews and decides.
 
 
2. The Brain Lives in Files, Not in Chat
 
A bot’s memory holds your preferences and short recaps of past conversations. Handy, but it’s not a source: details get compressed, and a new bot can’t see it. A file on the cloud computer stays word for word, and every bot on the account can read it.
So facts go into two buckets. Slow ones (who these people are, how you work, what you promised) live in notes. Fast ones, like prices, statuses, dates and amounts, get rechecked by the bot every time at the place they come from.
The other half is the chat. A long conversation drags along dead ideas, raw output and old corrections, and the bot rereads all of it at every step. Saving a file inside that chat doesn’t clean it. What helps is a short handoff written to a file, and a fresh conversation that starts from it.

3. Rules the Bot Never Forgets
A rule that lives only in a human’s head is a rule the bot will break. On their livestreams, the Grok Bot team decided to push changes straight through with no pull requests, and their chief bot opened a PR anyway. Everyone knew about the decision except the bot.
So a rule goes where the bot looks before every action: into its description rather than its memory. That description works as the bot’s system prompt. Clone the bot, and the description comes along while the memory starts blank.
Second, the wording. A corrected bot loves to write down the story: “on Tuesday the banner in the payment form covered the button.” A rule like that never fires twice. What works is a general principle: “after any layout change, check that nothing clickable is hidden.” The test is simple: would someone who never saw the incident understand the rule?
Third, corrections have to pile up. Every fix becomes a line in the rules file, and once a week the bot shows you what was added and what it broke.
 
 
4. By Hand → Skill → Routine
 
A skill is the recipe for a task. A routine is the trigger that runs it without you. The order never changes: do it by hand → make it reliable → store it as a skill → only then put it on a timer. A routine on top of a raw process just automates the mistakes.
You can record a skill by demonstration, but that’s only a draft: the recording runs up to 10 minutes, captures no microphone, and shows nothing but the happy path. Decisions, failure handling and approval limits still have to be written in by hand. The best skills come from corrections: when the bot reasons wrong, build a skill instead of another prompt.
A routine keeps going on the bot’s cloud machine after you shut the lid, and that’s where a plan burns fastest. A routine every 15 minutes is 96 runs a day, roughly 2,900 a month. Once a day is 30. The system lets you schedule runs as often as every 5 minutes, with up to 50 routines per bot, so the limits won’t stop you; you have to stop yourself.
Pick in this order: an event instead of a schedule, if the source supports it. Then a schedule matched to the real decision deadline. And always a cheap change check before the full analysis. One more thing: “the check failed” never means “nothing changed.”
If you disappear for a long stretch, Grok Bot asks whether to keep your routines, and if you don’t answer, it pauses them.
 
 
5. Read Less, Repeat Nothing, Cap Retries
 
Here’s a model calculation that shows where a plan goes. The queue: 100 identical tasks. The budget: one million tokens.
The old process spends 100,000 tokens per task: it gets through 10, and 7 of those are usable without edits. The rebuilt one spends 12,500 per task: it gets through 80, and 70 are usable. That’s 8x more tasks processed × a 1.25x higher share of usable ones = 10x more useful work.

Three moves account for most of the gap. Read only the passages you need and keep the full original in the archive. Give every result a “shelf life”: when the fact was checked and when it has to be checked again. And cap repeats: two attempts, saved progress, resume from the point of failure rather than from zero.
 
 
6. “Done” Means “Checked”
 
One bot that can prove its result is worth more than five that guess. Before you hand it a task, ask: is there a signal a machine can read on its own to see success or failure? Code has one: tests, a build, an app that starts. A task without that signal needs one created for it.
On stream, the Grok Bot team built a card game: the three stats on every card were supposed to sum to 100. The rule sat in a prompt, no test checked it, and the broken cards were caught by a human.
For work without code, the signal is a checklist. The bot goes through every item with evidence, fixes only what failed, and if anything is still open, the result ships as “needs review,” not “done.” A classic mistake this catches: a price of “$24 per seat, billed yearly” turns into a monthly subscription in the report.
The second safeguard is an autonomy rung you set for every task: investigate → draft → autopilot → full autopilot. Full is only for projects you don’t mind losing. On a test project, the team’s autopilot merged more than 400 PRs. Then it touched a database with real users for the first time, and one bad SQL query put sign-ups on pause. The moment something is live, step down a rung.
A human stays on four things, however well everything runs: migrations, deploys, money and access. Grok Bot has Auto-review rules for this (Settings → General → Auto-review): each one is a single line with the mode “Ask first” or “Allow automatically,” and when two conflict, “Ask first” wins.

7. Your First Week
Don’t build it all in one evening. One step a day:
Day 1. One bot. The description from steps 1–3 and the “Ask first” rules.
Day 2. One task, by hand. Have the bot restate it in its own words before it starts; keep it on “investigate” or “draft.”
Day 3. A way to check itself: the checklist from step 6.
Day 4. Your first skill, from a run that worked, or from something you had to correct.
Day 5. A single routine: daily at most, quiet when there’s nothing new. The safest first one is a morning summary.
Day 6. One connector (Gmail or Calendar) in place of the browser, which is faster and cheaper on every task.
Day 7. Audit. A second bot comes only when the first one can report on its own whether it succeeded.
 
Don’t buy the next tier until you’ve counted how much of the current one goes to “no changes.”
