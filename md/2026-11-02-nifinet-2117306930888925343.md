# Steal Leads From Your Competitors' Viral Posts Using Grok Bots

## Post

Every week, a competitor or a creator in your niche posts a free template and asks people to comment 1 word to get it.
Some of the people who comment run the kind of company you sell to. By commenting, they told you in public what they're working on this week.
At Sortlist, the B2B marketplace I run, we built 3 Grok bots that skip the reply and read each person's bio. We tested them on a cold email giveaway with 298 replies.
171 of the first 172 people they read had typed the 1 word and nothing else. From that 1 post, the bots still came back with 6 people worth writing to, each with a first email built on a public fact about them.
 
The bots run in Grok Bot, the desktop app where you set up a few bots that message each other and work on a schedule. LinkedIn needs 1 more tool, because Grok can't read LinkedIn comments. That's section 3.
By the end of this, you'll know:
How to find the posts in your niche that pulled the most replies
How to pull everyone who replied, with their bio and website
How to cut that crowd down to the few who could buy
What the first email opens on, and why it never mentions the post
 
1. Start with the posts that already pulled hundreds of replies
The first bot, Finder, picks which posts to read. It follows a settings file, config.md, that you fill in during setup.
In it you set who you sell to and where to look, how far back to look (14 days by default) and how many replies a post needs (50 by default).
You also give each run a budget: how many posts to read, how many X searches to spend, and how many people can make the shortlist. A search is any lookup on X, including each page of 25 replies.
You can point Finder at posts 2 ways, and you can use both.
Give it a list. Your competitors' X accounts, their founders, the creators your buyers follow, or the exact posts you want read.
Let it search X. Finder looks for giveaway posts in your niche and opens each one to note the word people had to comment. Here's the gist of its search instructions (the full text is in the repo, which you can get at the end):
 
On our first run, Finder found 5 posts in 10 searches, with 63 to 298 replies each. Then it started reading the replies.
 
2. Our first run spent its whole budget on 1 post
Finder started with the biggest post, the 298-reply cold email giveaway. Its budget counted each reply as 1 read, so it ran out after 174 replies from 172 people (a few replied more than once). Finder never read the replies on the other 4 posts.
 
We switched the budget to searches, so 1 page of 25 replies costs 1 search. Finder now also keeps at least half its searches for reading replies.
Everything in sections 4 and 5 comes from those 172 people, the run we sorted.
Grok can read much more than that. In a separate test on the same post, it read 293 of the 298 replies in 13 searches.
Grok Bot's X allowance in that test was 30 searches a minute and 1,000 a day, and we never hit it. That allowance is separate from the budget you set per run.
Finder saves each person to a spreadsheet, people.csv, with their handle, name, bio, follower count, what they replied, and the website in their bio. X won't give you anyone's email, and it won't return replies to replies.
On LinkedIn, the same approach read nothing.
 
3. LinkedIn hides its comments from Grok
LinkedIn sends logged-out visitors to a sign-up page, so Grok couldn't read a single comment on my 31 March post, which had 903 comments.
For LinkedIn, we use our own product at Sortlist, yourmax.ai (we call it max). It watches LinkedIn for buying signals. 3 of those signals are about engagement: who reacts to or comments on a competitor's company-page posts, a creator's posts, or 1 post you pick.

This summer we ran max for Overloop AI, an outbound tool Sortlist owns, watching rival company pages. In 3 weeks it pulled 335 people from 32 posts, and 11 of those 335 were strong fits for Overloop.
Back on X, we had 172 people, and almost all of them came for the freebie.
 
4. 120 of the 172 bios were too thin to judge
The second bot, Sorter, gives every person 1 of 5 labels, based on their bio, headline, name and website:
 
Sorter drops vendors, creators and bots, with a 1-line reason for each. Then it ranks the buyers.
People who wrote more than the 1 word come first, because you can answer what they asked. On our run, that was 1 person. Next come people whose title fits and whose website opens.
Sorter never marks anyone down for a small follower count, and it keeps 1 person per company.
On the first run, Sorter labeled the 172 people: 29 possible buyers, 14 vendors, 8 creators, 1 bot, and 120 unclear.
It rated each buyer high, medium or low fit against who you sell to, then shortlisted 6: 2 high fit and 4 medium.
The shortlist cap in config.md was 10. Sorter's rules say never to pad the list, so it stopped at 6.
 
Sorter didn't guess on the 120. A guess there means an email to someone who only came for the template.
With 6 people, Drafter can read each company before it writes.
 
5. Open the email on something they published
The third bot, Drafter, reads up to 3 public pages about each person and their company. It picks 1 fact to open on: something on their website, a post they wrote, a hire, a launch, a talk they gave. Then it drafts 2 things:
 
1 of the 6 runs a small agency, and its website shows a client result with real numbers. Drafter opened the email on that result.
 
We caught a second mistake in the drafts. All 6 pasted our product sentence from config.md word for word, so every draft carried the same stock line.
Drafter now describes what you sell in its own words, in 1 clause that fits the person.
 
6. Never say how you found them
A first message never mentions the post, the reply, the giveaway, the creator or the competitor.
They replied to someone else to get a free template. If you open on that, you sound like you were watching them, so Drafter opens on something they published.
You approve every draft and send it yourself. Each bot's profile (its standing instructions) tells it to only read. On X that means no replies, likes, follows or DMs. In max, it may run only read commands.
You find the email address last, after you approve a draft, in Overloop AI or whatever email tool you use.
During setup, Sorter saves those profiles for you, so setup is mostly answering questions.
 
7. Setup takes 6 steps and 1 paste
The bots hand work to each other by name. Finder fills the spreadsheet, Sorter shortlists and messages Drafter, and Drafter writes and messages Sorter back. So keep the names Finder, Sorter and Drafter.
Create 3 empty bots in the Grok Bot app with those names.
Open Sorter's chat and paste the installer from the repo. The installer is a long prompt that tells Sorter how to set everything up.
Answer Sorter's questions 1 at a time: who you sell to, what you sell in 1 sentence, the language for each country, your competitors' X handles and their founders', the creators your buyers follow, 3 to 6 giveaway search terms, and the people you never contact. Start with 3 competitors and 3 creators.
Sorter then creates the shared folder and config.md, saves 3 skills (the step-by-step instructions the bots run), and sends Finder and Drafter their profiles.
When Sorter asks about LinkedIn, say no for now. It needs a yourmax.ai plan and its API key, and you can add it later.
Open Finder's and Drafter's chats and approve their profiles if they ask. Sorter then has Finder do a first run and tells you the counts. Type go, and Sorter shortlists, waits for Drafter, and walks you through each draft.

8. Run it by hand for a week before you automate it
For the first week, start each morning yourself: ask Finder in its chat to run its morning, then ask Sorter to sort and shortlist. Read each shortlist and send 5 of the drafts from your own inbox.
When a shortlist is wrong, the fix is usually in config.md: a job title you forgot, or a vendor who looks like a buyer.
