Anti-selling: How to Sell Without Sounding Like You’re Selling
“Selling” is a tainted word.
Hell, it’s a tainted concept.
When we think of “sales” we think of someone being pushy and trying to rizz us into giving them money. Which is, ironically, a display of poor sales skills. Think about, say, the last 5 purchases that you made. Did anybody push you to make them like that?
Does someone have to hard-sell you your morning coffee?
Or a new video game that came out?
Or your favorite food?
I doubt it. In fact, I bet buying those things made you happy. You felt like you gained something you wanted, not like you lost your money.
That is exactly the experience you want to create for people who buy things from you. Your working concept of sales should essentially be anti-selling.
You want people eager and excited to pay you, and feeling satisfied after they did because they are happy with what they acquired. The way to start practicing this is simple. You focus on helping people over making money. When you are genuinely focused on helping someone, you paradoxically accomplish the first step of the sales process. Which is to build trust.
People can tell when you are just trying to get money from them. Think about how you feel about those people in the mall who come up to you and try to sell you things. It’s awkward, uncomfortable, and you do whatever you can to avoid them.
If you approach people with that intention, people may be too polite to end the interaction immediately, but they’re just waiting for their first opportunity to bounce. So by not selling...you are selling.
By not selling, you build trust and complete the first step of the sales process. It can be a bit confusing. This works because human beings are biologically wired for automatic indebtedness.
When someone does something for you or does something to you, you want to reciprocate. You may reciprocate positively, by balancing one kindness with another. Or reciprocate negatively, by balancing a harsh or angry action with your own.
For positive reciprocity or trust to form, you need to make sure that your genuine intention is to give them meaningful results. Which means helping them with something they actually care about and want help with.
Most of the time people who are trying to be “helpful” are just ignoring the other person and trying to shove what they think is right down their throat.
Unsurprisingly, that doesn’t get you very far.
Which leads us to the second list item.
Active listening: How to Communicate Better than 99% of People
If you don’t become great at active listening, you’re going to severely struggle through life. Genuinely helping someone begins with active listening. (it can actually begin a little bit before that, but let me explain active listening first.)
Active listening is like 10/10 important.
If you’ve ever been in a situation where you feel like you’re talking to a wall, or felt trapped in a conversation, that’s how it feels when someone doesn’t actively listen to you. It’s also how you make people feel when you don’t actively listen to them.
If you frequently run into verbal conflicts, or struggle communicating, active listening will help with most of that.
When you actively listen, people literally tell you their problems that they need solved. In a sales context, this is gold. They are feeding you opportunities.
Most of my friends are active listeners because that’s one of the standards that I filter the people I spend time with by. But most people are not active listeners. They are not in tune with the person or people they are communicating with, they can’t “read the room”.
You can practice active listening by doing these four things.
Shut up. Give the other person space and make them feel welcome. Yes, you’ll have back and forth interaction to build rapport. But this isn’t the time for you to mentally masturbate. Trying to help people isn’t a therapy session for you. Your attention should be curiously poised on the other person.
Ask questions. When they pause their speech, make an effort to understand what they said better, and learn more about it by asking questions. The more thoughtful your questions, the more people will feel heard and seen. The more someone feels heard and seen, the more they will trust you. This is how you build trust initially. Building a deeper level of trust has to happen over time, but at the start, the first factor of trust is “can I trust this person with my words, thoughts, and opinions?” If you listen actively, even if you disagree with them, they will usually feel like the answer to that is “yes”.
Get on the same page. Restate what they said back to them in your own words, and confirm with them that you understand correctly.
Let them know you’re engaged. Use gestures and sounds while they are speaking to let them know you are paying attention. Nod your head. Say “yeah” or “mm hm” so they know that you’re listening. If someone tells you something bad that happened to them, say “damn, that sucks”.
Over text, this process would be more cut and dry. Obviously minus body language and live reaction.
Active listening is a skill that has to be learned, so it does take effort and practice.
Now, primary to active listening is what I would call active noticing.
Where by paying attention to someone’s topic choice, tone of voice, body language, and emotional state, you can more or less detect what problem they have before you ask. This is like an advanced, more powerful form of active listening. But that is a topic for another article.
When you start playing in the realm of genuinely trying to solve people’s problems, you open up the door to people wanting to give you money.

Easy Payment: People Who Make More Money Aren’t Smarter. They’re Easier to Pay.
The more difficult it is for someone to pay you, the less likely they are to do it. Friction during the payment process introduces more opportunities for the person you’re selling to to change their mind.
Now, if you’re willing to only accept cash and raw materials, say less. Skip this step.
Otherwise you need a payment processor. Preferably as many as you can get your hands on.
I made this the third list item because it’s kind of useless without the first two, but when I’m talking to people I’ll usually list this one first.
Because technically, this is the first thing you need to make money.
If you have a way to accept payments, you can make deals on the fly. You could go door to door and offer to mow people’s lawns for $15, and whip our your trusty payment processor to seal the deal. it’s vastly more convenient if everyone doesn’t have to pay you cash. You can just take cards.
Payment processors can be connected to online storefronts like Shopify, Stan, or Eden. And in many cases they allow you to take payments from people globally.
On that point specifically, I see a lot of people sidelined because they can’t get a name brand payment processor like Stripe where they live.
My personal, honest advice here, is that if you don’t have access to proper payment processors, and you’re serious about trying to sell services and make your business work, invest in something like Stripe Atlas, or a service that lets you open an LLC in the US or another country that is favorable to payment processors.
The laws and regulations for that kind of thing vary everywhere, so this isn’t any sort of legal advice.
But I do know that we live in a global economy, and people are incredibly paranoid about the idea of opening an LLC (I know I was). It’s one of the lowest risk things you can do. It is literally a limited liability company. The whole point of it is to protect you by differentiating yourself from your business.
And if you’re opening one in the USA, the infrastructure here is built to make it quite easy for people in other countries to open a company. That’s a big reason why we have such a strong economy. (Outside of printing too much money)
There is way too much wasted energy trying to work around this in my opinion.
If you live somewhere where it is easy to have payment processors, then go crazy. Paypal, Venmo, Cashapp, Stripe, Paypal, Zelle, whatever you can get your hands on.
It makes it easier for someone who wants to pay you to give you money. Which reduces friction at the most important part.
But even if you have 500 payment processors, it’s not that likely you’ll be getting paid during your first encounter with someone. So you have to stay in contact.
Follow up: A way to stay in contact
This sounds obvious but it’s actually easy to miss. You meet someone, you’re having a great conversation and vibing with them, and you forget to get their contact information.
Ideally, you want to have multiple points of contact with a person. Not so you can relentlessly harass them with messages, just because they might not answer on all of those channels.
I have to pay attention to my emails, text messages, whatsapp, telegram, discord, slack, and messages inside of Eden. Many other people have to track as many communication channels if not more.
So when you’re in a conversation with someone, try to get at least two routes of contact with them. And ask them what their preferred form of contact is.
If you’re dealing with anyone decently busy they’re probably only going to respond on that one.
Before you part ways, plant some kind of seed for reaching out again.

Bonus: Selling free stuff
I see a lot of people pop up saying they’ve “tried doing work for free” and that it “doesn’t work.”
Whenever I see people say this, I see the same problem. They aren’t genuinely trying to help. When you press for more details, you find that they are trying to rush through a step they they think is supposed to poof money into their bank account.
Similar to how people think if one of their posts performs well on social media, money will poof into their bank account.
Money won’t poof into your bank account, somebody has to send it there through your handy dandy payment processor.
Selling free stuff isn’t that easy. You still need to go through the entire sales process.
“Well if I’m going to go through the entire sales process, why don’t I charge for it and make money?”
1) if you’ve already been trained/practiced doing client work, client management, and the specific skill you’re trying to sell, then go for it. I’d stake my bets that you’ve paid your dues.
2) Beginners tend to be very afraid of paid work. To the point that they won’t even attempt it. Free work is still a ton of pressure for someone starting out, but it’s manageable.
3) To take this to an extreme, if someone pays you and you fail to deliver, they can sue you. If you attempt it for free, and make it clear that you’re doing it for free to gain experience, this is quite unlikely. And you’ll form good relationships.
Offering, selling, closing, and delivering on free offers will teach you all of the skills you need to orchestrate the sales process.
And likely give you some reality checks about your current skills.
As a warning, it’s extremely difficult. But it’s the probably the easiest and most valuable step in a journey where most of the steps are extremely difficult.
Conclusion
You will make far more money by not selling things to people than you will by trying to jump at money.
If you’re in pursuit of making money then your primary focus (see step 1) is to serve, or to help people.
That’s why they call it a “service” because you are serving someone.
This stuff doesn’t just apply to one on one communication, it is relevant to digital products, paid communities, software, and anything else.
In those cases, you’re seeking to create these effects indirectly.
