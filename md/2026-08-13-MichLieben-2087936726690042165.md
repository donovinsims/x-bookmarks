# The Outlier Method: How to Build a LinkedIn Content Strategy From Zero

## Post

Before I write a single post for a new account, I spend a few hours reading other people's.

I pull up a profile, scroll until I hit a post that dwarfs everything around it, then work out exactly why that one broke. Only once I've got a few of those do I write anything.

Most people go the other way. They open a blank page, write something from instinct, publish it, and only afterwards try to explain why one post landed and the next one died.

I ran this live recently with a founder whose agency does six figures a year and whose LinkedIn sat at 893 followers. Here's the process we walked through.

Study first, write second

A post doesn't perform because the writer got lucky with phrasing. It performs because it matches a pattern the feed already rewards. Some writers find that pattern on purpose. Most stumble into it once and never work out what they did.

Before you write anything:

Block real time for research and treat it as its own phase with a slot on the calendar

Collect as many outliers in your niche as you have patience for

Filter that list down to the posts you could actually make yourself. Achievability beats raw size

The filter matters as much as the collection.

A post that did huge numbers because a celebrity reshared it teaches you nothing you can repeat. A post that dwarfed its own author's usual engagement, in a format you could build on a Tuesday afternoon, is the one to take apart.

Find a source before you find a post

Two starting points, and which one you use depends on whether you can already name accounts in your niche.

Path one: start with accounts.

Pick the accounts you want to study. They don't have to be competitors. When I asked the founder which accounts he liked, he named a design account he follows purely because he likes what they make. He competes with them for nothing. That still counts. You're studying what performs, and the widest useful set of examples wins.

Once you've got an account, check whether the person behind it also posts personally. That gives you a second surface to study, and it's often the more interesting one.

Path two: start with keywords.

When you can't name a single account, search by keyword instead of guessing at names. That's the normal case in a niche with no established creator yet.

Either path gives you a profile or a search result. Next you've got to find the specific posts on it that matter.

Learn the baseline before you call anything an outlier

An outlier is a post sitting far outside its own account's normal range. Every account has a different normal, so what qualifies changes from profile to profile.

How we do it:

Scroll a profile's history until you can feel where most posts land. On the profile we studied it went 100 likes, 110, 135, 80, 287, 600. That's the baseline

Watch for what sits far outside it. On that same profile, one post hit 6,000 and another landed just under 7,000. Those two stop you cold. Something a little above average doesn't

When you find a real one, don't move on. Read it twice, then read the comments underneath it. The gap between that post and the account's normal range means something specific happened, and you want to name it before you go looking for the next one

Don't overthink where the line sits. The point is to force a real gap, so you stop chasing posts that did slightly better than average.

Two outliers, two different reasons they worked

Two posts on the same profile broke out for completely different reasons. Take them one at a time.

The first outlier: riding a trend

The first was a rebrand concept: a new logo for the Amazon rainforest, drawn from a pattern inside the forest itself, posted into a topic that already had everyone's attention. It closed in on 7,000 likes on a profile that normally runs a few hundred.

It didn't win on writing. It rode a topic everybody was already talking about, then put a specific point of view on top. That's the part you can copy. Manufacturing a trend from nothing is a different and much harder job.

There's a second layer here. The asset carried the post.

The image was strong enough to stop a scroll on its own, and it takes up serious space in the feed.

That points at a bigger shift. LinkedIn started as a text platform, and the early creators wrote long blocks because that's what the feed paid for. It doesn't pay for that anymore. Infographics and strong visuals hold attention now, and most people in most niches are still publishing walls of text. I told the founder he's got an unfair edge here, because he runs a video agency and the best work his team makes is the stuff he's free to share publicly.

What to take from it:

Watch trends across your whole industry, well past your list of competitor posts

When something's already trending, add a real point of view. A neutral summary of the news gets you nothing

Treat the visual as half the post. It carries weight, it isn't decoration

The second outlier: appealing to emotion

The second post on that same profile got there another way. It was a film about the clichés people hold about people with Down syndrome, and it landed around 737 likes.

No news cycle carried this one.

It worked because it went straight at an emotion, telling a story that pushed back on an assumption most people scrolling had never really examined. Once I put it next to the rest of that profile the pattern was obvious: this creator's best posts are the ones that appeal to how people feel.

What to take from it:

Emotional, stereotype-challenging stories are their own category, separate from trend-jacking

A post doesn't need news relevance to perform. It needs a real point of view on something people already feel strongly about

Both mechanisms run on the same profile at the same time. Assuming only one exists is how you end up with a single trick

Study one profile long enough and you'll find several of these, each working for its own reason. Collect them all before you decide what to write.

One thing to think about before you reuse anything

Studying outliers is research. Taking the finished work someone else produced, a video or an ad their team made, and reposting it under your own logo is a different thing, and honestly I don't know how legal it is.

It happens more than people admit.

A well-produced video goes around, someone downloads it, drops their own branding on it, and posts it as though their team made it. I get why. It reads as organic, and the logo on the video works like a stamp of credibility for the agency running it.

Still, credit the source. If you reuse someone's content for its aesthetic, or as a reference the way this whole method encourages, put the origin in the post. Often they're fine with it, since the credit sends visibility back their way.

Stop scrolling manually past the first few accounts

Scrolling by hand is how you learn a baseline, and there's no shortcut for that part. It falls apart the moment you want to study more than a handful of accounts.

This is where we switch to Scripe.

Type in a profile name and you get that person's follower count, connections, and every recent post in one view. Then you filter.

Open the target profile in the tool

Set a minimum engagement threshold. I start at 300 likes

Set recency at the same time. Last three months only

Narrow by post type or format if you want to

What's left is your shortlist, and you never touch the rest of the feed

Recency gets its own slot for a reason. An outlier from three years ago proves a format worked once, on a platform that's been rebuilt several times since. An outlier from last month proves it works on this feed, under this algorithm, now.

The number one reason people fail here

The number one reason I see people fail on LinkedIn is they've got no bar to aim at. No standard for what a good post on this platform even looks like.

Most have never sat down and studied what actually performs here. Without that reference point, every post gets judged against personal taste, and personal taste and platform reality disagree constantly. A format that looks unpolished to you can be exactly what wins the feed this month.

It cuts the other way too.

LinkedIn being the worst major platform for content quality is precisely why it's worth investing in. The bar is low. Clearing it on purpose, with formats you studied rather than formats you like, is most of the job.

Run it on repeat

Run this on a cadence. It's a habit, and it keeps paying as long as it keeps running.

Source a profile or a keyword. Learn its baseline. Flag what sits far outside it. Name the specific reason it worked, trend, emotion, or something else entirely. Apply that reason to your next post. Then run the whole thing again on a fresh batch, because what performed last quarter isn't guaranteed to perform next quarter.

Do this enough times and you stop needing the checklist. You open a profile, and both the outlier and the reason it worked are obvious.
