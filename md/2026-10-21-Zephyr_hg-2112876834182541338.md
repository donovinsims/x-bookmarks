# Hand real work to AI by giving every bot one job: the four-bot team that runs my X account

## Post

The exact brief each bot gets, plus a prompt that finds the first job worth handing off in your own work.
Most people I talk to have one AI chat that does everything. It writes the emails, plans the week, checks the numbers and drafts the posts, all in the same long conversation.
And then they wonder why the rules they gave it in the morning are gone by the afternoon.
I worked like that for a long time. Now my X account runs on four bots, and each one has exactly one job. One researches, one reviews yesterday's numbers, one checks that a draft sounds like me and one writes tomorrow's posts.
All four run on Grok Bot, and I still read and approve every post before it goes out.
Below is how the team works, what goes in each bot's brief, and a prompt you can run tonight to find your own first one-job bot. I ran it on a test folder so you can see what came back, including the parts it got wrong.
Why one assistant doing everything breaks
I think giving one assistant everything feels efficient and is the reason most setups fall apart after a few weeks.
When one chat holds the research, the numbers, the voice rules and the drafts, it has to guess which rules matter for the job in front of it.
And once the chat gets long enough to compact, the first thing that usually goes is the set of rules you gave it at the start.
A bot with one job only needs the files for that job. So it gets it right more often, and when it does get something wrong, you know exactly which bot to look at.
The four bots and what each one does
The scout. Twice a day it reads what is moving in my corner of X.
It checks 17 accounts I follow closely and counts how many posts an hour a topic gets before it calls something a wave.
On Wednesday morning it watched a post about Claude fixing a slow computer go from 580.7K to 2.11M views overnight, counted 0 to 4 copycat posts an hour, and wrote that it was "two huge posts, not a crowd yet." That one line told me the topic was still open.
The reviewer. Every morning around 8 it pulls yesterday's posts, compares each one to my usual numbers and leaves me a note.
Wednesday's note said my call notes card did 1.74 times my median views. It also caught that the drafts for that day had two masterclass posts, which breaks one of my own rules.
The voice checker. It holds a profile built from six things I actually wrote, 4,369 words of rambles, emails, DMs and a newsletter draft.
Every draft gets a score against that profile. My own samples land around 77 and a generic AI draft around 52, so anything under 76 goes back for a rewrite.
The drafter. It reads the scout's report and the reviewer's note, then writes the next day's posts and sends them to me for approval in the morning.
It can look at my scheduling tool, but its brief says it may never post, schedule or create anything in it.
They talk to each other through files
This is the detail I wish someone had told me first. The bots never chat with each other. Each one writes what it found into a file in a shared folder, and the next one reads that file before it starts.
The scout writes a news file. The reviewer writes a dated note for each day. The drafter reads both before it writes a single line.
Which means I can open any file and see exactly what each bot knew when it did its job. When a post goes wrong, I can find the step that broke in a couple of minutes instead of rereading a conversation that is three days long.
What goes in a bot's brief
Every brief I give a bot has the same six parts:
The job, in one sentence.

When it runs, or what starts it.

What it reads first.

What it hands back, and in what shape.

What it may never do.

How I check its work in under a minute.
The never list is where I would spend the most time. A bot will happily fill a gap you didn't close (an extra discount, a date nobody agreed to, a reply sent in your name), so the brief has to close it for it.
The last part matters more than it looks. If you can't check a bot's work in under a minute, you'll stop checking it, and that is usually the week it starts making things up.

What came back when I ran it
I ran it on a test folder for a made-up one-person design studio: a price list, a client list, an invoice list, three call notes and three emails.
I used the free MiniMax M3.1 Flash model in OpenCode, in its read-only mode, and it took 84 seconds.
It listed six jobs that keep coming back.
The winner was quoting work that sits outside the fixed price list, with four cases across five files: online pre-orders for a bakery site, a schedule page for a yoga studio, a clinic website with online booking, and a second logo direction.
The brief it wrote for that job was honestly better than my first attempts. Two of its never rules were "never invent a price, a discount or an hourly rate" and "never charge rush outside 5 working days", both taken straight from the price list.
Its one-minute check was "every DKK number traces to a line in prices.md, or reads [ASK ME]."
It also got two things wrong. It said it had read 9 files and then listed 11, because it counted its own settings files.
And it decided that 12,000 DKK of one client's website was missing from the books, even though that client's own email says he paid the final invoice. The folder was simply missing one invoice, and it turned that gap into a problem to chase.
So I would treat the brief it writes as a first draft. Keep the structure, then rewrite the never list yourself, because you are the only one who knows where your business can get hurt.
Where the brief goes
Paste the brief in as the instructions of whatever you already use: a Claude Project or Skill, a custom GPT, a Gem or a Grok bot. Give it only the files the brief names and nothing else.
Start it on drafts only.
Read every output for the first week with the source files open next to it, and every time it gets something wrong, add a line to the brief so it can't happen again.
Once that first bot runs without surprises, look at the list it gave you and hand the second most common job to a second bot. That is how my four started, one job at a time.
