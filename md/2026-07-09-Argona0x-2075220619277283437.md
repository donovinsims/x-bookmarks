1. the pool-fence hunter: satellites find the lead before the phone rings

here is how a pool fence gets sold today. a toddler has a close call in a backyard pool, the parent panics, googles in a rush, and calls the first three installers who pick up. the fastest quote wins. the installer never moved until the phone rang.

now watch what a model does instead. it opens free satellite tiles of a town, finds every backyard pool from space, checks which ones have no fence around them, and hands you a list of exact addresses that need a fence and don't have one yet. the lead exists before the homeowner has even thought about calling.

the whole thing rides on one open image model. there's a tool called SAM3 that looks at a picture and outlines the objects in it, wrapped in a small project called geosam that aims it at satellite tiles instead of ordinary photos, so it reads a whole town from space the way it would read a single snapshot. one prompt pulls roughly 1,500 pools out of a ten-square-kilometer patch of suburb, and the county maps itself.

one trick makes or breaks it. do not try to prove a pool has no fence. proving a negative from a blurry satellite photo is exactly how these builds die. instead, look for a fence inside a narrow ring around the pool, and if you can't clearly find one, flag it. detect the barrier or fail, never argue the absence.

find: run geosam / SAM3 across free satellite tiles to pull every backyard pool in a town.

ring-check: look for a barrier in a 1.5m ring around each pool; found a fence, drop it; none found, keep it.

render: drop a rendered fence onto their yard photo (GPT 2).

deliver: a $75 exclusive lead, sold to one contractor per town.

that is a lead-finding machine that works a whole county without you touching it. the footgun is stale imagery and invisible mesh fences, so you feed it dated tiles and the permit records to stay honest. it doesn't work in the EU, where photographing yards like this runs straight into privacy law (GDPR).

the money is cleaner than it sounds. one installer ran a documented $722 postcard drop to a plain mailing list and booked $47,000 in jobs off it. that's sixty-five dollars back for every dollar out. now feed that same funnel a list of homes that provably need a fence, sold exclusively to one contractor per town, and you're renting out a quiet little monopoly for around $2,900 a month.

Fable 5's part is the grind. it shards a county into tiles, keeps a running ledger of what it has already checked, and picks up exactly where it left off after a crash. a chatbot can't do that. a model that holds the same job for days can.

3. the patent graveyard: sell the products nobody owns anymore

in 1937 a man patented a little tool for pressing dimples into your cheeks. it went nowhere for most of a century. then someone sold it again last year, and a video of it did sixty million views on TikTok. the invention was fine. the timing was just eighty years early.

here's the strange fact underneath that story: most patents die. a federal study found 62% of them never become a product at all, and owners walk away from roughly half by simply not paying the upkeep fee. every one of those is a design nobody owns anymore, sitting in a public file, free for anyone to make.

strip away the legal fog and what's left is a paid weekly feed that says: here are the abandoned products people still search for, and here's proof you can sell each one without getting burned. that safety proof is the actual product. sell one wrong and it gets ugly fast, which is why the danger is exactly what you're paid to defuse.

the filter that surfaces them is the first-window lapse: designs given up at the very first 3.5-year fee by a small-entity filer, which is where the cheap solo gadgets and the richest un-picked pile sit. and there are three clearance checks per product: no live continuation patent, no active trademark on the name or the look, no design patent still in force.

that feed is the whole business, and those three clearance checks are why anyone pays for it. skip them and here's what happens: one live design patent means a competitor files a single complaint and Amazon freezes your subscriber's account in 24 to 48 hours, on the accusation alone. the checks are the difference between a real side income and a shut-down.

be honest in the copy: an expired utility patent does not mean the coast is clear, this is not legal advice, and a human still has to read the 95% junk to find the 5% worth selling. it's real work. it also runs about $11,000 a month at a realistic subscriber count, with a ceiling closer to $38,000.

remember the meter i mentioned in the intro. this is where day-long autonomy earns its keep: over 100,000 fee events land every week, and each candidate needs a chain of checks across separate databases. Fable 5 keeps its own ledger and grinds the whole pile, Tuesday to Tuesday, on its own. no human sits through that.

this spot is wide open, mostly because it looks like legal homework. the next one drops the products entirely and sells something you can't hold at all.

4. the mispriced-bet scanner: two markets, one outcome, a locked gap

here's a number that shouldn't exist. on two different prediction markets, the same real-world outcome, "will this bill pass", trades at two different prices. one market's "yes" plus the other market's "no" can add up to less than a dollar. buy both, and when the event resolves, one side pays out a full dollar no matter what happens. you keep the gap.

notice what you're not doing here. you're not forecasting anything. you're not betting your opinion on the bill. a paper out of arXiv this year (2601.01706) showed no model reliably beats the market's own implied odds, so don't try. the edge isn't a better guess. it's that two venues wrote the same event with slightly different words and priced the difference wrong. traders have a name for it, semantic non-fungibility, two bets that only look interchangeable.

that's the one idea: hunt the places where two markets look identical but aren't quite, and let the mismatch pay you.

the product is a daily briefing of the live gaps, legal and US-safe: it tells people where they are, it doesn't place the trade. worth saying plainly, the "risk-free" gap is basis risk wearing a costume: the two events can quietly drift apart, and your cash sits locked while you wait for both to resolve. Polymarket is also geofenced out of the US, so the actual live arbitrage is a non-US upgrade, not the starter version.

the numbers are thin and real. a four-cent gap on a hundred contracts is about $1.38; one documented Clarity Act mismatch cleared 4.82% after fees. small per trade, capped by how much the market can absorb, but it compounds when a model watches every pair all day and night.

the build is do it once, then sit forever. you map how the two exchanges name the same events a single time, by hand, matching every quirk of wording between them, and from that point on you let Fable 5 hold both live sockets open for days and flag every gap the instant it blinks into existence. one setup, and it watches for years.

that's the fourth spot.

5. the amazon hound: wake up to a list of things to flip

imagine waking up, opening your laptop, and finding a short list already on the screen: buy these five things, from these stores, at these prices, flip them on Amazon for this much profit. sourced, priced out, and checked while you slept. that's this machine.

it's the oldest hustle on Amazon, retail arbitrage, buy something cheap in one place and sell it higher on the marketplace, run through a model overnight. watch where the naive version dies. two places, and they're the whole game.

the first place it dies is brand-gating. Amazon locks huge numbers of products so a brand-new seller literally can't list them, and a scanner that ranks by profit first will hand you a beautiful list of things you're not allowed to touch, every one a dead end you only discover after the stock is already sitting in your garage. check the lock before you rank. Amazon's own restriction-check throws out everything you can't sell before it ever reaches the list.

the second place it dies is the fake spread. the profit a basic tool shows you is a lie: it hasn't subtracted a single one of the real costs.

the sale: $32.99 sale price, $14 to buy, so it looks like +$18.99 a unit.

now the real costs: Amazon referral -$4.95, FBA pick, pack and ship -$5.85, returns + storage + inbound -$8.09.

the actual result: -$4.80 a unit.

that's a product that reads as easy profit and quietly loses you nearly five dollars a unit. the fix is a truth layer: pull SellerAmp's metrics and Keepa's price history, subtract every real fee, and only then rank the list. fork a real MIT project called rover, bolt on the gating pre-check and the true-cost math, and run it twice a day on a cheap server.

this one carries the heaviest catch of the seven. it pays in real dollars, but it demands them up front: real capital, $2,000 and up, plus real labor to buy the goods, prep them, and ship them, with Amazon free to gate or suspend you at any point along the way. done right it clears around $500 to $2,000 a month on three or four thousand dollars of capital you keep rotating.

Fable 5 makes it compound: it remembers which product categories actually paid out, sharpens its own search each night, and heals around dead ends without you babysitting it. this spot is open. watch how the last two stop selling physical things entirely.

6. the buy-signal sniper: email the one hour they're ready to buy

most people think cold email is a numbers game: send ten thousand, pray for a few. that's why the average cold email gets a reply about 3.4% of the time. the wrong belief is that volume is the lever. timing is.

look closely at what a buyer does right before they buy. a company quietly adds an "enterprise" pricing tier, then a SOC-2 page shows up (the security badge you need before you can sell to big customers), then three sales roles get posted in a single week, and each move is small enough to miss on its own. none of it looks like much. but all of it says the same thing: we are about to spend money. email them that week and your reply rate jumps to 15-25%.

so the machine watches for the moment, not the mailing list.

the way it catches the moment is mechanical: it normalizes each pricing page and hashes it, so a structural change like a new tier or a SOC-2 page outweighs a $2 price tweak. it keeps a per-account "why now" graph and re-reads it right before every draft, so each email is tied to the exact signal that fired.

that pipeline turns a spray into a single sniper shot, fired the exact week a company is ready to buy. one hard rule, never break it: never write "i saw you visited your pricing page." it reads as surveillance and it torches the deal on contact. point at the public signal, the new tier, the job posts, never at the person. the open blueprint here is a repo called Tendril.

the catch is deliverability, the plain question of whether your mail even lands in the inbox. push your bounce rate over 5% and you burn the sending domain for everyone sitting on it, so the whole operation lives or dies on keeping that number clean long before you ever worry about the copy. it's also not for small deals. below about $15,000 a deal it costs more attention than it returns, so you run a hybrid list: a hand-picked core of dream accounts with a wider auto-discovered layer underneath.

the numbers hold up. the whole stack runs about $70 a month, and a single $22,000 deal pays for the entire year in one shot. one real split of 11,000 emails: the signal-timed ones replied 19.4% of the time, the plain template got 3.1%.

Fable 5's edge here is time. intent decays in minutes, so the model sits on 200 accounts around the clock and moves the hour a signal fires, not the next morning when you finally check.

and the cost warning is real: watch that bounce rate, because one sloppy blast undoes months of warm-up in an afternoon. this spot's still open. the last one is the one that sounds impossible.
