# One video, 30 pieces of content: the repurposing engine that fills a two-week calendar from a single

## Post

You are not short on ideas. You are short on extraction. Here is the full pipeline, prompt by prompt.
Most creators publish a 40-minute video, wait for the numbers, and start a new script the next morning.
That video held eight separate ideas.
Seven of them got watched once, by the people who were already subscribed, and then died inside a file nobody opens again.
The bottleneck was never the ideas. It was the extraction.
This guide takes one upload and turns it into 30 published assets across five platforms, on a 14-day schedule, using prompts you can copy today.
Five steps. By Step 3 you'll have the full matrix built. I'll flag exactly where this breaks, because it breaks in the same three places for everyone.
Bookmark this one. You'll want the prompts open in a second window when you run it.
 
The method, in one screenshot:
Transcript with timestamps → extract 8 load-bearing ideas (not clips) → build the idea × format matrix → write each asset natively → schedule across 14 days → read the comments, pick the next pillar
 
Chapter 0: what repurposing actually is
Gary Vaynerchuk published this model in 2018 and called it the reverse pyramid. One piece of pillar content at the top, a documented day, a keynote, an interview, a Q&A. His team pulls it apart into 30 other pieces, each one shaped for the platform it lands on. He later shipped a free 270-page deck on making 64 pieces in a single day.
 
Eight years later, most people still do the broken version of it.
The broken version: cut three clips, put the same file on TikTok, Reels, and Shorts, post the link on X, call it repurposing.
That is not repurposing. That is redistribution, and the platforms can tell.
Real repurposing changes the unit of work.
The unit is not the video. The unit is the idea.
A 40-minute pillar holds six to ten ideas that can each stand alone. Every one of them can be rewritten as a Short, a thread, a carousel, a pin, an email, a blog section. Eight ideas across four formats is 32 assets before you have recorded anything new.
The video was never the product. It was the raw material.
 
Step 1: get the raw material into text
You cannot process video. You can process a transcript.
Pull the transcript with timestamps. YouTube's own auto-captions work if the audio is clean. Whisper, Descript, or any transcription tool gives you a cleaner file if the audio is not.
 
Two things matter in the output:
Timestamps on every block. Without them you cannot find the moment again, and cutting the clip becomes a manual scrub through 40 minutes.
Speaker labels if there is more than one voice. Interviews fall apart in extraction when the model cannot tell who said the good line.
Drop the transcript into Claude. Long transcripts are exactly the workload the current context windows were built for, and the whole pipeline below assumes the model can see the entire thing at once.
That is the prerequisite. Everything after this is prompting.
 
Step 2: extract ideas, not clips
Here is where almost everyone loses the value.
The instinct is to ask for "the best moments." You get back timestamps of the loudest 30 seconds. Loud is not the same as load-bearing.
 
A load-bearing moment is one where you said something the audience cannot get from the other forty videos on the same topic. A number nobody else published. A mistake you made. A mechanism explained in a way that clicks. A claim someone would argue with.

That last instruction matters. A model asked for eight will produce eight. The "do not pad" line is what keeps you from building four assets on top of a moment that was never good.
Expected result: six to eight ideas, each with a timestamp and a reason to exist. If you get three, your pillar was thin. That is useful information, and it is a scripting problem, not a repurposing problem.
 
Step 3: build the matrix
Now the multiplication.
Every idea gets crossed with every format it fits. Not every idea fits every format, and forcing it is how you end up with 30 pieces of filler.
A contrarian claim works as a Short, a thread, and an email subject line. It dies as a carousel. A step-by-step works as a carousel, a pin, and a blog section. It struggles as a 20-second Short.

That count at the bottom is your calendar.
Thirty is a typical number for a strong 40-minute pillar. Twenty is fine. If it comes back at forty-five, half of them are duplicates and the flag instruction will have caught some of them.
 
Step 4: write each asset natively
The rule that decides whether this works: each platform gets a piece written for that platform, not a piece translated into it.
 
Reasons that are structural, not stylistic:
YouTube Shorts runs around 200 billion daily views in 2026, up from 70 billion in early 2024, and 74% of those views come from people who do not subscribe to the channel. Shorts is a discovery surface. The asset needs to work for someone with zero context.
X rewards dwell time and bookmarks. A thread is not a caption. It is a piece someone reads for ninety seconds and saves.
Pinterest arrives in planning mode, not scrolling mode. A pin sells a future action.
Email is the only one where the audience already knows who you are, so the hook can be quieter and the ask can be direct.
Four prompts, one per surface.
Short-form video scripts. YouTube raised the Shorts ceiling from 60 seconds to 3 minutes in December 2025, which changed what fits. A mechanism explanation that used to be impossible now runs comfortably at 90 seconds.

Step 5: schedule it, then close the loop
Thirty assets published in two days is not a content strategy. It is a burnout weekend that trains the algorithm to think you are a bot.
 
The 14-day layout that works:
Days 1 to 2. The pillar goes live. Two Shorts, the strongest thread, one pin. You are testing which idea has legs before spending the rest.
Days 3 to 7. Two short-form videos per day, spaced. Pins spaced 10 to 20 minutes apart if you are posting several. The email goes out mid-week.
Days 8 to 14. The winners get remade. Whichever Short outperformed gets a second version with a different hook and a different first frame. The blog section publishes. The idea that pulled the most comments becomes the next pillar's opening.
The loop nobody runs: the comments on your micro-content tell you what the next pillar should be. Gary's version of this is asking viewers to timestamp what resonated. Yours can be simpler. Whichever of the eight ideas produced the most replies is the topic of the next 40-minute video. The engine feeds itself.

The three places this breaks
One: clipping by time instead of by idea. A clip cut on a 30-second boundary starts mid-sentence and ends mid-thought. Cut on the idea's edges, even if that means 41 seconds or 97. The timestamp from Step 2 is a starting point, not a border.
Two: the same hook thirty times. Extraction from one source produces assets that share a voice, which is fine, and a hook structure, which is fatal. If four Shorts all open with a number, the fifth gets scrolled. Force hook variety at the prompt level, not in editing.
Three: repurposing a pillar that had nothing in it. This pipeline is a multiplier. Multiply zero and you get thirty pieces of nothing, published on five platforms, with your name on all of them. If Step 2 returns three weak ideas, the fix is upstream. Record a better pillar.
 
When not to run this
The pillar is thin. A 10-minute video with one idea produces four honest assets. Take the four. Stretching it to thirty is how a feed turns into filler.
The content is news. A 14-day schedule outlives a news cycle. Timely content gets a compressed version of this, three days, six assets, or it gets stale mid-calendar.
You have no position. Repurposing amplifies whatever is in the pillar. If the pillar is a summary of what everyone else already said, this pipeline distributes that at five times the volume.
 
 
Swap the pillar
Nothing here is specific to video.
A podcast episode is a pillar. A live stream is a pillar. A 90-minute client workshop is a pillar. A long interview you gave on someone else's channel is a pillar, and it is the cheapest one, because you did not have to produce it.
The transcript is the only input the pipeline needs.
 
 
The shift
MrBeast has 463 million subscribers and a production apparatus behind every upload. You have one video and an afternoon.
The gap is smaller than it looks, because the constraint is not production. It is extraction.
Karpathy, a founding member of OpenAI and now on Anthropic's pretraining team, coined "vibe coding" for the moment developers stopped writing every line and started directing the work. Content is going through the same move. You stop writing every asset and start directing the extraction.
Most creators will keep publishing one video and starting over the next morning, leaving seven ideas unread in a file.
The ones who run the matrix publish the same work five times, in five voices, to five audiences, and none of them notice it came from one afternoon.
The video was never the product.
Run this on your last upload, not your next one. It is already recorded, and the ideas in it are already paid for.
