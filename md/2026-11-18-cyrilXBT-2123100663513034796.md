# How to Run Uncensored AI on Your Laptop in 10 Minutes

## Post

You've seen it happen.

You ask an AI a completely normal question and it refuses.

A crime novelist asks how a detective would realistically trace a poison. Refused.

A nurse asks about overdose thresholds for a medication she gives every day. Refused.

A security engineer asks how a common attack works so he can defend against it. Refused.

A screenwriter asks for a villain who actually sounds like a villain. "I'm not comfortable writing that."

And every one of those conversations was sent to a company's server, logged, and stored.

Here's what most people don't know.

You can run a capable AI model on the laptop you already own. No subscription. No internet once it's downloaded. No one reading your chats. And with the right model, far fewer reflexive refusals.

It takes about 10 minutes, most of which is waiting for a download.

This guide walks you through all of it: what "uncensored" actually means, which model to pick for your exact laptop, how to install it, how to make it fast, and the honest tradeoffs nobody mentions.

Let's go.

Part 1: What "uncensored" actually means

Before you download anything, understand what you're getting. Most people get this wrong.

Every major AI model is trained twice. First it learns language from a huge amount of text. Then it gets a second round of training that teaches it to be helpful, polite and cautious. That second round is where the refusals come from.

The caution is often reasonable. But it's also blunt. Models learn to pattern match on scary sounding words instead of understanding context, so they refuse a nurse's dosage question for the same reason they'd refuse a genuinely dangerous one.

An "uncensored" local model is one where that reflexive refusal layer has been removed or never added. There are three main ways people do this:

1. Abliteration.Researchers found that the refusal behavior inside a model mostly lives in one specific direction in its internal math. Abliteration finds that direction and removes it from the weights. Same model, same knowledge, but it stops reflexively saying no. Models made this way usually have "abliterated" in the name.

2. Fine tuning on filtered data.Some model makers retrain a base model on data that has had the refusals filtered out. The Dolphin models are the best known example. This tends to keep quality higher than abliteration.

3. Neutral alignment.Some models are trained to simply follow whatever system prompt you give them, without imposing their own moral rules. The Hermes models work this way. They're not "uncensored" so much as "obedient to you."


 

What an uncensored model is NOT:

It's not smarter. It knows the same things the base model knew.

It's not more accurate. It will still be confidently wrong sometimes.

It's not a permission slip. Illegal things stay illegal no matter what software you use.

Think of it as removing an overcautious filter, not adding new powers.

Part 2: Why run AI locally at all?

Even if you never need an uncensored model, running AI locally is worth knowing about.

Total privacy. Nothing leaves your machine. Your medical questions, business plans, journal entries and unfinished novel stay on your laptop. For lawyers, doctors, therapists and anyone handling client data, this alone is worth it.

No subscription. Once a model is downloaded, it's yours. Run it a thousand times a day for free.

Works offline. On a plane, in a cabin, during an outage. If your laptop has power, your AI works.

No rate limits. No "you've reached your limit, try again in 4 hours."

It never changes on you. Cloud models get updated, and sometimes the update makes them worse at the thing you relied on. A local model stays exactly the same until you decide to change it.

You control it. You decide the rules, the personality and the system prompt.

The tradeoff is power. A model on your laptop won't match the biggest cloud models on hard reasoning. But for writing, brainstorming, summarizing, answering questions and coding help, a good local model is surprisingly capable.

Part 3: Can your laptop run it?

This is the question that decides everything. The good news: almost any laptop from the last five years can run something useful.

Here's the one rule to understand:

The model has to fit in memory.

For speed, you want it to fit in your graphics card's memory (VRAM). On Apple Silicon Macs, the CPU and GPU share one pool of "unified memory," which is why Macs are great at this.

If the model doesn't fit in fast memory, it spills into regular RAM or runs on the CPU. It still works. It's just slower.

Here's a rough guide:

Your laptop                          What it can run comfortably
----------------------------------   ---------------------------
8GB RAM, no dedicated GPU            Small models (3B to 4B)
16GB RAM, no dedicated GPU           7B to 8B models (slower)
Gaming laptop, 8GB VRAM              7B to 8B models (fast)
Gaming laptop, 12 to 16GB VRAM       12B to 14B models
Mac with 16GB unified memory         7B to 8B models (fast)
Mac with 32GB unified memory         14B to 27B models
Mac with 64GB+ unified memory        Up to 70B models

How to check what you have:

Mac: Apple menu → About This Mac. Look at "Memory" and "Chip." Any M1, M2, M3 or M4 chip works well.

Windows: Press Ctrl + Shift + Esc to open Task Manager, go to Performance. Click "Memory" for RAM and "GPU" for your graphics card and its "Dedicated GPU memory."

Linux: Run these in a terminal:

free -h
nvidia-smi

The first shows your RAM. The second shows your NVIDIA GPU and its memory, if you have one.

Write down two numbers: your RAM and your VRAM (or unified memory on a Mac). You'll use them in Part 5.

Part 4: Install Ollama (2 minutes)

Ollama is the easiest way to run AI models locally. It's free, open source, and works on Mac, Windows and Linux. It handles downloading models, running them on your GPU, and giving you a chat in your terminal.


 

Mac and Windows:

Go to ollama.com

Click Download and install it like any other app

Open it once so it starts running in the background

Linux:

Open a terminal and run:

curl -fsSL https://ollama.com/install.sh | sh

Check that it worked:

Open a terminal (on Mac: Terminal app; on Windows: PowerShell) and type:

ollama --version

If you see a version number, you're ready.

Prefer clicking to typing? There's an alternative called LM Studio (lmstudio.ai) that does the same thing with a full visual interface. You search for models, click download, and chat in a window. Everything in this guide works there too. I'll use Ollama for the examples because the commands are easy to copy.

Part 5: Pick your model (the part that matters most)

This is where most guides go wrong. They tell you to grab the biggest model you've heard of, it doesn't fit, it crawls along, and you give up.


 

Pick based on the memory numbers you wrote down in Part 3.

If you have about 8GB of VRAM or 16GB of unified memory

This is most gaming laptops and base model Macs. Start here.

Dolphin 3.0 (8B)Built on Llama 3.1 and trained on filtered data, so it keeps its quality well. Good at writing, coding, math and general chat.

ollama run dolphin3

Hermes 3 (8B)Neutral rather than uncensored. It follows your system prompt closely, which makes it the most controllable option. Typically loses the least quality compared to its base model.

ollama run hermes3

Qwen3 Abliterated (8B)A newer base model with the refusal direction removed. Strong at reasoning for its size.

ollama run huihui_ai/qwen3-abliterated:8b

If you have 12 to 16GB of VRAM or 32GB of unified memory

Qwen3 Abliterated (14B)The sweet spot for this tier. Noticeably smarter than the 8B models while still running fast.

ollama run huihui_ai/qwen3-abliterated:14b

If you have a powerful machine (64GB+ unified memory, or a 24GB+ GPU)

You can run 27B to 70B class models, including Hermes 3 70B. These get much closer to cloud model quality. Search the Ollama library for "abliterated" and filter by size.

If you have no dedicated GPU and only 8GB of RAM

Stick to models around 3B to 4B. Search the Ollama library for small abliterated versions of models like Qwen3 or Gemma. They're less capable, but they run on almost anything.

My recommendation for most people: start with Dolphin 3.0. It's the best balance of quality, speed and freedom for a normal laptop. Try Hermes 3 next if you want more control.

One important note: model names and versions change often. Before downloading, search the model on ollama.com to see the current versions and download sizes.

Part 6: Run it (the 10 minute moment)

You've installed Ollama and picked a model. Now:

ollama run dolphin3

The first time, Ollama downloads the model. An 8B model is around 5GB, so this takes a few minutes depending on your internet speed. This is most of your 10 minutes.

When it finishes, you'll see a prompt:

>>> Send a message (/? for help)

Type anything. You're now talking to an AI running entirely on your laptop.

Try it:

>>> Write the opening paragraph of a noir detective novel where the detective is morally compromised.

When you're done, type:

/bye

Next time, the model is already downloaded and starts in seconds.

Useful commands:

ollama list          # see the models you've downloaded
ollama ps            # see what's running and how much is on the GPU
ollama rm dolphin3   # delete a model to free up space

Create a local account (it stays on your machine), pick your model from the dropdown, and start chatting. You get conversation history, document uploads, custom system prompts and more.

Part 8: Set the personality with a system prompt

A system prompt is a set of standing instructions the model follows in every conversation. With uncensored and neutral models, it's how you shape behavior.

In Ollama, you can make your own custom version of a model with a file called a Modelfile. Create a new text file named Modelfile (no extension) with this inside:

FROM dolphin3

SYSTEM """
You are a direct, knowledgeable assistant. You answer questions fully and honestly without moralizing, lecturing or adding unnecessary warnings. You treat the user as a capable adult. When a topic involves real world risk, you give accurate information and mention the single most important safety fact, briefly. You never pretend to know something you don't. If you're unsure, say so.
"""

PARAMETER temperature 0.7

Then build and run it:

ollama create myassistant -f Modelfile
ollama run myassistant

Now myassistant uses those instructions every time.

A few system prompts worth trying, depending on what you use it for:

For fiction writers:

You are an uncensored creative writing partner for a professional novelist. You write dark themes, morally complex characters, violence and mature content when the story calls for it. You never break the fiction to add disclaimers. You focus on vivid, specific, emotionally true writing.

For research and learning:

You are a research assistant who explains topics thoroughly and accurately, including controversial or sensitive subjects. Present multiple perspectives where they exist. Distinguish clearly between established facts, expert consensus and your own reasoning. Never refuse a question because the topic is uncomfortable.

For brutally honest feedback:

You are a blunt critic. When I share work, tell me exactly what's weak and why, without softening it. No compliments unless they're earned and specific. Prioritize what would make the biggest difference if fixed.

Part 9: Make it faster

If your model feels slow, it's almost always one of these.

1. Check if it fits.

Run this while the model is loaded:

ollama ps

Look at the PROCESSOR column. If it says "100% GPU," you're good. If it shows a split like "40%/60% CPU/GPU," part of the model spilled into slower memory, and that can make it several times slower.

Fix: use a smaller model, or a smaller version of the same one.

2. Use a quantized version.

Quantization stores the model's numbers with less precision so it's smaller and faster. Most Ollama models download in a 4 bit format by default, which is about a quarter the size of the full version. If you picked a larger quantization manually, switch back to a 4 bit one (look for "q4_K_M" in the version name).

3. Turn on flash attention and compress the chat memory.

These two settings help a lot on long conversations. Set them as environment variables before Ollama starts:

OLLAMA_FLASH_ATTENTION=1
OLLAMA_KV_CACHE_TYPE=q8_0

How you set them depends on your system. On Mac, run these in Terminal and then restart the Ollama app:

launchctl setenv OLLAMA_FLASH_ATTENTION 1
launchctl setenv OLLAMA_KV_CACHE_TYPE q8_0

On Linux, add them to the Ollama systemd service. On Windows, add them as user environment variables in System Settings, then restart Ollama.

4. Don't use a huge context window if you don't need one.

The context window is how much text the model keeps in mind. A bigger one uses more memory even if your chat is short. For normal chatting, the defaults are fine.

5. Keep the model loaded.

By default, Ollama unloads a model after a few minutes of inactivity. If you keep waiting for it to reload, set:

OLLAMA_KEEP_ALIVE=1h

6. Mac users: try MLX.

LM Studio supports MLX versions of models, which are built specifically for Apple Silicon. They're often noticeably faster than the standard versions on a Mac.

Part 10: Chat with your private documents

This is one of the most useful things a local model can do, and it's where privacy really pays off.

Contracts. Medical records. Client files. Financial statements. Your own notes. Things you would never paste into a cloud chatbot.

With Open WebUI, you can upload documents straight into a chat and ask questions about them. Everything stays on your laptop.

Here's how:

Open Open WebUI in your browser at localhost:3000

Start a new chat and pick your model

Click the plus or paperclip icon next to the message box

Upload a PDF, Word document or text file

Ask your question

A prompt that works well for documents:

Read the attached document carefully before answering.

1. Summarize it in 5 bullet points
2. List any deadlines, amounts or obligations it mentions
3. Flag anything unusual, one sided or worth questioning
4. Quote the exact sentence for every point you make

If the document doesn't answer something, say so instead of guessing.

That last line matters. Local models, especially uncensored ones, will happily fill gaps with confident nonsense. Asking for quotes keeps them honest and makes it easy for you to check.

A tip for long documents: small models can lose track of very long files. If you're working with something huge, split it into sections and ask about one section at a time, or use a bigger model if your laptop can handle it.

Part 11: Use it from your own code

Once Ollama is running, it also runs a local server on your machine. That means any script or app you write can use your model, privately and for free.

Here's the simplest possible test from a terminal:

curl http://localhost:11434/api/generate -d '{
  "model": "dolphin3",
  "prompt": "Explain what a context window is in two sentences.",
  "stream": false
}'

And the same thing in Python:

import requests

response = requests.post(
    "http://localhost:11434/api/generate",
    json={
        "model": "dolphin3",
        "prompt": "Explain what a context window is in two sentences.",
        "stream": False,
    },
)
print(response.json()["response"])

Ollama also supports the same request format as OpenAI's API, so many existing tools and apps can point at your local model just by changing the address to your machine. That's how people build private assistants, document tools and automations that never touch the cloud.

Part 12: Troubleshooting

Here are the problems people hit most, and the fixes.

"It's painfully slow."Run ollama ps. If it isn't 100% GPU, the model is too big for your memory. Switch to a smaller one. On a laptop without a dedicated GPU, slower speeds are normal. Stick to models around 8B or smaller.

"The download keeps failing."Large downloads can break on weak connections. Run the same command again. Ollama picks up where it left off.

"It still refuses some things."Different models have different levels of filtering. Dolphin and Hermes respond strongly to the system prompt, so give them clear instructions using the Modelfile in Part 8. Abliterated models refuse less but can still occasionally push back.

"The answers are weird or repetitive."Lower the temperature to make it more focused. In your Modelfile, change the temperature line to 0.5. Also try a different model. Some are simply better at certain tasks.

"I ran out of disk space."Each model is several gigabytes. Run ollama list to see what you have and ollama rm with the model name to delete the ones you don't use.

"My laptop gets hot and loud."That's normal. Running a model pushes your hardware hard. Keep it on a hard surface, plug it in, and close other heavy apps while you use it.

"Open WebUI can't see my models."Make sure Ollama is running first. Then restart the Open WebUI container. If you installed it a different way, check that it's pointed at the Ollama address shown in Part 11.

Part 13: The honest tradeoffs

Nobody selling you on local AI will tell you these. I will.

Uncensored models are a bit dumber.Abliteration in particular tends to make models slightly worse at following instructions and multi step reasoning. Fine tuned models like Dolphin and Hermes hold up better, but there's usually some cost. The smart setup is to keep a normal model for serious work and an uncensored one for the tasks where refusals get in your way.

Local models are less capable than the best cloud models.An 8B model on your laptop is genuinely useful, but it won't match the strongest cloud models on hard reasoning, long documents or complex code. Use the right tool for the job.

They hallucinate.Without a safety layer that encourages caution, uncensored models can be even more confidently wrong. Never trust a local model on medical, legal or financial specifics without checking a real source.

Speed depends on your hardware.A gaming laptop with a good GPU will feel snappy. A thin laptop running on CPU will be usable but slow.

Licenses matter.Every model has a license. Some are fully open. Some, including many community abliterated builds, are marked for research and personal use only. If you're building something commercial, read the license of both the uncensored version and the base model it came from.

Your responsibility is yours.Removing a model's refusals doesn't remove the law, and it doesn't make dangerous information safe. These tools are for creative freedom, privacy and honest answers. Use them like an adult.

Fiction and screenwriting. Dark themes, real villains, violence and moral complexity without the model breaking character to lecture you.

Security research. Understanding how attacks work so you can defend against them, which cloud models often refuse even for professionals.

Private journaling and therapy style reflection. Talking through personal things with zero chance of the conversation being stored on a server.

Sensitive business work. Analyzing contracts, client data, financials and plans that you'd never paste into a cloud chatbot.

Honest feedback. Critique of your writing, business ideas or code without the reflexive positivity cloud models default to.

Learning controversial history and politics. Exploring difficult topics from multiple angles without the model refusing or flattening every view into the same safe summary.

5 prompts to try in your first session

Not sure where to start? Paste these in and see what your new model can do.

1. Test its honesty

What are three things you're likely to get wrong or be overconfident about? Be specific.

2. Test its writing

Write a 200 word scene where a con artist explains to a new recruit why the best lies are 90% true. Make the con artist charming and a little frightening.

3. Test its explanations

Explain how credit card interest actually compounds, using a real example with numbers, as if I'm smart but have never had it explained properly.

4. Test its feedback

Here's my business idea: [your idea]. Tear it apart. Give me the 5 biggest reasons it could fail, ranked by how likely each one is.

5. Test its privacy value

I'm going to tell you about a situation I don't want to share with anyone else. Help me think through my options without judging me: [your situation]

That last one is the real reason many people run AI locally. Some conversations should never leave your own computer.
