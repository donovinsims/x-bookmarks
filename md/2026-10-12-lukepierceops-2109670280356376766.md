# How to Price, Run, and Deliver an AI Audit (Template Attached)

## Post

One of the operators in our group had two wins last month: a $3,000 paid audit for a commercial flooring company, and a $15,000 second phase with a roofing company to redefine their processes and roles before a single agent gets built (AKA an audit). 
That's what the audit should look like. Paid, structured, and the start of the real engagement.
Here's how to price it, run it, and deliver it, with the full structure we use at Boom. Save this one.
Quick disclaimer before we get into it: nothing in here is a guarantee of income or results. Your outcome depends on your execution, your skills, your market, and how consistently you run this. The numbers here come from my agency and the operators I work with, and yours will look different. Treat it as a framework, and nothing in it is financial or legal advice.
Where this came from
When I started posting about this on LinkedIn back in April 2025, mapping the processes before building anything was a new idea for an AI agency. Almost everyone was selling specific AI automations, and that's what most of the courses taught too.
I didn't set out to be different. The process came out of building for a lot of clients. Even back when I was getting jobs off Upwork, about 75% of the time what the client asked for in their post wasn't what they actually needed. You can only build the wrong thing so many times before you decide to build a process for finding the right thing first.
That's the audit. And to be clear, selling specific automations still works extremely well. I see people crushing it that way, and you don't have to run an audit to build a good agency. But posting about this approach is a big part of why my LinkedIn grew from zero to about 25,000 followers in eight months, why I was invited to speak at a conference, and why we won an award for the work.
When I started taking X seriously this January, I didn't see anyone else here talking about it. Now plenty of people have taken it and made it their own, adjusted to the size of company they work with. That's a good thing, but here's the version we run.
A one-hour audit isn't an audit and you need to stop doing it
A lot of agencies run their audit as a single call with the owner, then go build a proposal. They're optimizing for their own time and how much they make for that hour.
They end up making a lot less. Part of the audit is building the report, and the bigger part is building the relationship with the client and their team. You're figuring out how you're going to help them, and the people who will use what you build get to be part of the process.
If it's a one-person company, the founder is the whole team and one conversation can cover it. But when you only meet with the owner and there are ten other people in the business, you might get 80% of what needs to be built. Those ten people never got invited into your process, and they're the ones who decide whether your build gets used.
Here's what that looked like for an agency we coach recently. They showed a home service company a first-phase ROI of over $300K a year, and the owner said it seemed like a big number. The lead estimator, the person who would use the system every day, was never on a call, and the owner's biggest objection was that they'd tried new software before and the team didn't stick with it.
Every one of those problems traces back to the audit. The ROI was questioned because the client never confirmed the inputs. The adoption objection came up because the users were never in the room. I see this most from people without B2B experience, who treat the audit like a billable hour. A real audit is how you earn the build.
What your numbers should look like
Use the two wins above as your benchmark. A few thousand dollars for an audit at a smaller company is a fair starting point, and the price goes up with the number of departments and people you interview.
The roofing deal shows what a good audit leads to. Their second phase is $15,000 to fix processes and redefine roles, and no agents get built until that's done. The operator put it simply: get the processes right first instead of automating broken ones. That's the foundation the build sits on, and clients will pay for it when the audit makes the case.
Charge for it, and don't lean on the credit
A lot of agencies offer to credit the audit fee toward the build. You can include it, and it won't hurt anything, but it doesn't make the offer much stronger.
When the owner buys the audit, they don't know what the build will cost yet. A credit against a number they haven't seen doesn't change their decision. The audit has to be worth the fee on its own, and if you run it the way this guide lays out, it will be.
Instead of offering a credit, offer something of direct value. Something that works great right now is access to some sort of training library, but it needs to be something they 'get'. 
Who's in the room
The owner, and the people who will actually use what you build.
An audit with only the owner gives you the owner's version of the process. The person who runs it every day usually has a different one, and theirs is the one that's real. They're also the person who decides whether your build gets used after you hand it off.
Ask every one of them the same question: what part of your job would you happily never do again? You'll get your best use cases from that answer, and you'll get buy-in at the same time, because they told you what to fix.
The timeline: 2 to 4 weeks
Interviews and dissecting, about a week to a week and a half. Meet with the owner and each person who runs the workflows. Map how the work actually moves.
Building the deliverable, about a week. Turn everything into the maps, the numbers, and the roadmap.
The live walkthrough. Book it before the audit even starts, so the end date is set from day one.
Anything shorter than this is a discovery call. Anything much longer and you're doing the build for free.
What you capture
A workflow is anything that happens more than once a week. For each one, write down:
The trigger. What starts it.
Every step, and who owns each one.
The handoffs. Where it waits on someone else, and for how long.
Duplicate entry. Every place the same data gets typed twice.
The manual hours. Time it with the person who does it. Don't estimate it from the owner's office.
The timed hours become the ranking for everything that follows. The workflow that eats the most hours goes first, whoever suggested it.
What you deliver
Process maps, current and future. This one is non-negotiable. A lot of companies have never seen their own processes drawn out, and seeing it is often the moment they decide. Show the current state with every handoff and duplicate entry flagged, then the future state next to it.
ROI built on numbers they confirmed. Use only inputs the client gave you, then read the calculation back to them: "You said you get this many leads a month, you said this many estimates go out late, your average job is this much, so here's how we got the number." If they say a number is wrong, ask what the fair number is and run it again. Give a conservative number and a best-case number instead of one big figure, and back it with a similar case study if you have one.
A prioritized roadmap. Ranked by the timed hours. When the processes are broken, the first phase is fixing them, the same way the roofing deal was scoped. Agents and automation come after the foundation is right.
Two documents. The full branded report, with their logo and colors, is the detailed version they keep. The presentation is what you actually walk through: less text, the pain points, the numbers, the recommendation, and the next step, with a talk track for each slide so the call stays on the value.
How you deliver it: live, never by email
Never send the audit ahead of the call. A document sent alone invites objections you aren't there to answer, and it gives people a reason to skip the meeting.
Walk it live. Open with: "The full report is here so you have it, but we're not going to go through it in detail today. We've built a presentation to make the most of our time." Then take them through the pain, the numbers they confirmed, and what you recommend.
Book the next call before you hang up. That call is the proposal review, and it gets walked live too.
What the audit leads to
The audit is the first rung of the whole offer:
The audit. Paid, 2 to 4 weeks.
The foundation or a focused build. Process fixes first if they're needed. If the client wants to start small, run a pilot on one thing, charged at full value. Don't discount it and don't credit it toward anything.
The full build. Quote it as a specific total. Or, depending on your credibility, your authority, and the relationship you've built with that client, structure it as a 6-12-month engagement billed every 30 days. It pays like a retainer, but the scope and timeline are capped, and everything you'll do for them is laid out up front. Don't call it a retainer and don't run it like one. This isn't a marketing service where you send 10,000 cold emails a month, book a set number of calls, and the count resets next month. It's a defined build with a defined finish.
Annual maintenance. Required, the same as the build fee, with each new project adding its own number.
The enablement layer. Monthly live training with their team, a recorded library reviewed every month, and lifetime access for the team.
Every rung comes out of the one before it. When the audit is done right, the next step isn't a pitch. It's the obvious move.
The checklist
Save this and run it on your next audit.
Charge for it. A few thousand dollars is a fair starting point for a smaller company.
Interview the owner and every person who'll use the build.
Ask each one what they'd never want to do again.

Time the manual hours with the person who does the work.
Book the live walkthrough before the audit starts.
Deliver current and future process maps.

The audit template
Use this structure for the full report. The presentation pulls its highlights from it.
Cover. Company name, audit dates, and everyone you interviewed.
One-page summary. What's broken, what it's costing them in numbers they confirmed, what you recommend, the investment range, and the next step. If they only read this page, they should be able to say yes.
Who we talked to. Each person's name and role, and the part of their job they'd never want to do again.
Current state. A process map for each workflow, with handoffs, wait times, and duplicate entry flagged.
Workflow inventory. One row per workflow: department, owner, trigger, manual hours per week, and the main issue.
Future state. The same maps redrawn, showing what changes at each step.
ROI. Every input, labeled with who gave it to you. Then the calculation, a conservative number, a best-case number, and a similar case study if you have one.
Roadmap. Phases ranked by hours. Phase one is process fixes when they're needed. Each phase gets the outcome, the timeline, and the investment.
Team and enablement. Who uses what, and how they'll be trained.
Next step. The date of the proposal review.
The presentation is 8 to 10 slides: the pain points in their words, the confirmed numbers, the before and after maps, the roadmap, the investment, and the next step. Every slide gets a few lines of talk track, so the call stays on the value instead of turning into reading a document.
