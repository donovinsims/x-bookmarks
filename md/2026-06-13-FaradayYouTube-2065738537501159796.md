# how to force your brain to crave making content

## Post

Alex Hormozi spends 7% of his time on content. The other 93% he uses to run a portfolio doing $200 million a year.

That's only thirty minutes a day. Here's how to steal it.

Maker vs. Manager

In 2009, the venture capitalist Paul Graham published a short piece on his website. It was called "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule," and it made a deceptively simple observation: there are two different ways of organizing a day.

The manager's schedule is dense. Hour-long meeting blocks back-to-back. The maker's schedule is entirely different. It's long, uninterrupted stretches. of time.

Drop a single meeting into a maker's afternoon and you kill productivity for the entire day. You lose the mental flow-state that makes deep-work creative-tasks possible.

When busy people try to make content on a manager day, two things happen.

Either they procrastinate because some part of them knows that the mental state required for good creative work isn't available in a 45-minute window.

Or they push through, and what comes out sounds like ChatGPT wrote it. Because the reactive, context-switching, high-dopamine mental state of a manager day is essentially the same one ChatGPT operates from. It pattern-matches to what sounds right. It doesn't find anything new.

Hormozi built his content machine around this. If you can move all the maker work out of your schedule entirely, you can run content from a 30-minute manager slot. The slot looks like every other meeting on his calendar. What makes it different is what happens before and after it.

As a busy person, without the time to dedicate an entire day to content, he found out how to do more than any creator.

The Machine (Three Phases)

The machine has three phases.

Pre-Production Shield.

His team spends weeks doing the maker work: researching topics, pulling YouTube data, building outlines that tell Hormozi exactly what to cover. By the time he sits down, the maker work is done.

The Shoot.

Hormozi improvises over the outline with stories from his life-experience. He does this, and then he leaves.

The Multiplication Engine.

The team picks up from here, and does the rest until 297 pieces of content have been scheduled.

For this to work, Hormozi's team cannot write scripts.

A fully scripted video is a processed, cleaned-up rendering of an idea. This means it sounds like every other processed, cleaned-up rendering of the same idea. By using an outline instead, his team forces real-time thinking.

This "script" is a list of questions. These questions exist to pull the insight only you can give.

The Org-Chart

You can run this entire system with just four people. You can also run it alone, which we'll get to in the end.

VA/Producer. Absorbs scheduling, team coordination, and feedback. The single point of contact between you and everyone else.

Editor. Handles cuts, pacing, formatting.

Designer. Thumbnails and graphics.

Strategist. Their job is to understand your audience well enough to know what only you can say to them. They plan the videos.

Build this in-house, not through an agency. An agency applies the same process to every client.

The Solo Version

If you can't put together a team, then this is what you need to do.

Carve out one full day a week as a maker day.

Delete all meetings. On this day, you will do all the maker work (ideas, structure, recording, rough drafts).

The reason Hormozi out-publishes most founders isn't time or discipline. It's that he's engineered a week where the only thing he personally does is the part nobody else can do.
