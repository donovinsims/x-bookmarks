Before we start: what Sonnet 5.5 can and cannot do here

Sonnet 5.5 is the right model for local SEO because local SEO is mostly writing and sorting.

Categories, services, 30 pages, 52 posts, review replies, landmark pages. Hundreds of small, structured writing jobs.

What Sonnet 5.5 is great at:

Turning a list of categories into 30 services that semantically belong to them

Writing 30 pages that follow one structure without drifting

Reading a rank-map export and doing the maths you would skip

Rewording questions so they stop looking copied

What it cannot know:

Where your listing ranks on the map, street by street

How many people search "water heater replacement Houston"

What Google shows on the results page today

Which of your pages Google already shows for a neighbourhood name

The first one needs a geogrid rank tracker (Local Falcon, BrightLocal, whichever you like).

The other three are what the ChatSEO MCP is for. Real data goes in, Sonnet does the thinking, you make the call.

Setup takes five minutes:

Create a Project in Claude and pick Sonnet 5.5.

Paste a business brief into the project instructions: exact business name, address and phone as they appear on your profile, primary category, city, service area, what you actually do and refuse to do.

Turn on web search.

Create a ChatSEO account, add your site, connect Search Console. Then give this to your Claude: https://api.chatseo.app/mcp (the MCP is on the Pro plan).

Every prompt below assumes that brief exists. Without it, Sonnet invents your address.

Module 1: Rank the profile, not the website

Local SEO is not regular SEO in a city.

When someone searches "plumber Houston", Google shows a map with three listings on top. Those are not websites. Those are Google Business Profiles.

60 to 70% of the clicks go to those three.

Fourth place is page two. Nobody scrolls the map.

Google picks those three on three factors:

Proximity. How far the searcher is from your address. You cannot change it.

Relevance. How sure Google is that you do this exact service. Content fills it.

Authority. How much the web trusts you. Links fill it.

Think of them as three glasses. A business far from the searcher needs its relevance and authority glasses fuller to overflow, but it can still win.

Most of your competitors filled in their profile once, three years ago. One category. Zero services. They are ignoring the only two glasses they control.

-> That is why an old business can be outranked in weeks.

Step 1: Run a rank map before you touch anything

A rank map checks where your profile ranks from a grid of points across your city. 13 by 13 is common, 169 points.

Green: positions 1 to 3. You get calls.

Yellow: 4 to 10. Invisible, but close.

Red: Google does not believe you do this service there.

Most businesses see green around their own front door, then yellow and red everywhere else.

That pattern means one thing. You win on proximity only.

Step 2: Turn the map into one number

The number that decides everything later is your top-3%: the share of grid points where you are in positions 1 to 3.

Only green counts. Position 4 is worth zero.

Export the grid as a CSV and hand it to Sonnet.

```

Attached is a rank-map export for my Google Business Profile on the

keyword [KEYWORD]. Columns: point, latitude, longitude, position.

1. Compute my top-3%: points in positions 1 to 3, divided by all

points. Show the count and the division.

2. Describe the shape: where is the green, how far does it reach from

my address (in my brief), which direction is weakest.

3. List every point in positions 4 to 6 separately. Those are the

cheapest wins.

4. Use only the numbers in the file. If a column is missing, say so

and stop.

```

Keep step 3's list. It comes back in Module 5.

Module 2: The 30-minute profile audit

The profile is the thing that ranks, so fix it before building anything.

Three mistakes, the same ones on almost every profile.

Mistake 1: one category

Google allows up to 10 categories. Most businesses pick one.

Every category is another set of searches you can appear for. A plumber with only "Plumber" never shows for "Drainage service" or "Gas installation service".

Aim for 2 to 4 minimum, as long as each one is true.

This is where Sonnet fails, and it fails quietly.

Ask it for categories cold and it hands you ten that sound perfect.

A few of them do not exist in Google's list. No warning, no hedge, same confident tone as the real ones.

You find out when the profile won't take them. Or worse, you settle for the closest real one and it is not what you meant.

The fix: never let it choose from memory. Get the real list (the GMB Everywhere Chrome extension shows which categories your competitors use and how common each is), paste it, then run this.

Rule 1 is the most important line in this module.

Mistake 2: five services

You want 20 services minimum. 30 to 40 in a competitive city.

Each service is one more signal about what you actually do. And each one has to sit under the category it belongs to. Water heater replacement, pipe repair and leak detection go under Plumber. Google checks that services and categories match semantically.

Services are a free text box, so this is pure writing.

Mistake 3: empty boxes

An empty profile reads as a business that closed, a spam listing, or an owner who does not care.

Fill every box:

Description: 750 characters, the maximum. Use all of it.

Photos: 20 minimum. Exterior, interior, team, work in progress.

Attributes: tick every one that applies, and answer No to the ones that don't.

Products and services: from the table above.

Posts: weekly, see below.

Then the part nobody keeps up: activity. Posts, photos, review replies.

One prompt writes the year.

Schedule them in any profile manager and you are done until next September.

The page your profile points to

The profile is half the test. Google also checks that the page in your profile's website field belongs to the same business.

On most single-location businesses that page is the homepage. It needs seven things:

Title tag: primary category + city. "Houston Plumber, Same-Day Service | Brand".

H1: primary category + city.

A Google Maps embed of your profile.

Your secondary categories as H2s.

A widget showing your Google reviews.

The address, character for character as on the profile.

The phone number, character for character.

Bonus: LocalBusiness schema.

Most local homepages have "Home" or the brand name as the title. That is free ground.

Sonnet cannot open your live page and compare. ChatSEO can read it.

Module 3: The Core 30

Your site should mirror your profile, page for page.

3 to 4 categories and 25 to 30 services give you about 30 pages:

1 homepage. The profile's landing page from Module 2.

3 to 4 category pages. Keyword: category + city. "Drainage service Houston".

25 to 30 service pages. Keyword: service + city. "Water heater replacement Houston".

Title and H1 carry the keyword on every one. Same pattern, no exceptions.

The links are the structure

Every level links down from inside a paragraph.

Homepage: one H2 per secondary category, 50 to 100 words, a link in that paragraph to the category page.

Category page: one H2 per service, 50 to 100 words, a link to the service page. 5 to 10 per category.

Service page: why people need it, what it includes, how long it takes, what to expect. In this city.

Menu and footer links do not count here. Google reads those as site structure, not as one page vouching for another.

-> A link in a sentence tells Google what the next page is about. A link in a menu tells it nothing.

Order the 30 by real demand

Sonnet can draft the sitemap in a minute. It cannot tell you which service page to write first.

ChatSEO can.

Write one, then the other 29

Rule 3's marker is the point. A page with three honest gaps gets finished by the owner. A page with three invented facts gets published.

Google does not care who wrote the page. Their guidance says their "focus on the quality of content, rather than how content is produced" and that "appropriate use of AI or automation is not against our guidelines".

What it does care about is whether the page helps. And it judges it as rendered on a phone. A wall of text with no photos reads exactly like what it is.

So a human edits every page. Real job photos, the owner's voice, a table, short paragraphs.

Module 4: Two kinds of links

Content fills the relevance glass. It does nothing for authority.

Since AI can write a thousand pages overnight, links are how Google tells a page someone vouches for from one nobody has read.

You need two types.

Proof links. Medium-quality, from reasonable sites, not necessarily local. One per page minimum. 30 for the Core 30.

Local authority links. From organisations in your area that say "this is a real business here". This is what gets you from yellow to green.

The best one is the chamber of commerce. $200 to $300 a year in most cities, a directory listing, a link.

You can join more than one. Every chamber within driving distance of your service area counts.

Then everything that sponsors local life:

Youth sports leagues

Local charities and their events

Community events with a sponsors page

School fundraisers

Yes, most of it costs money. It is also good for the business anyway. And these are exactly the links nobody can fake.

Finding them used to be someone's full-time job. With web search on, it is one prompt.

Module 5: The number decides the next page

This is where most local campaigns go wrong.

Once the Core 30 and its links are live, you have two options. Write more content about your services (topical), or build pages about places (geographic).

Most agencies pick by feel. They build "plumber in River Oaks" pages before Google trusts them, and those pages do nothing. Or they keep answering FAQs forever when they should be expanding.

The rank map settles it.

Step 1: Set your threshold

30 days after launch, rank-map your keyword again. Then rank-map the 3 or 4 businesses that dominate it.

The leader's top-3% is your ceiling.

In a small town the leader can sit above 90%. In a city like Houston, the best plumber might be under 30%. Competition caps everyone.

Your threshold is 25 to 50% of the leader's number.

Below it: Google does not believe you're an expert yet. Build topical.

At or above it: Google trusts you. Now push outward. Build geographic.

Step 2a: Below threshold, answer questions

Not blog posts. Not "10 signs you need a new water heater".

Supporting pages that answer the exact questions your customers ask, each linked from the Core 30 page it belongs to.

Two sources. The first is what Google shows for your keyword without the city. That is live data, so it comes from ChatSEO.

Rule 2 exists because copying Google's questions word for word used to work. Every SEO did it. It was abused enough that it stopped being worth much.

Then on each Core 30 page: the question as a heading, a 20 to 30 word answer, and a link inside that answer to the full page. "Replacement cost depends on tank type and installation, see our full breakdown of water heater replacement costs."

Every supporting page gets its own proof link. 10 to 20 more.

Rank-map every month. Keep going until you cross the threshold.

Step 2b: At threshold, go after the yellow

Remember the list of points in positions 4 to 6 from Module 1?

That is your map. Moving from 4 to 3 is cheap. Moving from 19 to 4 gets you nothing, because 4 is still invisible.

Find the places Google already knows in those zones: a neighbourhood, a park, a big intersection, a shopping centre, a school. If Google Maps names it, Google considers it real.

Only where your customers live. Not the lake.

Then check what you already half-rank for. ChatSEO reads it from your Search Console.

Write each page about the place, not about you. The housing (old homes with cast iron, new builds with PEX), the problems common there, businesses nearby, the route your team drives to get there.

A page that swaps the city name and nothing else is what Google's spam policy calls doorway abuse. Its example is literally "pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page".

Link every place page from one locations hub, and from each place page to its service page. One proof link each.

Step 3: Every month, the same loop

Rank-map the keyword.

Below threshold: topical.

At threshold: geographic.

Green everywhere: defend. Keep the posts going, reply to every review, add services as the business changes.

A competitor who is still publishing will push a sleeping leader out of the top 3.

What this does not prove

ChatSEO is not a local business. This is not our case study, and I would rather say that than have it said in the replies.

It is the system local SEO agencies run for their clients, rebuilt so Sonnet does the writing. The steps are standard. The speed is the only new part.

The threshold is a rule of thumb, not a law. 25 to 50% is a range because markets differ, and you will misjudge yours at least once.

Proximity still wins at the edges. The glasses can overflow, but a listing across town is carrying the one penalty you cannot fix. In a dense city there will be streets you never turn green.

And it is not free. A geogrid tracker, chamber fees, sponsorships, proof links. The prompts are free. The authority is not.

The part I am confident generalises is the order. The part I am not confident generalises is how fast.

What I would do on Monday for a local business

Write the business brief and set up the Claude Project

Rank-map the main keyword and compute the top-3%

Fix categories from the real list, then services, then every empty box

Make the homepage pass all seven checks

Build the Core 30 in volume order, one human edit per page

Join every chamber in range and get one proof link per page

Rank-map again at 30 days and let the number pick topical or geographic

In that order, because each one only works if the previous one exists.

Map it. Fix the profile. Mirror it in 30 pages. Earn the links. Let the number choose.
