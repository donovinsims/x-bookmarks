# How to Use Classic SEO for a Local Business (Blogs First): "Content Is Still King"

Everyone is trying to game the system for AI, he says, or do something different.

In fact, Tim argues that classic SEO works the best with AI.

He shows dashboards from businesses he is working with. In his words they are seeing exponential growth, more traffic, more AI citations and overviews, and more backlinks.

What Tim walks through after that, screen by screen, is the classic work he would start with, not a day-by-day calendar.

1. Treat blogs as the fastest classic SEO win

When Tim says classic SEO, he means SEO content, specifically blogs.

He calls blogs the easiest way to get some big wins fast, because you can publish high-quality pages that target specific services, keywords and locations.

Those pages, in his view, still get picked up by Google, by AI overviews and by AI citations.

"Content is still king," Tim says.

2. Aim at the bottom of the funnel

The first tactic on his list is bottom-of-the-funnel keywords. That is where Tim says the money is made, because those searches have the highest chance of turning into a customer.

He draws a funnel. The top is people gathering information, searching broad terms, still far from a decision.

The bottom is people who already know what they want. Their searches are more specific, usually a problem and a solution.

His example: searching for a new car is top of the funnel. Searching for a minivan that holds eight people, is easy on gas and is reliable is bottom of the funnel, from someone who has already done the research.

If you can put content in front of that person that shows why your offer fits, and why it is even better, Tim says they are much closer to buying.

3. Match search intent, then get a list of those queries

Bottom-of-the-funnel only works if the article also matches search intent.

Tim has a prompt for that, called AI Search Intent Extractor. You give it your brand and your website, and it outputs high-intent, bottom-of-the-funnel queries.

The AI Search Intent Extractor prompt Tim opens, which maps buyer stages and focuses on product-aware and most-aware queries.

Each row, he says, gives you the query or prompt, the buyer stage, the intent angle and why it converts. Then you turn those prompts into articles.

He is careful about the wording. Tim calls them prompts, but he means the way people actually search: a prompt, a search term or a long-tail search term.

I would treat that list as a starting point and delete anything that does not sound like a real customer.

4. Give every article real structure, and do not copy-paste from AI

Tim's next check is technical SEO structure. H2s, H3s and a meta description still matter, or the page will not get indexed and picked up.

He is just as firm about the writing itself: no copy-and-paste from AI.

Claude, Tim says, just released watermarks. You cannot see them, but they are embedded, and in his view they can show if the text was copied directly from AI.

That is his claim about the watermarks, not a test I ran.

5. Embed your brand so the article is not generic

The way Tim avoids that paste problem is to write inside Arvow, using your own brand assets.

In the template he can set the language, a format, the structure and a call to action. He can link a sitemap and include a knowledge base or case studies, so those details get written into the article.

An SEO article template, where Tim sets language, tone of voice, point of view and custom writing instructions.

Images can be your own or generated, or it can place a relevant YouTube video. Then you drop in the keyword or prompt as the title and create the article.

He opens one to show the SEO pieces already in place: a meta description, H2s, H3s and alt text.

From there you publish it to your website. Tim says it integrates with any website provider.

A generated lawn-care article with H3s, a focus keyword, a meta description and alt text on the featured image.

I would still read the article before it goes live. Brand assets help, but your name is on the page.

6. Publish one article a day, not twenty

Content only works, Tim says, if you have a cadence. Consistency is what compounds into the kind of growth he showed at the start.

He is not talking about creating 20 articles a day. That dump, in his view, looks spammy, and the algorithm will not like seeing a pile of pages land at once.

What he recommends is closer to one article a day, every day. A slow, consistent release of high-quality content is what Tim thinks gets you ranking, shown more and picked up.

7. Make the articles non-commodity, then add variables

Quality, for Tim, means the article is not something anyone could get from a generic chatbot.

His example of a commodity piece is "what are the top five things new home buyers should consider when buying a house." That is not original, and it does not add anything new.

Non-commodity articles add an original aspect, some information that is yours. That is why he keeps coming back to brand assets, a knowledge base and case studies.

Variable articles are how Tim takes that further. Google, he says, has specifically said they want non-commodity articles that add value for the end user, and a variable article is how you can do that.

Inside Arvow you go to article templates, pick a custom template, add a prompt and a markdown outline, and reuse it. Each run creates a different result.

He shows a simple plumbing template. You fill in the service, such as toilet repair, the city, such as Naples, and the state, such as Florida, then generate the article.

The Plumbing variable template Tim uses, with fields for service, city and state.

He already has one for sink repair targeting Buffalo, New York, with sink repair as the keyword. He also shows a more granular real-estate version, with variables for the topic, the market, a firsthand pattern, a common mistake and insider advice.

The better you fill those in, Tim says, the more unique the article becomes.

He has used this across niches. One example he mentions is a protein-powder piece: they tested eight protein powders, and two big names were 18% underdosed.

8. Pair the blogs with your Google Business Profile

Tim's last content point is that this plan pairs with your Google Business Profile.

He mentions you can connect Google Search Console, automate posts on the profile and automate review replies. In his words, those two things alone pair nicely with the content strategy, because the profile is not going anywhere and is even more important now.

I would not let the profile wait until the blog is done.

To be precise about Automatic Backlinks: it is a one-way, credit-paid network of in-content links placed inside articles that AutoBlog publishes. You get 1 free link a day, then each verified link costs 15 credits, with no reciprocity, no Domain Rating matching and no vetting.

Where to start

If I were a local business starting this week, I would pick one bottom-of-the-funnel service and city topic, write one structured article with my own details in it, and publish it. Then I would reply to my last few reviews and schedule the next article for tomorrow: see Arvow pricing. Use code Tim10 for 10% off.

What I would not conclude

The thumbnail says Tim would fix your rankings in 30 days, and it shows a number-one pin on Maps with a 5.0 rating. The video never walks through a 30-day calendar, and it shows no ranking, call or Maps result for a business that followed this plan.

The YouTube title uses the same 30-day framing. I am treating that as packaging, not as a claim the recording supports.

The dashboards at the start belong to businesses Tim says he is working with. They show real growth on screen, including 1.1K AI Overviews, but they are not a before-and-after for this exact workflow, and I am not naming the sites.

Tim's view that a daily cadence gets you ranked, and his view of Claude watermarks, are his opinions. The video does not test them.
