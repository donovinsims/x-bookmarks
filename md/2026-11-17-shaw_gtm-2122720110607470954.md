# Google Maps scraping playbook for local outbound (full course)

## Post

Google Maps is the best free database of local businesses you'll ever get, and most people scrape it like they're grabbing a handful of sand.

They type "plumbers in Dallas," export whatever comes back, and call it a list. Then they wonder why half the city is missing and the emails bounce.

I've been running outbound for B2B clients for about two years, and local lead gen is a motion I take seriously. For local businesses, Maps is literally the database. The owner usually isn't in a professional database. The website, if there is one, is years out of date. The public record of the business is a pin, a phone number, a category, a handful of reviews, and an address.

So this is the full playbook. How I decide what to search, how I search so I actually cover the market, the tools I use to scrape and enrich, and how I turn the raw pull into a list you can send from. Bookmark it.

I pull the map with RapidAPI Maps Data. I fill gaps and confirm listings with Serper. I enrich the blank rows with Parallel AI (@p0).

Decide what "local" means before you pull

This sounds obvious and almost nobody writes it down.

Local can mean a service area, a city, a set of zip codes, a drive-time radius around a warehouse, or a list of metro areas you're willing to serve. Those are not the same thing. If you pull "HVAC companies in Texas" you will get a pile that includes a one-truck shop in a town you'll never visit and a regional player with six locations. If your offer only works inside a service radius, the pull has to respect that radius.

Write the GTM first, same as any other list. What you offer, who it's for, who it's not for, the geography, the size of shop you actually want, and the person you want to reach. An owner-operator shop and a 15-location group can share a category name and have nothing else in common.

Also write the exclusions while you're sober. Chain stores, if you don't want them. Franchises, if the local manager can't buy. Categories that look adjacent in Maps and are a waste of time for your offer. Businesses with no website, if your motion depends on email. Businesses that are only a pin and a phone number, if you do plan to call. Be explicit, or the scrape will decide for you.

Search the map the way it actually works

One big search will never give you the whole market.

Maps shows you a limited set of results for any single search, weighted toward whatever is closest to the center of the map and whatever it thinks is most relevant. Type "HVAC companies in Texas" and you'll get a sliver of Texas. The businesses on the edges of every search just never show up.

So I break the market into small pieces and search each one.

→ Tile the geography.

Search zip code by zip code, or neighborhood by neighborhood in dense cities. In rural areas, a town or a county at a time works.

→ Run category synonyms.

A roofer can be listed as "roofing contractor," "roofer," "roofing service," or "construction company." I write the synonym list before I scrape and run every one in every tile.

→ Log every search.

Tile, category, date, and how many results came back. If a tile returns a full page of results, it's probably too big, so I split it and search again.

This is where most of the list comes from. The overlap between tiles and synonyms creates duplicates, and that's fine. You'll clean them later. Missing businesses is the problem you can't fix later, because you won't know they exist.

Scrape the fields that matter

Don't do this by hand. I run the tiles and categories through RapidAPI Maps Data and take the file it gives me back. Serper is there if a tile looks thin or a listing needs a second look. You want one row per location, with the fields the listing actually has:

→ business name

→ category

→ address, 

-> city 

-> state

-> zip

→ phone

→ website, when there is one

→ rating and review count

→ hours, if they're listed

→ the Maps URL or place identifier

→ a short status if the listing says it's closed or temporarily closed

If a company has multiple locations, I keep the locations distinct at this stage. You can roll them up later. You can't un-merge a mess once you've collapsed three addresses into one row and lost the phones.

A raw pull will look bigger than the real list. That's normal. Maps is generous with categories. A company can show up under more than one label. A dentist and a dental lab can sit closer in a category tree than they sit in real life. Cleaning starts here, in the acceptance that the first export is dirty.

The tools I use to pull and enrich

RapidAPI Maps Data does the bulk pull. It's a Google Maps data API hosted on RapidAPI. You send a search query, and you can pin that search to a latitude and a longitude so each call stays inside one tile. You get local business listings back. When you already have a business id or a Google place id, a Place info endpoint fills in the record. This is what I run across every tile and every category synonym.

Serper is a Google search API with regular web search, plus maps and places endpoints. I use it after the bulk pull. If a tile comes back thin, I run the same query through Serper Maps and compare. If a website is missing or looks like a directory, or a listing might be closed or duplicated, I search the business name plus the city. Serper is how I fill gaps and confirm the row I kept.

The pull still leaves holes. Plenty of Maps rows have a website and no email.

For company emails that are still empty, I use a waterfall. Only the remaining blanks go to the next step.

→ Plain HTML-2-text first. (@ENowoslawski showed me the pypi project - https://pypi.org/project/html2text/)

I fetch the website and read the contact page, the footer, and the about page as text. A lot of local shops publish info@ or contact@ in plain HTML.

→ Spider Cloud second. (spider.cloud -> MASSIVELY UNDERRATED)

If the simple fetch comes back empty, the site may be JavaScript-heavy, or the email may sit on a rendered page the first pass never saw. Spider Cloud is a scraping API that fetches a URL, renders JavaScript when the page needs it, and returns the content as text or markdown.

→ Parallel AI last.

Parallel is a web research and extraction API. The Extract API reads public URLs and returns clean markdown. The Task API can take a company name and a website and research extra fields, with citations. I use it on the rows still blank after the first two passes. I ask for the published email, and for any owner name or service area on the site.

First cleaning pass: drop what should never have been in the file

I go through a hard cut before I spend money finding people or emails.

Closed listings come out. Temporarily closed is a judgment call, but I don't email a business that the map already says is shut. Duplicate pins for the same location come out. Same phone, same address, slightly different name, that's usually one business with a messy listing.

Then I look at category junk. Maps will hand you adjacent categories you did not ask for. If you wanted commercial landscapers, you do not need lawn-mower repair or a garden center, and you don't need tree removal unless that was in the brief. I keep a reject reason on those rows so I can see whether the pull is sloppy or my category list is.

Chains and franchises get flagged first. I only delete them when the GTM says they're out. If you only want independents, this is a hard drop. If you sell something a multi-location operator would buy, you may want to roll locations up to the parent and reach the person who actually buys for the group. Either way, you have to notice that "7 locations, same logo" is not 7 companies.

Geography gets a second pass too. Pins land in the wrong town. A service-area business lists a virtual office. A company is "in" the city you searched because that's the search radius. It doesn't mean they actually serve the city. If the offer is local, I trust the address and the service language on the website more than I trust the fact that it appeared in the search.

Website and phone, plus a basic sanity check

A surprising number of Maps rows are not a business you can sell to.

Some have no website and no real phone. Some have a Facebook page and nothing else. Some have a website that belongs to a directory or to another company. Social profiles show up here too. I check the domain when a website is present. If the domain is a Facebook URL or a booking directory, I fix it or drop it. Same if the page doesn't match the business name.

No website doesn't always mean drop. If the motion includes calling, a clean phone and a confirmed open listing can still be a row worth keeping. If the motion is email-only, a row with no domain is just a row on a spreadsheet. I'm blunt about this with teams that want a big list more than a usable one.

I also look at the listing like a buyer would. Zero reviews and a brand-new pin can mean a real new shop, which is interesting. It can also mean a thin or fake listing. A site that doesn't load, or a name that doesn't match the signage in the photos, gets held for review instead of being treated as ready.

Deduplicate like it matters

Local data duplicates in annoying ways.

The same shop shows up twice with an "Inc" on one name and not the other. A second pin sits 40 feet down the street. A headquarters and a location share a phone. Two towns have a business with the same name and different owners. If you dedupe only on name, you'll merge the wrong things and keep the wrong things.

I dedupe on a stack of keys, in practice: the place identifier when I have it, then a normalized phone, then a normalized address, then a normalized domain. The name is only a hint. When two rows might be the same company in two locations, I keep both locations and link them, and I wait to collapse them until I know whether the offer is sold per location or to a parent.

This is tedious. It's also where bounce risk and embarrassed emails come from, when the same owner gets the same message twice from two "different" records.

Why info@ is the best data you'll get for local

For a local business, the generic inbox is usually the owner's inbox. A 6-person plumbing shop doesn't have a receptionist triaging email. The owner set up info@ when they built the website, it forwards to their phone, and they check it between jobs because that's where quote requests land. When you email info@ at a shop like that, you're landing exactly where they look for new business.

It's also the most reliable address you can find. The owner's personal email often isn't in any database, and a guessed firstname@ on a tiny domain is a bounce waiting to happen. Info@ is published on the website, the Maps listing or the Facebook page by the business itself, so you know it's real and it's meant to receive mail from strangers.

Finding it is the easy part. The website came with the Maps pull, so you scrape the contact page, the footer and the about page for any published address. Info@, contact@, hello@, office@, whatever they use. If the site has nothing, check the Facebook page, because a lot of local shops list their email there and nowhere else.

Then treat it like any other address. Run it through MillionVerifier or BounceBan before it goes near a sending inbox. Small business domains are full of catch-alls and dead mailboxes from a website someone built in 2014, and a published address can still be abandoned. Verification is what separates the info@ that the owner reads from the one nobody has opened in years.

Write for the person who actually opens it. Address the business by name, since you don't know for sure who's reading. Keep it short enough to read on a phone in a truck between jobs. One clear reason you're writing, one easy question. The owner who reads info@ is busy, but they're the one who decides, and they read every email in there because any one of them could be a paying job.

So I flip the usual logic for local lists. The generic inbox is my primary contact, and I only go hunting for a direct owner email when the shop is big enough to have someone screening the inbox, like a multi-location group with an office manager. For the one-truck shop and the 10-person crew, info@ is the decision maker's front door, and it's the best data you'll get.

Now find the person

Only after the account list is clean do I go look for people.

For a local shop, the title is often owner, founder, operator, or general manager. For a multi-location group, it may be a regional manager or the person who actually runs marketing or operations. I don't pretend every HVAC company has a VP of Demand Generation. Write title families that match how these businesses are actually staffed.

For the small shop, I already have the published inbox from the site, and that is the contact I send to. I only spend a person-level waterfall on bigger shops, where someone may be screening info@, and on the rows where no generic inbox showed up. Then I run the same email waterfall I use on other lists: @blitzapi, QuickEnrich, GetLeads, Icypeas, @prospeo_io, then TryKitt.

Phone already came from the map in many cases, which is one of the quiet strengths of this source. If I need a mobile later, when someone replies or when we want to call, @prospeo_io is what I use if the signature or the listing doesn't have one.

Then verify, then suppress

Finding an address and verifying it are two separate steps. I don't load a local list into a sending inbox just because a finder returned an address.

After the whole queue has been through finding, I verify with MillionVerifier or BounceBan. Catch-alls are common on small business domains. I don't treat a catch-all as "good enough." It goes to a second pass, and if it still isn't clean, it doesn't get sent.

Suppression still applies. Existing customers, do-not-contact, people you've already booked, other locations of a company you already reached. Local markets are small, and word travels. Sending into a group you already work with, or emailing the same owner at two locations without noticing, is how you look careless in a town where everyone knows each other.

What "clean" actually means

A clean local list is smaller than the scrape, and that's the point.

Every remaining row should have a reason it's in scope, a geography that matches the GTM, a way to reach the business (a verified email, a real phone, or both), and a person or a well-understood generic inbox. Closed, junk-category, chain-excluded, duplicate, and wrong-geo rows should be out, with a reason, so you can defend the list if someone asks why it shrank.

If you want a signal layer on top, Maps gives you some of the better ones for this market. A new listing, a review spike, a rating that's slipped, or a second location that just appeared. I add those after the list is clean. A bad review on a closed shop doesn't belong on the list.

Do this in this order. Write the GTM, pull with RapidAPI Maps Data, fill gaps with Serper, clean, find the inbox, find the person, then verify. Enrich only the blanks, with Parallel last. The local list stops feeling like a messy scrape. It starts feeling like a list you can actually send from.

Pull every pin. Clean every row. That's the whole course.
