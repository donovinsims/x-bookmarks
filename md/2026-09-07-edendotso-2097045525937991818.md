# How to Write a Viral Substack Article as an Absolute Beginner

## Post

Most beginner creators fall into one of two camps.
The first copies whatever trend is trending, chases viral formats, and starts saying that they’re shadowbanned whenever their growth stalls.
The second insists on being “original” and “authentic” without ever studying the psychology behind what gets attention.
Both fail for the same reasons.
They don’t understand the actual principles behind social media growth, and they don’t know how to balance their favorite ideas with what’s proven to work.
That’s what this article is about.
The stuff that works across any format.
Reel scripts
YouTube scripts
Tweets
Newsletters
Because the fundamentals of what makes content good don’t change between platforms.
And yes, when I say “viral,” I’m not guaranteeing you’ll go viral.
Nobody can guarantee that.
At minimum, this will make you a better article writer. You will increase your skill, which means you’ll be able to sustain whatever growth you do get. Unlike the people who copy a viral format, get a spike, and then crash back to zero because they never learned why it worked.
At maximum, you will become a legendary writer whose name is discussed for generations.
Why Substack?
From what I’ve seen in the latest Creator Bootcamps (next one starts in 2 days, by the way) new creators are seeing more success on Substack and Threads than on any other platforms right now.
I’ve been bullish on Substack for a long time, here’s why.
1) You own your audience
Your followers on Substack aren’t followers, they’re newsletter subscribers.
You literally get their email, which means you’re not locked into the platform.
You can take your list and leave anytime.
On Instagram, Twitter, or YouTube, your account can get shut down tomorrow and your entire livelihood disappears. Everyone tells you to build an email list so that you can “own your audience”, Substack does that by default.
2) It rewards depth
Substack culture is somewhat anti-influencer.
The clickbait, the attention hijacking and cheap dopamine that exists on every other exists less there.
At least for now.
Every platform eventually conforms to what people’s want, and they want to waste time and get addicted.
We can’t stop that, but we can contribute to the deep thinking by maintaining our own minds and creating content we want to see in the world.
3) It’s got short and long form
Substack has two formats, newsletter articles (long form) and Notes (short form, like tweets).
Notes give you an incredible way to grow your email list. That’s how most people are growing on Substack right now.
Articles let you create more “bottom of funnel” content.
Short-form content is good for getting distribution quickly, but it is not a great way to build a lasting brand. You can build a brand with it, but long-form content like YouTube videos, podcasts, Substack articles, and X articles. are where people really have a chance to connect with you.
Think of Substack as YouTube for articles
The Substack algorithm feels similar to growing on YouTube. And anyone who understands YouTube will tell you that growth comes down to three things.
The title (does someone want to click it?)
The hook (does the intro grab them?)
The structure (are the sections, key points, and headlines structured to capture and hold attention?)
Are your section headers structured like YouTube titles?
Are you including block quotes?
Big ideas?
How are you holding attention beyond the opening?
A useful mental model is to think of a long-form article as stringing a bunch of tweets together.
If you can write one hard-hitting, condensed, one-idea-per-paragraph tweet, then a long-form article is just you stringing those together and structuring your sentences so people don’t want to stop reading.
That takes practice. But that’s the frame.
Study what works before you try to be original
People get upset about this when I talk about it.
But whenever you hear “Just be original! Be authentic!”
Take a step back and question that.
How do you actually learn effectively?
It’s not from schools and it’s not from memorization, it’s from the master-apprentice model. A mentor has the skill, and an apprentice (you) emulates them until they develop the taste and ability to be creative on their own.
How can you create something effectively without actually experiencing how it works?
I would rather you study how great content is written, reverse engineer it, and break it down than stick your head in a book or watch endless tutorials.
This is not about copying. It’s about imitating and emulating to learn. There’s a difference.
It’s also where Eden comes into play.

I’m not going to re-hash how to manually do research across platforms, how to build a swipe file, how to reverse engineer what works, how to organize files and documents, etc. The whole reason that we built Eden is to radically simplify that whole process.

Step 1: Build a swipe file
Your first task is to find five “damn, I wish I wrote that” ideas.
It doesn’t matter what platform they come from.
It’s actually ideal if they don’t come from Substack, because you’re just looking for ideas. You’ll research title structures and packaging later.
Here’s how to find them.
In Eden
Browse the Discover feed.
 
Try to look for content that did better than the creator’s average (outlier content).
This is very easy in Eden, because we put a little outlier score next to every post.
If a post did 33x better than their typical work, you can assume the idea and the title structure are worth studying.
The Breakouts tab specifically surfaces smaller creators going viral right now.
Everyone copies the big creators. You want to find ideas that are just now picking up so you have a competitive advantage.
Follow creators in your niche.
 
Use the Creators tab to find top creators, filter by their best content, and save what resonates.
Ask the chat.
 
“What are the top performing Substack articles right now?” or “What are top performing ideas in productivity?”
The chat can search across the entire Discover feed.
Save from anywhere.
When you’re scrolling social media and see a great idea, save it. Use the Chrome extension or mobile app. A book, a conversation, a personal experience. Anything. You just need an idea to start with.
Save these five ideas to a board. Call it your swipe file. The point isn’t to copy any single one. The point is to develop pattern recognition. You look at all of them, and then you merge them together. Creativity, in a simple definition, is pairing two things together that haven’t been paired before. What if you mash ten things together in one article? That’s extremely unique.
Step 2: Reverse engineer what worked
Pick one of the five posts you saved. The one that makes you think “I wish I wrote that” the most.
Click the lightning bolt Icon and then hit the “reverse engineer” button.
It will open a chat, and produce a full breakdown document.
The overall structure, each beat, the hook, line-by-line breakdowns, why it worked, the psychological tactics/principles behind it.
 
Save this breakdown to a board.
Call it “Content Breakdowns.”
Do this with multiple posts and read through every breakdown.
This is better than almost any course you could take on content writing.
You’re essentially creating your own curriculum by deconstructing real, proven work.
Why do that?
Because it creates a layer between what they created and what you’re creating.
You’re not taking their ideas, you’re learning their structure.
This is what copywriters have always done.
You use a structure like PAS (Problem, Amplify, Solution) or AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action) until you’ve used it enough that you understand how content works. Then you don’t need one anymore.
It’s training wheels. You put them on, and once you learn to ride, you take them off.
Step 3: Interview yourself for the outline
Now you have a structure.
You have a swipe file of ideas.
You’re ready to start making it yours.
Open the chat with the board you saved all of your breakdowns to and tell it:
“Interview me for all the context you need to create an outline with my own topics, ideas, key points, and point of view. The goal is to create a bullet outline with the structure and principles from the breakdown we just did. I do not want you to write the newsletter for me.”
It will ask you questions like
What’s the specific transformation or change you’re writing about?
What’s your proof? The personal before and after?
Who are you writing to?
What are the 2-3 things you believe about this topic that most people get wrong?
What’s your newsletter called? Who subscribes?
Answer them in as much detail as you want.
If you don’t have an answer to one of the questions, tell the AI to ask you more questions to draw it out. That’s one of the beautiful things about this, you can use it to make yourself think deeper.
The result is a bullet outline that uses the structure of a proven article but is filled with your ideas, your experience, your perspective.
It will mark spots where you need to supply specifics from your own life.
It’s a framework you can follow vs filling in blanks with made-up content.
Step 4: Research title variations
Don’t confine yourself to one title yet. A lot of writers get stuck in the trap of trying to write their article based on title choice.
Write the article first.
There is a strong chance that you wind up including things in the article that you wouldn’t have thought you would before you wrote them. Writing the article gives you a better sense of what it actually delivers. You can then create a more unique and accurate title.
Your article should at least deliver on, if not overdeliver on the promise/claim made in the title.
Once your outline is filled in and you’ve written the draft, take the big ideas from your newsletter and ask the chat to research Substack title structures that are performing well right now. Then ask it to give you 10 to 20 title variations based on those structures and your article’s big ideas.
Your newsletter now looks completely different from the one you reverse engineered.
But it’s built on a proven structure and proven title patterns.
That’s infinitely better than writing from scratch with zero research.
Step 5: Write in short segments
Writing is romanticized, so nobody likes to admit that it is a slow, difficult, uncomfortable process.
I work on my newsletter for 30 to 60 minutes a day, because I know I can’t write it all at once. Even if I can write it in one sitting because I have enough experience, it still isn’t the best way to write it.
My best ideas come when I give myself time to munch on them.
I write one section. Then I go on a walk, or read a book, or scroll on social media.
And because my mind is already subconsciously/unconsciously working on the next section, that down time creates space for new ideas to form. When they come I save them to a card on my phone or onto a board so I have them ready for tomorrow morning.
A board that starts with just two documents (your outline and your draft) should grow to 8-10 items over the week. Social posts that inspired an opinion, a tweet you want to quote, a highlight from a book, a card with a voice-memoed idea from a walk.
While I’m writing, I keep my library open in a pane and filter by highlights. That lets me search for ideas related to my topic. I can ask the chat to find novel connections in my library that fit the article. And use Deep Synthesis to find where any saved sources agree or disagree.
This is what makes your newsletter unique. You’re not copying one person. You’re mashing together ideas from ten different sources and creating something new.
Cite your sources where you need to. “As someone said...” or a block quote. You don’t need academic citations, but you need to be honest about where ideas come from.
Discomfort as fuel for fast progress
The first time I wrote a newsletter, I wrote one sentence and then sat there for ten minutes staring at the screen.
That’s normal.
That’s what happens when you haven’t developed the writer’s mind yet.
Your mind hasn’t wired itself with the connections that allow ideas to flow continuously. So you put in the reps and sit through the discomfort until that comes.
And it does come. But only if you stick with it. The people who push through the suffering, the not-knowing-what-to-write-next, the uncertainty of whether this is even good. Those are the ones who succeed.
Discomfort isn’t a sign you’re doing it wrong, it’s sign that your mind is about to adapt and grow, and you’ll see noticeable improvements shortly.
You will fill in the space that equals not knowing.
That’s how skill acquisition works.
You use the training wheels, you struggle, you take them off, and then you can do things with the bike you never imagined.
The structure and the frameworks and the principles in this article start you off on a much better foot than starting from scratch.
But they don’t eliminate the work.
So go build your swipe file!
Find five ideas you wish you wrote.
Break one of them down.
Interview yourself for the outline.
Work on the article for 30 minutes at a time across a week. Keep your library open and your phone nearby for ideas that hit you on walks.
And then hit publish.
Because the gap between “someone should write about this” and “I wrote about this” is smaller than you think.
