# Our Updated Local Business SEO Strategy For Q4 2026...

## Post

7 weeks into working with us this summer, a personal injury attorney had doubled his calls and signed ~23 new cases in 6 weeks.

An automotive business in Colorado had its SEO appointments up 32% in Q1 against Q4, with 2 record months back to back in its slow season.

A peptide brand landed the #1 spot on a 40,500 searches a month keyword in under 30 days.

None of those came from doing what worked in 2024.

Local search changed more in 2026 than in any year since I started the agency.

Google put Gemini inside Maps.

Almost half of consumers now ask AI tools for local recommendations.

And Google rewrote parts of its review rules in April without announcing it.

This is the exact strategy my team is running for clients going into the end of 2026, step by step, with what changed and why.

Some context on me: before the agency, my job at FAANG was engineering ranking and search systems, and since then my team has put 50+ law firms at #1 on Google.

What changed in 2026:

Before the steps, here's what moved, because every step below is built around it.

Gemini is inside Google Maps now.

In March 2026, Google launched "Ask Maps," a button in the Maps app that lets people ask questions in plain language and get an AI answer with recommended businesses.

The answer pulls from business listings, reviews, photos, websites, social media and even Reddit, with little icons linking to the sources it used.

A Google product lead said "standard impressions apply," so Ask Maps visits get counted like normal Maps views in your profile stats.

Google's Gemini team also said ads aren't in Ask Maps yet, and they're "not ruling out ads there."

Translation:

Right now, the only way into those answers is being the best documented business in your area.

People ask AI who to call.

BrightLocal surveyed 1,002 US consumers for its 2026 review survey.

45% now use ChatGPT or another AI tool for local recommendations, up from 6% the year before.

82% read AI-written review summaries, and 23% would rely on those summaries alone.

Fresh reviews matter more than ever.

In the same survey, 32% said they look for reviews from the past 2 weeks, up from 20%.

74% want reviews from the past 3 months.

31% will only use a business rated 4.5 stars or higher, up from 17% a year earlier.

47% won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews.

Google isn't the only place people look anymore.

Google is still the most used review platform, but usage dropped from 83% to 71%.

Apple Maps nearly doubled, from 14% to 27%.

The average consumer now checks 6 different review sites.

Google changed the review rules quietly.

Google slipped 2 new clauses into its review policy on April 17 2026.

One bans giving staff a review number to hit, and the other bans telling staff to ask for particular content in a review, like mentioning an employee by name.

The penalties range from review removal to a warning banner on your profile saying fake reviews were taken down, up to full suspension.

The profile itself keeps changing.

Since late September, Google Maps asks people to sign in before they can read all of a business's reviews.

The old Q&A section is being replaced with AI answers built from your profile, your website and public sources.

In October, Google moved Posts higher up the business panel, added WhatsApp and Messenger buttons to some profiles, and started testing new ways to verify businesses, including government ID and asking some businesses for their EIN.

So that's the board.

Here's how we're playing it.

Step 1: Get a baseline before you touch anything:

Every new client starts here, and so should you.

If you don't measure the starting point, you can't tell what worked 90 days from now.

Run a grid scan.

A local rank tracker checks your position from dozens or hundreds of points around your office.

Run it for your top 3 money searches.

Write down how many points you're in the top 3 at.

Run the citation gap search.

Put your top competitor's exact business name in quotation marks and Google it.

The result count is roughly how many places online list them.

Do the same for yourself.

In one market, the 2 leading firms showed roughly 5,000 results each, against 20 to 100 for the firms telling me they "don't rank anywhere."

Count review velocity.

Count how many new reviews you got in the last 90 days.

Then count the same for your top 3 competitors.

That number matters more to Google than your total.

Ask the AI tools.

Put the same question to ChatGPT, Gemini and Ask Maps: which [your service] in [your city] would they recommend?

Write down who they name, and what they say about each business.

Check where your calls come from.

Pull 3 months of calls and form fills from your profile and your website.

That's the number the whole plan gets judged on.

What this step produced for one client:

One Midwest divorce lawyer's first scan showed him outside the top 3 at all 169 points.

Because we had that starting scan, we could prove 90 days on that every one of those points now showed him at #1, with Google calls up from ~4 a month to 50+.

Step 2: Build the profile for Ask Maps and AI answers:

Ask Maps and the AI answers read your profile like a document.

The more complete and specific it is, the easier you are to recommend.

The primary category.

It should name the one service that's worth the most to you.

A firm sitting on "law firm" as its primary category gets buried for the exact cases it wants.

A personal injury attorney who switched from "law firm" to personal injury (along with a review system and real photos) saw calls double in month 1 and climb another 70 to 80% in month 2.

Every service, one by one.

AI answers get matched against oddly specific questions, like "who handles motorcycle accidents and speaks Spanish."

If the service isn't listed, you can't be the answer.

Every case type or treatment goes in separately, with a couple of lines explaining it.

Attributes and details.

Fill in every attribute Google offers you, from accessibility to online appointments to languages spoken.

These are exactly the details an AI answer uses to match a specific request.

Hours, including the holidays.

Business hours are a ranking signal, because Google favours businesses that are open when the search happens.

Q4 is full of holidays, so set your special hours for every one of them before they arrive.

An evening answering service lets you list longer hours honestly.

Posts, now higher on the panel.

Since Google moved Posts higher on the business panel in October, they get seen more.

Get a post up a couple of times every week, using photos you took yourself of the team, the office and the work.

Phone numbers in posts now have to be verified.

The Q&A is going away, so answer questions on your site.

With Q&A being replaced by AI answers built from your profile and website, the answers have to live somewhere Google can read them.

Put your 10 most common client questions on your website, each with a short, direct answer.

Bilingual where it fits.

One immigration client got a full Spanish version of the profile and every service page, next to the English one.

Spanish-speaking clients are underserved in most markets, and the competition mostly stops at English.

That firm went from ~32 calls a month to 104 in its first 4 months, and went from 0 to #1 across 225 points statewide.

How to use this:

Change your primary category today if it's generic

Add every service you offer as a separate entry

Set special hours for Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year now

Step 3: Get reviews under the new rules:

Reviews are still the strongest lever on the map, but the rules for getting them changed this year.

What's banned now.

Setting staff a quota for a number of reviews

Scripting what clients should say, such as which employee to mention

Offering anything in return for a review

Picking out only the clients you think will leave 5 stars

Asking for reviews in a way that discourages negative ones

Unusual spikes in review volume get flagged too, whether you meant anything by it or not.

So here's how we run it now.

The business sets a target, and the system does the asking.

The request goes out automatically when a case closes or a job is finished

It's a direct link to the review form, by email first, then text

It chases up to 4 times, since most people forget after saying yes

Every client gets asked, happy or not

Nobody tells the client what to write

How many you need.

In a quiet market 5 a month does the job, and in the busiest cities you want closer to 20.

Spread them out.

With 32% of people now looking for reviews from the past 2 weeks, a steady few every week beats a big push once a quarter.

Reply to every one.

Answering each review, including the bad ones, inside a day or two counts as a ranking signal, and most businesses skip it.

It also matters to the people reading.

89% of consumers expect a reply from the owner, and half of them are turned off by one that reads like a copy-paste.

So write each reply as a real response, and never confirm someone is a client or patient in it.

The thresholds that decide the call.

31% of people won't use a business under 4.5 stars, and 47% won't use one with under 20 reviews.

If you're below either of those, that's the first thing to fix this quarter.

Clients who feel unsafe reviewing.

Some client groups, like immigration clients, can worry about putting their name on a review.

If the request doesn't feel safe and simple to them, they won't leave one.

That same immigration firm grew from 70 reviews to 90 while holding 5 stars, once the process felt safe.

How to use this:

Check your review process against the banned list, today

Set up an automatic request with follow ups, triggered at the end of every case or job

Reply to every review from the last 30 days, each one written fresh

Step 4: Show up beyond Google:

With Apple Maps nearly doubling in a year and consumers checking 6 review sites on average, Google can't be the only place you exist.

Apple Business Connect.

Apple Maps usage went from 14% to 27% in BrightLocal's 2026 survey.

Claim your listing, fill it in the same way you filled in Google, and keep the details identical.

Bing Places.

ChatGPT's search leans heavily on Bing's index.

So claim Bing Places and add your website to Bing Webmaster Tools.

Yelp.

For ChatGPT in particular, a strong Yelp listing gets you cited more than your Google profile does.

Claim it, fill it out and start getting reviews there too.

The industry directories.

Lawyers need Avvo, Justia, FindLaw, Martindale and Super Lawyers.

Doctors and clinics need Healthgrades, Zocdoc, WebMD and Vitals.

Med spas need RealSelf, plus the provider finder on each treatment brand's site.

These are the listings that AI answers and Ask Maps read when they decide who's trusted.

Identical details everywhere.

Same business name, same address, same phone number, on every site.

The most common break is an old phone number or a new partner's name that never got updated across the directories.

A finished client of mine sits on 80 to 100+ directories, all matching.

How to use this:

Claim Apple Business Connect, Bing Places and Yelp this week

Google each old address and phone number the business has had, and update anything still showing them

Add the top 5 directories for your industry if you're missing any

Step 5: Make your website the source AI quotes:

AI answers quote websites that answer questions clearly.

Your site should be the clearest page on the internet about your service in your city.

Answer first, explain second.

Open every section with the answer itself, in a sentence or two.

Then go into the detail.

That's the format AI tools pull from most easily.

The 3 things that matter most on every page.

The service and the city belong in 3 places on every page: the page title, its search description and the top heading.

Nothing else on the page moves rankings as much as those 3.

A page for every service and every town.

Every case type or treatment gets its own page.

Each town you serve needs a page of its own, with local detail you couldn't copy to another town, like its courthouse, its landmarks and a map of it.

Write for clients, not DIYers.

Do-it-yourself guides attract readers who won't hire anyone.

Write each page to rank and to get the reader on the phone.

Schema that matches the profile.

Schema is a bit of code that tells search engines what's on the page.

Law firms use LegalService, clinics use MedicalClinic, and the name, address and phone in it have to match the profile exactly.

Built for the phone.

Most of your traffic is on mobile.

That means a call button pinned to the screen, a form short enough to fill in without scrolling, and a calendar to book from on every main page.

Accident victims ring several firms, and whoever books them first tends to get hired.

How to use this:

Rewrite the opening of your 5 most important pages so the first 2 sentences answer the main question

Add an FAQ section with the 10 questions your intake team hears most

Open your site on your phone and count the seconds until you can tap the number

Step 6: Build links that hold up:

A backlink works like a referral, and Google weighs who's doing the referring.

You do need to buy links.

Google says good content earns links naturally.

In practice, that doesn't happen, so you have to go and get them.

Never from a marketplace.

If everyone buys from the same marketplace, Google penalising the domains connected to it is a matter of when.

The metrics on those sites look fantastic until you check where the traffic comes from, and it's 99% from India for random unrelated searches.

Where we get them.

Manual outreach to real sites, which is far harder for Google to flag.

Sites in your industry

Sites in your city

Ideally both

The Q4 angle.

The end of the year is full of local events, sponsorships and roundups.

Holiday events, charity drives and year-end "best of" lists are all local sites looking for businesses to mention.

Every one of those mentions is a local link and a reason for AI answers to see you as part of the community.

How to use this:

Check where your current backlinks get their traffic, using a tool like Ahrefs

Write down 20 local or industry sites you'll pitch before December ends

Sponsor 1 local event before the end of the year and ask for a link on the sponsor page

Big, crowded metros stretch that to around 6 months.

Stay consistent through the holidays.

How to use this:

Pick the 1 location or service you want ranking by February, and start there

Put review requests, posts and replies on autopilot before the holidays

Book a 90 day check-in on your calendar for the same week you start

Step 8: Track what turns into money:

Rankings are the input.

Calls, forms and signed clients are the output, and those are what decide if the work paid.

What I track for every client:

Calls and form fills from Google, every month

Views, clicks to the website and direction requests, straight from the profile stats

The grid, rescanned monthly for the main searches

Review velocity against the top 3 competitors

What the AI tools say when you ask them who to call

Where Ask Maps shows up.

Ask Maps visits get counted inside your normal Maps impressions.

There's no separate line for it in your profile stats, and Search Console doesn't show it either.

So the only way to know if Ask Maps recommends you is to ask it yourself, every month, and write the answer down.

What clients get from us.

Clients get a report every month, a dashboard showing live rankings from Google, and a call once a month.

Most stop joining that call around month 2, since they're too busy with new business.

How to use this:

Set up call tracking that separates Google calls from everything else

Keep a simple monthly log of the grid, reviews, calls and AI answers

Compare every month against your Step 1 baseline

The mistakes we're seeing this quarter:

These are the things showing up again and again in Q4 2026 audits.

Review quotas for staff.

"Everyone gets 10 reviews this month" is now against Google's policy.

Keep the target at the business level and let the system do the asking.

Copy-paste review replies.

Half of consumers say generic, copy-paste replies put them off.

A generic "Thanks for the review!" on 200 reviews looks automated, because it is.

Treating Google as the only map.

Apple Maps usage almost doubled in a year.

A business with no Apple listing is missing more than a quarter of the people checking reviews.

Relying on the Q&A section.

The Q&A is being replaced by AI answers built from your profile and website.

If your answers only live in the Q&A, put them on your website.

Going quiet for December.

The firms that stop posting, asking for reviews and replying over the holidays lose the momentum they built.

Put it all on autopilot before the holidays start.

Buying the shortcut.

Marketplace links, review spikes and AI tools promising #1 overnight all look like progress for a few weeks.
