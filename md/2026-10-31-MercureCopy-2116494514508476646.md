# How to tell captivating stories (that actually sell)

## Post

In 1862, right in the middle of the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln met a small woman in her fifties named Harriet Beecher Stowe.

He took her hand, looked down at her, and said...

“So this is the little lady who wrote the book that started this great war.”

Ten years earlier, she’d published a novel called Uncle Tom’s Cabin, which follows an enslaved man through the cruelty of his owners and the families torn apart around him.

It sold 300,000 copies in the US in its first year, and by the end of the century, only the Bible had sold more.

But what made it famous is what it did to the people who read it…

Because millions of them, people who’d never given slavery much thought, closed that book and couldn’t stop thinking about it...

All of that, from the story of one man.

That’s how much power a good story has. It can get people to agree with you before they’ve even noticed they’re being persuaded.

So you’d think the lesson is simple: tell captivating stories, and people will buy, right?

Well... I believed that too. And it cost me a lot.

A few years ago, I wrote one of the most captivating stories of my career. Over 70,000 people opted in the email, because they couldn’t wait to find out how it ended...

Yet it made fewer than 20 sales.

I’ll tell you exactly what I did wrong in a bit, because it’s one of the most expensive lessons I’ve ever learned about storytelling, and almost nobody talks about it:

Telling a captivating story is one thing. Telling a story that sells is something else altogether.

Most people who write about storytelling only teach you the first one. Which is why in this article, I’m giving you both.

By the time you reach the end, you’ll know:

Why your reader’s brain treats a good story as if it happened to him (and why that makes him stop arguing with you)

The mistake that turned 70,000 eager readers into fewer than 20 buyers, so you never make it

The 3-question test I now run on every story before I tell it

Why most sellers tell stories that are way too long, and how to pick the right size

The 5-part shape that turns a captivating story into a sale

The one role you should never give yourself in your own story

How to keep people glued to your story until the last line, with a trick from a 1926 ad that’s still being copied today

But first, to understand why stories have so much power over us, we need to go back a long way...

To a time before anyone had ever made an argument.

I. People thought in stories long before they thought in arguments

Logic is young. Much younger than we like to think.

For most of human history, people made sense of the world another way, by comparison: “this is like that, so it must work the same way.”

In Quiet Persuasion, I call it analogical thinking, and it was our first way of thinking, long before reason appeared and started taking all the credit.

And there’s one example in the book that shows just how far it goes…

Humans stand upright, which means the parts of us we think of as the noblest, the head and the heart, are at the top... while the parts we’d rather not talk about at dinner are down below.

Nobody ever had to prove anything. People just took that comparison and applied it into the world, so that what’s above became good and pure, and what’s below became dirty and base...

And that’s how you get Heaven above and Hell below.

A map of the universe, drawn from the way our bodies are put together.

That’s the kind of thinking stories run on. A story never explains a rule to you. It shows you one case, and then it lets you work out the rule on its own.

Which is exactly what the Greeks did.

They could have told their children not to be arrogant, but instead, they told them about Icarus, the boy whose wax wings melted when he flew too close to the sun... and every child got the point without anyone having to spell it out.

And it still works the same way today.

Tell a child not to play with matches, and there’s a good chance he’ll try it anyway. But tell him about the time you set the living room carpet on fire when you were 7, and he’ll most likely get the lesson.

So when you give a prospect an argument, you’re asking him to think in a way humans only picked up recently.

But when you tell him a story, you’re speaking to him the way people have always understood each other...

And the difference shows up right inside his brain.

II. Why a story feels like it happened to you

A few years ago, researchers at Princeton put a woman in a brain scanner and recorded her telling a long, unrehearsed story from her own life.

Then they played the recording to other people, and scanned their brains while they listened.

And as the story went on, something strange happened.

The listeners’ brains started to mirror hers, with the same areas lighting up in the same order, just a beat behind...

What’s more, the better someone understood the story, the closer his brain matched hers.

Almost like the story was travelling from one brain to another.

Other studies have found something similar with reading. When you read about someone running, the part of your brain that moves your legs gets active, and when you read about food, the part that tastes fires up.

That’s why you flinch when someone falls in a film, and why your heart beats faster during a chase scene even though you’re on the couch with a drink in your hand.

And it’s why, if I described stepping on a rusty nail in an old barn... the dull pop as it punches through the sole of your shoe, the way it slides into the soft part of your foot, that strange split second where you feel nothing at all...

And then you look down, and see the head of the nail sticking out of your shoe, and the leather around it slowly turning dark red...

Yeah. Part of you just winced.

So your prospect doesn’t just hear your story. In a way, he goes through it himself, and what he’s gone through himself, he believes.

He also remembers it, far better than anything else you could tell him.

In 1969, two psychologists at Stanford gave students 12 lists of 10 words to memorise. They told half of them to make up a story that linked the words together, while the other half just studied the lists the normal way.

At the end, they tested everyone on all 12 lists. The students who’d made up stories remembered 93% of the words...

The others remembered 13%.

Now think about what that means for your offer…

By tomorrow, your prospect will have forgotten most of your features, your numbers and your promises.

But the story you told him will still be there... and so will the conclusion he took from it.

Keep that last part in mind, because it’s the key to everything that follows.

Your prospect remembers your story’s conclusion. And he doesn’t fight it.

III. Why nobody argues with a story

In my last article, “Why you can’t change a buyer’s mind (and what to do instead)”, I explained that every buyer has a guard at the door: a set of beliefs whose only job is to turn away anything that threatens his world.

So when you make a claim, the guard checks it, and when you push, he pushes back.

But a story walks straight past him.

Why?

Because a claim asks your prospect to trust you, and he doesn’t trust you yet.

A story asks him for nothing at all.

It just tells him what happened to someone, one event after the other... and there’s nothing to argue with in “and then this happened.”

In fact, you learned how this works when you were about 2 years old.

That’s the age when children start to pretend, according to psychologist Jean Piaget.

And if you ever played cops and robbers as a kid, you know the rules: once you’re the cop, you act like a cop. You chase the robber, and nobody stops the game to ask whether you’ve got a badge.

Stories work exactly like that game.

They set up a little world with its own rules, its own characters, and its own causes and effects... and for as long as the story lasts, we all play along.

That’s why you can believe in dragons for 2 hours at the cinema and never once think “hang on, dragons don’t exist.”

Writers have a name for it: the suspension of disbelief.

So while your prospect is inside your story, he’s not checking your claims. He’s following along, watching what causes what, and coming to his own conclusion at the end...

And a conclusion he came to on his own is one he’ll defend for you.

(Hold on to that game of cops and robbers, by the way. I’ll tell you more about it at the end of this article, because it’s much bigger than it looks.)

So far, so good. A captivating story gets past the guard, it sticks in his memory, and it gives him a conclusion he won’t fight.

Which is exactly what I was counting on when I sent that email to 70,000 people...

IV. The most captivating story I ever wrote (and why it flopped)

Early in my career, I was getting a launch ready for a big email list.

The way it usually works is pretty simple. Before the launch, you send one email to tease what’s coming, and everyone who clicks gets added to a separate list that receives the launch sequence. So, obviously, you want as many people as possible to click.

And I thought I had a genius idea...

Films and TV shows get millions of people hooked with stories, right? So I figured I’d do the same. I opened my email with a story, cut it off right at the good part, and wrote something like “click here if you want to know what happens next.”

And it worked. Like, really worked.

Over 70,000 people clicked.

I’m not going to lie, I was pretty proud of myself. I already had the launch in the bag, at least in my head...

Then the launch went out, and it made fewer than 20 sales.

From 70,000 people. Ouch.

It took me a while to figure out what went wrong, because the launch emails themselves were fine.

The problem was that very first email.

Those 70,000 people didn’t click because they cared about the problem my product solved... they clicked because they wanted to know how my story ended.

So I gathered a huge crowd of people who were interested in a story, and then I tried to sell them something that wasn’t related to it.

That’s when it hit me.

My story was captivating, no doubt about it… but when you sell, captivating is NOT the goal.

There’s the story that sells, and the story that doesn’t, and mine, as captivating as it was, had been a terrible one.

Since then, I’ve thought about it like this...

A good story is like a great jacket.

When you wear a great jacket, people don’t come up to you and say “wow, what a great jacket.”

They say “you look great.”

But the stories most sellers tell are the opposite. They’re the jacket everyone talks about, so people end up admiring the story and forgetting all about the guy wearing it... which, in our case, is your offer.

So now, before I tell any story, I put it through a 3-question test:

Does it point at the problem my offer solves?

Does it make my prospect feel something about that problem?

Does it walk him, one step at a time, towards my solution?

If the answer to any of those is no, the story might still get likes, comments, and a nice pat on the back…

But as JK Molina says, “Likes ain’t cash.”

So say your story passes all 3. It points at the right problem, it makes the reader feel it, it walks him to your offer...

Can it still flop?

Yes. And the reason has nothing to do with what is in it…

V. The mistake that kills good stories

There are 3 kinds of stories you can tell, and they go from short to long.

The shortest is the plain report.

Who someone was, what he did, and what changed, all in a sentence or two, with no drama and no scenery. It’s fast, it builds trust, and often, it’s enough for someone who already knows his problem.

The middle one is the metaphor, where you explain something hard to grasp by comparing it to something everyone already knows.

Say you sell something that helps people lose weight. You could spend three paragraphs explaining how metabolism works... or you could say the body is like a house with a broken thermostat.

Everyone knows what a thermostat does. But the best part is, everyone knows a thermostat works.

And that’s what a good metaphor does: it uses something familiar your prospect already understands and knows works, so you’re essentially borrowing that proven cause-and-effect for your own stuff.

The longest is the emotional scene: details, feelings, a place your prospect can see and a scene he can feel.

Like when you’re telling him about a woman in a fitting room, surrounded by clothes that don’t fit anymore, hearing her daughter ask through the curtain how pretty she looks...

That last kind is the most captivating of the three, which is exactly why most sellers overuse it.

They assume that more emotion means more sales, so every offer gets a long, dramatic story... and most people give up halfway through, because they never asked for a novel.

All they wanted to know was whether it works.

Sometimes, one line from A to B works better than an entire page. Sometimes, one simple metaphor explains your offer better than any scene could.

The strongest pitches mix all 3: a scene to pull the prospect in, a plain report to show it’s true, and a metaphor so he remembers how it works.

So before you write a story, ask yourself what it’s there to do.

If it’s there to build trust, use a report.

If it’s there to explain, use a metaphor.

And if it’s there to make him feel understood, use a scene.

Then there’s a second question, and most people never even think to ask it...

VI. Whose story should you tell?

You’ve got two good options here.

The first is your own story.

Nothing works like your own story, because you went through it, which means you know the details only someone who was there could know: what the room looked like, what you kept telling yourself, the exact point where you almost quit.

And those details are what make it believable.

(It’s also why I told you about my 70,000-click disaster instead of some made-up example.)

The second is someone else’s story.

Can be a customer, a client, or the person who created what you sell. It takes more work, because you have to dig the details out of them... but it shows your prospect the result through a different pair of eyes, and often through eyes much closer to his own than yours.

Then there’s what the story is about, and most stories that sell fall into one of 3 types:

‣ Origin stories, about how something began and why. Don’t just say what happened, though. Say why it mattered, and why it should matter to him.

‣ Transformation stories, where someone goes from where your prospect is now to where he wants to be. These are the ones you’ll use the most. The best transformation stories spend a lot of time on the turning point, where change became possible, rather than only on the result.

‣ Vision stories, about what his life could look like. They start from where he is today and build a bridge to something better, close enough that he feels like it’s possible for him too.

But whichever one you pick, your story still needs a shape.

Because without one, it’s just a list of things that happened, and nobody buys anything after reading a list of things that happened...

VII. The 5-part shape of a story that sells

Novel writers and screenwriters love the Hero’s Journey, the 17-stage structure Joseph Campbell found in myths from all over the world.

Seventeen stages is great for Hollywood, but it’s way too much for when you just want to sell something.

To sell, you only need 5:

1. Where your prospect is now.

You start with your prospect’s everyday reality: the small frustrations, the problem that keeps coming back, and what it costs him to stay where he is. And you write it so close to his life that he thinks “wait, that’s me.”

2. The last straw.

Then comes something small that makes the problem impossible to ignore any longer. Jeans that won’t button, a presentation that went wrong in front of their colleagues, something specific that could happen to him.

3. His doubts.

This is the part most stories skip, and it’s a big mistake... because your prospect has reasons he’s put this off. He’s been let down before, and he’s not sure this time will be any different. So say those doubts out loud and treat them with respect, and he’ll trust you more than any proof could make him.

4. The guide.

Next comes someone, or something, that shows him the way, and that explains why it works instead of just saying that it does. Can be a person, an old forgotten book… As cliché as it seems, this element adds a bit of drama that works really well.

5. The change.

And finally, what happened. But also how it felt, what it meant, and what it made possible.

Now, look closely at who plays which part in that structure, because this is where I keep one of the promises I made you at the start...

Your prospect is the one going through all 5 steps, which makes him the hero. And you, or your offer, only show up at step 4, as the guide who helps him win.

So the one role you should never give yourself is the hero.

A lot of sellers get this backwards. Their story is all about THEIR struggle, THEIR breakthrough, and THEIR success... so their prospect has no other role to fill except the audience.

Even when you tell your own story, you should tell it as the story of someone who was once exactly where your prospect is now.

One copywriter knew this, and applied it to one of the most successful ads of all time, one that is still studied today…

His name was John Caples, and in 1926, he wrote an ad for a school that taught music by mail. His headline went:

“They laughed when I sat down at the piano. But when I started to play...”

The ad tells the story of a man at a party. When he walks over to the piano, his friends start laughing, because they’re sure he can’t play, and someone even whispers that he’s never played a note in his life…

Then he starts to play, and everybody listens.

It’s only afterwards, when everyone crowds around him asking how on earth he learned, that he tells them about the course he took at home.

So look at where the course shows up: at step 4, as the guide.

The hero is the man at the piano, and every reader who’s ever been laughed at, underestimated, or told he couldn’t do something can picture himself on that piano stool.

This headline went on to become one of the most copied in the history of advertising.

(There’s a second trick hidden in it too, and it’s the one that keeps people reading. I’ll come back to it in a minute.)

But for your prospect to step into the hero’s shoes like that, the hero first has to feel like someone he could actually be...

VIII. How to make your prospect care about someone he’s never met

This comes down to 3 things.

First, the hero has to be human, which means flawed.

Perfect characters are hard to believe and dull to follow, which is part of the reason Deadpool is more popular than Superman. So show his doubts, his past failures, and the times he nearly gave up.

Second, the hero has to be specific.

“He wanted to lose weight” is lifeless, whereas “he started avoiding mirrors” makes him see someone.

Also, give him a reason that goes deeper than the surface, because he’s not losing weight just to reach a number on the scale... he’s doing it so his kids can be proud of him.

Being specific also means knowing who is reading.

Men and women don’t react to the same things. Tell most men they’ll make $10K a month, and they’re in. But tell most women the same thing, and the first question that comes up is often “but can I actually do it?”

That’s because, for men, the struggle tends to point outwards: what other people think of them, where they rank, and proving everyone wrong. “Me against the world.”

Whereas for women, it points inwards: am I good enough, do I deserve this. “Me against me.”

And third, the hero has to be universal.

His details are specific, but what he wants and what he fears should be things everyone knows: wanting to be better, being scared of failing, and hoping something can finally change.

The skill is in mixing those last two, so that the feelings are universal while the details belong to your prospect.

Because a man trying to lose his belly won’t see himself in a woman crying in a fitting room, even though they feel the same thing...

It also helps to know which character archetypes keep coming back in stories that sell, because there are 3 you’ll use again and again:

‣ The everyday hero, someone ordinary who started where your prospect is now, so his change feels possible.

‣ The expert guide, someone who brings authority, as long as he stays approachable. The best ones have made mistakes, and they’re not afraid to admit it.

‣ The success story, who proves it can be done. But be careful with this one, because if he looks too perfect, your prospect stops believing him. The ones that work still remember what it felt like to struggle, and they talk about it.

So now you’ve got the right character in the right shape, and there’s only one job left: keeping your prospect with you all the way to the end...

IX. How to keep your prospect reading to the very last line

The first tool is one you’ve probably heard a hundred times: show, don’t tell.

“He was nervous” is a fact, whereas “he read the same email four times before he dared to open the attachment” makes you feel it.

And every time you add how something looks, sounds, smells or feels, the scene gets more solid in your prospect’s mind.

But the second tool matters even more, and it’s the trick I promised you was hiding in Caples’ headline…

Read it one more time: “But when I started to play...”

It stops halfway. You know something happened, but you don’t know what, so you have to keep reading to find out.

This is tension: a question your prospect’s mind NEEDS answered.

A good story keeps opening those questions, then answering them a little later than he’d like.

(You might have noticed I’ve been doing it to you in this article.)

Remember my 70,000 clicks?

Tension is what makes a story captivating, and it’s also what gave me 70,000 readers and fewer than 20 buyers. So the question you open has to be about his problem, or you’ll keep the wrong people reading.

The third tool is rhythm.

Short sentences speed things up.

They create urgency.

But longer sentences, the kind that take their time and let the scene unfold slowly around the person reading them, slow him down and pull him deeper in.

So mix the two, and you get to decide how he feels from one line to the next.

And finally, think about where your story goes, and who it’s for.

Stories work best with people who don’t know much about their problem yet. Because think about it... if you walked up to a stranger in the street and told him he had a few kilos to lose, he’d tell you to go fuck yourself.

But tell him the story of a guy who couldn’t keep up with his kids at the park, and he’ll listen all the way to the end. He’ll see the problem for himself, without ever feeling attacked.

That’s what stories are for in selling.

They carry the parts of your message that would be boring, awkward, or too complicated if you just said them outright.

Which is why you can open with a story, so your prospect recognises his own situation before you’ve asked him for anything.

You can use one to explain why your offer works, by telling how it came to be.

Or you can use one as proof, by telling what happened to someone who used it.

Then size it to the format.

A long sales page has room for the full 5-part structure, while a short post only has room for one scene and one feeling... so keep the details that lift the most weight, and cut everything else.

X. The game you played at 2 years old

Remember cops and robbers?

A story builds a little world for a few minutes, and your prospect plays along until it ends. Suspension of disbelief.

But some brands found a way to make the game last forever.

They build an entire world around what they sell, with its own rules, its own heroes and villains, and its own causes and effects...

And once someone steps inside, he doesn’t ask whether he should buy, because buying is just what people like him do in that world.

This is called world-building, and it’s the secret weapon of persuasion.

It’s how you get the most loyal customers. It’s how some brands get people to wear their logo for free, line up outside their stores overnight, and defend them in the comments like family...

A captivating story gets your prospect to read. A story that sells gets him to buy.

But a world?

A world gets him to come back, again and again, without you having to sell him anything at all.
