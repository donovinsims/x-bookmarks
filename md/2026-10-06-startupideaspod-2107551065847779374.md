# 13 Businesses left to build in the Agentic Age

## Post

My map of what to own when agents do the work
You can prompt a few times and end up with something close to a startup.
I see a future full of agents and abundance. In that future, owning the right assets puts you in a better position.
I think there are only 13 businesses left to build. Below is each one, why it works in a world of agents, and a real example to study.
01. AI-Native Service Firms
 
Agents handle most of the delivery. A human handles the exceptions.
Some people say an AI-native firm means agents do all of the delivery. I disagree. An agent can do 90% of the work and the firm still counts as AI-native.
Example: a bookkeeping firm for one type of business.
Client records come in
Agents prepare the books
A human reviews the exceptions
You deliver completed monthly books
Charge a monthly fee or a price per completed job. Margins are high, and a small client list is enough.
I'm actively ideating here. Think logos as a service or ads as a service. I did a full episode on building a creative agency this way.
02. Offline Businesses
 
People pay to do things with other people.
I heard about Othership from its founder. It started as a neighborhood sauna club in Toronto. You walk into a huge room with other people, music, tea, and someone to help you through cold plunges.
It sells memberships and is scaling in New York. It rides two trends: wellness and community. Agents can run the admin. People pay for the room and the experience.
Offline businesses cost more to start, especially in high-rent cities like Manhattan or Toronto. So I think about every offline idea with one question.
What is the food truck version of your business?
A restaurant can prove its model with a food truck first. The first Othership was in the founder's backyard. He invited friends and community members, then moved into a real location.
Take it one step at a time.
03. Distribution
 
Shipping a product takes a few prompts. Getting people to it is harder than ever.
Facebook ads cost about 5 cents a click when the ad platform launched around 2009. My rough estimate for today is $1 to $5 a click, depending on who and which country you target. The price of reaching people will keep climbing.
So own an organic audience. Advertisers pay you to publish useful niche information, and your audience tells you which products to build.
I call this the ACP funnel:
Audience → Community → Product
People who own meme pages, YouTube channels, and Instagram audiences will do well in this era. Start here, even if your end goal is an offline business or a service firm.
04. Proprietary Data Sets
 
Data is the new oil.
Companies like Mercor and micro1 sell data to the big labs. They're getting valuations in the billions and hundreds of millions in ARR.
The big version: pay people for data the big models lack, then sell it to the labs at a markup.
The solo version: gather useful information that people can only get from you, beyond what ChatGPT or their personal agents give them. That can be a business that makes a few million a year.
We're in the first inning of a data gold rush.
05. Domain-Specific Harnesses
 
People throw the word harness around a lot, so I'll keep it simple. The model is the engine. The harness is the rest of the car.
Speed comes from the engine. The harness is everything around it: tools, workflow rules, memory, and checks. In the car, that means the body, the steering wheel, the paint, and the electrical. It's an imperfect analogy, but it's the easiest way to explain it.
Turn a general model into a system that understands one industry. My example is an operating system for a freight brokerage agent.
Harnesses are the new GPT wrapper.
Two years ago, people called GPT wrappers useless. Many faded out, the same way 90% of startups fail. The top 10% added industry tools, workflow rules, and memory, and became harnesses. You saw a lot of this in legal, with Harvey and Spellbook.
06. Robotics and Physical AI
 
A lot of people think robotics is Jetsons-level far away. We're almost there.
Osmo sells window cleaning as a service. Building owners pay for clean windows, and Osmo deploys and runs the equipment. I think they raised around $6 to $7 million recently. It's a beautiful business.
My friend Brian Norgaard tweeted that the best way to sell software is to sell hardware. You'll see a lot more of that.
If a robotics company feels like PhD territory, you have other paths. Buy a few robots and rent them out. If you come from sales or marketing, sell them.
07. Physical Products With a Fan Following
 
Niche fans pay high prices for well-designed things.
I love coffee, and specialty coffee people get very nerdy about gear. When Fellow launched its kettle, it was the first one that felt designed for coffee people. It took off, and now Fellow has a full product line. (I have a Fellow grinder behind me. Zero affiliation, just a fan.)
With AI and the latest models, you can design physical products that were once too niche to make. Prototype on a Bambu Lab printer, then mass-produce.
A friend of mine started the Minimal Company. They make phones for people who want to scroll less, and I think they did millions on Kickstarter. That's a fan following at work.
If you really understand a niche, especially one where a product can go viral, now is a good time.
08. Compute and Energy
 
Every agent needs chips, electricity, cooling, and somewhere to run. If you own that scarce input, you're in a good place.
Crusoe recently raised $3.9 billion for its vertically integrated AI infrastructure platform. Compute and energy are the scarce inputs of our time. Get involved in these businesses any way you can, and you'll probably do well.
09. Health, Longevity, and Care
 
I talk a lot about older adults on this podcast. They need products and services, and few younger founders build for them. Make high-quality products with a high NPS.
Care is the service side: caregiver agencies and other ways to help older adults. These businesses are hard to run. They're also great businesses, and you're doing a good thing by caring for people.
In health, maybe that was Huberman. In longevity, maybe that was Brian Johnson, who tapped into the Don't Die movement. Niche down and there's still room to be the gal or guy in your corner of this space.
10. Marketplaces and Social Networks
 
Hard to build. A real moat once you do.
When I first came to Silicon Valley around 2013 to 2015, these were the startups to build. Uber matched drivers and riders. Thumbtack matched people with handymen. Instagram and TikTok sit in this group too.
Liquidity is the hard part: supply and demand at the same moment. Marketplaces are more art than science.
They're coming back. Marc Lou built TrustMRR, a marketplace for buying and selling startups. Personal agents will want access to marketplaces and social networks, and the data from both is interesting. I love these businesses.
11. Real Assets
 
The agentic world still runs on physical space.
My example: small industrial workshops rented to local trades like electricians, cabinet makers, and plumbers. Industrial warehouses and real estate fit here too.
12. Vertical Agents
 
Give an agent one recurring job in one industry.
My example is purchasing for indie restaurants:
Check stock → Compare supplier offers → Ask for approval → Place the order
People have built vertical agents for about 12 months. There's still a ton of room here, for both harnesses and agents.
13. Security
 
People need security more than ever in the AI age.
On the software side, think 1Password and Cloudflare, which has a lot of AI security features. My example is agent access control for accounting firms. The agent can read invoices and draft entries, and its access stops there.
Sell it through the firms' existing IT providers, so data stays private and systems stay safe. I expect a big breach at an agent startup at some point, and more breaches after that.
Physical security grows too. There's an Uber-style service for bodyguards with bulletproof cars, and more businesses like it are coming. People feel less secure, so they'll pay for security.
Action Checklist
Start an organic audience now (Audience → Community → Product)
Pick the business type that matches a niche you understand
Find the food truck version and test it cheap
Let agents run delivery and admin, and keep a human on the exceptions
Find the data in your niche that ChatGPT lacks
Final Thoughts
I built this list around one principle. In a world of agents, owning the right assets puts you in a better position.
This is the map I'd pay attention to. Pick one of the 13, start with the smallest version, and take it one step at a time.
